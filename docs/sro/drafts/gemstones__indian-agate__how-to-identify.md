# How to Identify Real Indian Agate

Real Indian Agate is a microcrystalline chalcedony (SiO2) with a Mohs hardness of 6.5–7, waxy to vitreous luster, conchoidal fracture, and no cleavage. Natural specimens show earthy banding, mossy inclusions, or mottled patterns in muted greens, browns, creams, reds, and yellows. The main things to distinguish it from are glass, dyed agate passed off as undyed, and softer look-alikes.

## Step 1 — Visual Inspection of Color and Pattern

Natural Indian Agate shows muted, complex color patterns. Earthy greens, warm browns, creams, rust reds, and pale yellows in concentric bands or moss-like dendritic inclusions are characteristic. Colors transition gradually; they are not uniform throughout.

**Signs of dye:** Intense, saturated, or neon-bright colors (electric blue, vivid purple, hot pink) in a stone labeled Indian Agate are almost certainly dyed. Agate is porous enough to accept dye, and much Indian agate sold in craft markets is dyed to produce brighter commercial colors. On a dyed piece, color often concentrates at the edges of banding and fades toward the center; cut surfaces may show the dye penetrating only partially. Undyed natural Indian Agate has a subtler palette.

Dyed agate is still genuine agate — it is real stone, not fake — but it should not be sold as "natural" or undyed if it has been treated. See [Indian Agate meaning](/gemstones/indian-agate/meaning) for a description of what genuine natural material looks like.

## Step 2 — Hardness Test

Indian Agate at Mohs 6.5–7 scratches glass (Mohs ~5.5) easily. Use the corner of the specimen to score a glass surface: if it leaves a clear scratch with minimal effort, hardness is consistent with chalcedony. If the specimen itself is scratched by the glass, or if you can scratch the stone with a copper coin (Mohs ~3), it is too soft to be agate.

Common soft look-alikes that fail this test include calcite (Mohs 3), howlite (Mohs 3.5), and serpentine (Mohs 3–4). Green serpentine in particular is sometimes sold under misleading names in markets where Indian Agate is popular.

## Step 3 — Check the Luster and Transparency

Agate has a waxy to vitreous luster. Hold a polished piece to the light at an angle: it should have a subtle, smooth sheen rather than a bright glassy brilliance or a flat matte finish. Thin pieces of agate are translucent; thicker sections are opaque. Both are normal.

**Glass imitations** are fully transparent (not just translucent), often perfectly uniform in color and clarity, feel slightly warmer to the touch than stone, and may show small bubbles inside when held to strong light. Glass also chips rather than fracturing conchoidally under a sharp blow, though this test destroys the specimen.

## Step 4 — Weight and Temperature

Real agate has a specific gravity of 2.60–2.64, comparable to most quartz-family stones. It feels noticeably cool to the touch when picked up from room temperature, and takes a few seconds to warm from body heat. Glass has similar density and temperature response, so this test is useful for ruling out plastic imitations, which feel light and warm up rapidly.

## Step 5 — Check the Price

Indian Agate is an abundant, inexpensive material. Tumbled stones retail for roughly $1–5 each (USD, as of September 2026); bracelets sell for $2–8. A specimen priced dramatically below this range in large quantities may be plastic or glass; one priced far above it for plain tumbled pieces may be misrepresented. For a full breakdown, see [Indian Agate price guide](/gemstones/indian-agate/price-guide).

## What the Tests Cannot Tell You

Visual and hardness checks identify the mineral family but cannot distinguish Indian agate from other agate origins. Laboratory techniques — measuring the refractive index (1.530–1.543 for agate) or specific gravity precisely — provide stronger identification but require equipment.
