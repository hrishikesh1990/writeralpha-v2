# Citrine vs Yellow Topaz: How to Tell Them Apart

Citrine is yellow quartz (SiO₂) and yellow topaz is an aluminum fluorosilicate (Al₂SiO₄(F,OH)₂) — completely different minerals that overlap so closely in color that visual identification alone is unreliable. The most useful distinguishing tests are refractive index (a refractometer separates them quickly) and specific gravity (topaz is noticeably denser). Yellow topaz is also harder and has perfect basal cleavage; citrine has neither.

## Side-by-Side Properties

| Property | Citrine | Yellow Topaz |
|---|---|---|
| Mineral family | Quartz | Topaz |
| Chemical formula | SiO₂ | Al₂SiO₄(F,OH)₂ |
| Crystal system | Trigonal | Orthorhombic |
| Mohs hardness | 7 | 8 |
| Refractive index | 1.544–1.553 | 1.619–1.627 |
| Specific gravity | ~2.66 | ~3.53 |
| Cleavage | None | Perfect basal (one direction) |
| Optical character | Uniaxial positive | Biaxial positive |
| Common treatment | Much sold is heat-treated amethyst | Generally untreated in yellow |
| Best ID test | Refractometer or SG | Refractometer or SG |

Both stones are available in pale lemon, golden yellow, honey, and orange-yellow colors — the ranges overlap completely. Color alone does not reliably distinguish them.

## Citrine: Yellow Quartz

[Citrine](/gemstones/citrine) is the transparent yellow-to-orange variety of quartz. It shares the same SiO₂ formula and Mohs 7 hardness as amethyst, rose quartz, and clear quartz. Natural citrine is relatively rare in the gem trade; GIA notes that much of the citrine sold commercially is produced by heating amethyst, which drives out the violet color centers and produces yellow or orange-brown material. Heat treatment is permanent and does not affect durability, but it does not turn amethyst into a different mineral — it remains quartz.

Natural citrine's color comes from iron impurities in the quartz lattice, likely Fe³⁺ color centers. The Ural Mountains of Russia and Minas Gerais, Brazil, produce natural citrine; commercial supply is predominantly heat-treated amethyst from Brazil and Uruguay.

Citrine has no cleavage — it breaks conchoidally like glass — which makes it more resistant to accidental splitting during setting or wear.

## Yellow Topaz: A Different Mineral

Yellow topaz belongs to an entirely different mineral family. Its formula (Al₂SiO₄(F,OH)₂) includes aluminum and fluorine alongside silicon and oxygen — a fundamentally different structure from quartz. Topaz crystallizes in the orthorhombic system, giving it prismatic crystals with a distinctive diamond-shaped cross-section. It also has one direction of perfect basal cleavage parallel to the base of the crystal, meaning it can split cleanly with a sharp blow — a characteristic that requires careful handling during cutting and setting.

Yellow is one of topaz's natural colors, along with colorless, pink, blue, orange, red, and brown. The most famous topaz variety is "Imperial Topaz" — an orange to orange-pink color found primarily in Ouro Preto, Brazil.

Yellow topaz is significantly denser than citrine: at a specific gravity of approximately 3.53 versus citrine's 2.66, a topaz of a given size feels noticeably heavier in the hand.

## Why They Are Confused

Historically, yellow topaz and citrine were used interchangeably in trade. GIA records that citrine's warm "tawny" appearance led to widespread confusion with topaz before modern gemological testing became available. Trade names like "Madeira topaz," "Rio Grande topaz," or "Spanish topaz" were used for citrine in older jewelry — these are citrine, not topaz. The confusion persists in antique and estate jewelry descriptions.

## How to Tell Them Apart

**Refractometer**: The most reliable test for a cut stone. Citrine reads 1.544–1.553; yellow topaz reads 1.619–1.627. These ranges are well separated and a properly calibrated refractometer distinguishes them without ambiguity.

**Specific gravity**: Topaz at 3.53 is roughly 33% denser than citrine at 2.66. A loose stone placed in a heavy liquid set near 2.9 will float if citrine and sink if topaz (though this requires laboratory equipment).

**Hardness**: Topaz at Mohs 8 scratches quartz (citrine, Mohs 7). A small piece of citrine scratched by topaz confirms the identification. See the [Mohs hardness scale](/learn/mohs-hardness-scale) for context. Note: this test is destructive and only suitable for rough or back facets of stones of no significance.

**Cleavage**: A loupe examination of a rough topaz specimen reveals a flat, glassy cleavage face. Citrine rough shows conchoidal fracture — curved, shell-like break surfaces. Cut stones do not display this obviously.

**Weight in hand**: For similar-sized stones, topaz is markedly heavier. This is not a definitive test for small stones but is noticeable for large ones (above 5 ct).

See the [citrine identification guide](/gemstones/citrine/how-to-identify) for a full test sequence on loose and set citrine.

## Price Considerations

Both citrine and yellow topaz are generally affordable gemstones compared to sapphire, emerald, or ruby. Natural citrine in fine grades commands a modest premium over heat-treated amethyst-citrine; Imperial Topaz (orange-pink) commands the highest prices among topaz. Plain yellow topaz in commercial grades and citrine of similar size and quality are often comparable in retail price. If a stone is sold at a significant premium specifically because it is "topaz," ask for gemological documentation — without a laboratory report, visual identity claims are unreliable.

## Metaphysical Traditions

[Citrine](/gemstones/citrine) is traditionally associated with abundance, confidence, and creativity. It is sometimes called the Merchant's Stone and is carried as a prosperity talisman. Yellow topaz is associated in crystal traditions with joy, generosity, and clarity of intention. Both associations are cultural traditions, not evidence-based health or financial claims.
