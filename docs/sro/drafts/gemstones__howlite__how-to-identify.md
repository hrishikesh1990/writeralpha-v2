# How to Identify Real Howlite — Tests That Work

Natural howlite is white or pale gray with dark gray-to-black spiderweb veining, opaque, and soft (Mohs 3.5). Dyed howlite — most often blue, sold as turquoise — can be identified by an acetone dye-transfer test. Magnesite, its closest lookalike, is distinguished by an acid test, UV light, and vein color. Glass and plastic copies lack howlite's chalky softness.

## What Real Howlite Looks Like

Howlite almost always appears in the gem trade as compact, polished nodules, tumbled stones, beads, or cabochons. The base color is white to pale gray, and the characteristic feature is a network of dark gray-to-black veins running across the surface — described as spiderweb-like or resembling cracked porcelain. The veining is produced by mineral inclusions in the surrounding borate deposit.

In its natural state the surface is chalky and dull; polished howlite has a low-lustre, slightly waxy appearance. It does not have the bright vitreous gloss of glass or the deep color of most colored gemstones.

Any howlite in vivid blue, green, red, purple, or another strong color has been dyed. That is not a sign of fraud on its own — honestly labeled dyed howlite is a legitimate and affordable stone — but it does mean you are looking at treated material. The detail that matters is whether the seller discloses the dye. For context on what it should cost, see the [howlite price guide](/gemstones/howlite/price-guide), and for what the natural stone looks like and is used for, see [howlite meaning](/gemstones/howlite/meaning).

## Test 1 — Hardness (Quick Visual Screen)

Howlite in its typical massive form has a Mohs hardness of 3.5. A copper coin (Mohs ~3) will barely scratch it, but a steel knife blade (Mohs ~5.5) will scratch it clearly. If the stone resists a steel scratch, it is not typical howlite — you may have glass, plastic, or a harder mineral such as turquoise (Mohs 4.5–6).

Perform this test on an inconspicuous area: the back of a cabochon or inside a drill hole. After scratching, wipe away any powder and look for a groove, not just residue from the metal.

## Test 2 — Dye Transfer (For Colored Specimens)

For any howlite in a non-white color, wet a cotton swab with acetone (nail-polish remover) and rub it on a hidden area of the stone. If the color transfers to the swab and a lighter or white patch appears on the stone, the color is a surface dye. Real turquoise, lapis lazuli, and most naturally colored gemstones are not affected by acetone.

Cheap dyes may even transfer with plain water. If you notice a dyed stone leaving color on your fingers or on cloth, the dye is not fully set. Even quality dyes on howlite tend to concentrate darker inside veins and surface pits — a painted-in look rather than a color that is uniform all the way through.

## Test 3 — Magnesite vs Howlite

Magnesite (MgCO3) is the stone most often confused with howlite. Both are white or pale with veining, soft, and cheap. The key differences:

| Feature | Howlite | Magnesite |
|---|---|---|
| Vein color | Dark gray to almost black | Light gray, often pale tan |
| Acid test (dilute HCl) | Does NOT fizz | Fizzes (carbonate) |
| UV fluorescence | Orange glow = strong howlite indicator | Bluish-white (shared) |
| Tongue test (raw, undyed) | Does not stick to tongue | Sticks slightly |
| Colemanite inclusions | Yellow patches possible | Absent |

**Acid test**: Place a tiny drop of dilute hydrochloric acid (or white vinegar in a pinch, though less reliable) on an inconspicuous area. Magnesite is a carbonate and will fizz visibly. Howlite is a borosilicate and will not fizz. Use dilute acid carefully, rinse immediately, and avoid skin contact.

**UV test**: Under shortwave or longwave ultraviolet light, howlite often fluoresces orange. This is a strong positive indicator. Magnesite may fluoresce bluish-white, which is not diagnostic on its own.

**Vein color**: The most immediately visible difference. Hold the two stones side by side if possible — howlite's veins are distinctly darker.

## Test 4 — Glass and Plastic

Glass imitating the howlite look is uncommon but exists, typically as mold-pressed beads or cabochons. Glass:

- Feels cooler to the touch initially (though this is not a reliable test)
- May show tiny air bubbles, mold seams, or perfectly circular drill holes
- Is harder than howlite (Mohs ~5.5) and will not scratch easily
- Has no chalky surface texture — glass is glassy
- Breaks with a conchoidal (shell-like) fracture

Plastic is softer than howlite and lighter in weight. It may show mold seams and feels distinctly light for its size. Both glass and plastic lack the natural surface porosity of howlite, and an acetone swab applied to an untreated area will not produce the same chalky surface texture that howlite has.

## Test 5 — Price as a Signal

Very inexpensive stones sold as turquoise at high prices are almost certainly dyed howlite. Natural turquoise commands substantially higher prices per piece, especially for well-matched sets of beads. If a strand of perfectly matched, vivid blue beads costs only a few dollars, the material is almost certainly dyed howlite or dyed magnesite.
