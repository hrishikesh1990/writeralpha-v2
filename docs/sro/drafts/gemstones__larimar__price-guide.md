# Larimar Price Guide — What It Costs and Why

Larimar ranges from approximately $2 to $250 per carat for cut cabochons, depending on color grade, pattern quality, and size. Commercial-grade material falls below $10 per carat; fine to premium deep-blue stones with attractive pattern run $20–$100 per carat; exceptional pieces above that. As a single-source stone mined only in the Dominican Republic, supply is constrained and prices tend to rise with quality faster than for multi-source materials. Prices checked September 2026; market conditions vary.

## Why Larimar Has a Constrained Supply

Larimar is the trade name for blue pectolite (NaCa₂Si₃O₈(OH)) found in commercial quantities at one mining area in the Barahona Province of the Dominican Republic. No comparable deposit exists elsewhere. The mine's output is limited, and only roughly 30% of rough material is gem-grade enough for jewelry — the remainder goes to tumbled stones, carvings, and lower-grade display pieces. This geographic exclusivity is the principal reason Larimar trades at a premium over similarly soft semi-precious stones.

## What Drives Larimar's Price

**Color saturation** is the single most important factor. Deep, vivid blue — often described in the trade as "volcanic blue" or "deep ocean blue" — commands the highest premiums. Pale or whitish stones are significantly cheaper. Green tints (from iron) or brownish matrix reduce value relative to pure blue.

**Pattern quality** is the second factor. Larimar's characteristic white cloud-like, feathered, or "turtle-back" webbing is prized when it is bold and well-distributed. A stone with an attractive pattern in a good blue body fetches more than a plain, low-contrast piece of equal color depth.

**Translucency.** Material that transmits light through thin sections is considered more desirable than fully opaque pieces of comparable color.

**Size.** Large high-quality Larimar is disproportionately rare. The per-carat price rises sharply above 10 carats for fine material and exponentially above 25 carats, because large clean blue rough is uncommon.

**Cut and finishing.** Calibrated cabochons for jewelry settings carry a 20–30% premium over freeform cabochons of equivalent weight, due to rough waste in achieving the precise shape.

## Price Ranges by Grade (September 2026)

Retail and trade prices observed across multiple sources in 2026 fall within the bands below. Seller grades (AAA, AA, A, B) are not standardized; the descriptions capture what each grade label typically represents in practice.

| Grade description | Typical characteristics | Approximate price per carat |
|---|---|---|
| Commercial / B grade | Pale to very pale blue, white-dominant or gray areas, weak pattern, rough or basic polish | $2–$10 |
| Standard / A grade | Light sky blue, moderate white pattern, competent cabochon | $8–$30 |
| Fine / AA grade | Strong medium blue, attractive defined pattern, good translucency and polish | $20–$80 |
| Premium / AAA grade | Deep vivid blue, dramatic pattern, high translucency, no significant matrix marks | $80–$250+ |
| Exceptional (large or collector) | Rare saturated blue, unusual size (25 ct+), custom carving, documented provenance | Individually evaluated; $250–$1,000+/ct reported |

## Tumbled Stones, Beads, and Finished Jewelry

Tumbled stones are sold by piece rather than by carat. A commercial-grade tumble typically costs $4–$12; better-quality pieces with strong color run $12–$25 or more.

Bead strands (16-inch) range from $50–$250 for commercial-grade material to $250–$600 for well-matched AA-grade strands.

Finished jewelry in silver — the dominant setting metal because it complements Larimar's blue color — runs approximately:

| Item type | Approximate U.S. retail range |
|---|---|
| Small silver earrings or pendant | $50–$300 |
| Silver statement ring | $100–$600 |
| Silver bracelet or larger pendant | $150–$1,200 |
| Gold earrings or pendant | $500–$3,000 |
| Gold ring | $700–$4,000+ |

These ranges widen considerably with stone quality, metal weight, and maker reputation. Dominican Republic craft pieces sold locally or through Caribbean markets may carry different pricing from international retail.

## How to Avoid Overpaying

**Know the grade descriptors.** Because grade labels are seller-defined, ask for specific descriptions: color coverage (proportion of deep blue vs. white or matrix), visible fractures or matrix inclusions, and any treatments. A piece disclosed as wax-stabilized is legitimate but should price lower than untreated equivalent material.

**Compare against the size and weight.** A very cheap piece that claims to be fine-grade Larimar is a red flag. Genuine deep-blue Larimar below $10 per carat is almost certainly commercial grade at best. See [how to identify Larimar](/gemstones/larimar/how-to-identify) for how to distinguish genuine material from imitations.

**Request photographs under neutral light.** Wet presentation and blue-filtered photography can make pale material appear richer than it is. The back and edges of a cabochon reveal structural condition and actual color.

**Ask about treatment.** Wax or resin impregnation stabilizes porous or softer material and is acceptable in the trade when disclosed. Undisclosed treatment that inflates apparent quality is a reason to return a piece.

**Understand that Larimar is not lab-created.** Unlike sapphire or emerald, no synthetic or lab-created Larimar exists commercially. Any stone sold as "lab Larimar" is a different material entirely.

## Durability and Value Retention

Larimar's Mohs hardness of 4.5–5 means it will develop surface scratches with heavy daily ring wear. A pendant or earring setting that protects the stone from impact and abrasion will last better than a ring with an exposed table. For context on how hardness affects durability, see [Mohs hardness scale](/learn/mohs-hardness-scale).
