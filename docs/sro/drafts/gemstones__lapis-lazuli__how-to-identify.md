# How to Identify Real Lapis Lazuli

Real lapis lazuli is a metamorphic rock — an aggregate of several minerals, not a single uniform crystal. Its blue color comes from lazurite, its white veins from calcite, and its characteristic gold flecks from pyrite. Because lapis is composite, genuine specimens always show some internal variation. Material that appears perfectly uniform and flawless deserves closer scrutiny.

## What Genuine Lapis Lazuli Looks Like

Lapis lazuli is always opaque. Under a direct light source, the blue body appears slightly granular or textured rather than glassy — this is the multi-mineral aggregate structure of the rock. High-grade Afghan material presents a rich, medium-dark royal to violetish blue. Lower-grade material may appear patchier, greener from excess pyrite, or streaked with white calcite.

Pyrite inclusions, when present, are metallic in character. Under a 10x loupe, real pyrite flecks appear three-dimensional within the rock matrix, catching light with a mirror-like flash at different angles. They are not flat, painted-on, or uniformly placed. High-quality lapis (particularly Afghan-grade) can contain very little visible pyrite, so its absence alone does not disprove genuineness — but the presence of metallic pyrite with the right three-dimensional character is a strong positive indicator.

Calcite appears as white or pale areas, often in irregular veins or patches. In the most valued material, calcite is absent or invisible. Its presence in irregular natural patterns is consistent with authentic lapis.

## Common Imitations and How to Distinguish Them

| Imitation | Key distinction from lapis |
|---|---|
| Dyed howlite | Softer (Mohs 3.5 vs 5–6); dye concentrates visibly in grey veins; blue transfers on acetone swab |
| Sodalite | Lacks pyrite flecks; more uniform blue; different mineral identity |
| Cobalt glass | Too uniform; bubbles or flow lines visible under loupe; conchoidal chip pattern |
| Dyed jasper | Harder than lapis (Mohs 7); no pyrite inclusions; more homogeneous microcrystalline texture |
| Reconstituted lapis | Too uniform; no natural matrix variation; color may appear surface-only |

[Howlite](/gemstones/howlite) dyed blue is by far the most common substitute. [Sodalite](/gemstones/sodalite) is often mislabeled rather than intentionally fraudulent — it is mineralogically related to lazurite and visually similar at a glance, but it consistently lacks pyrite flecks.

## At-Home Tests

**Visual check under magnification.** A 10x loupe reveals the granular multi-mineral texture of lapis, the three-dimensional character of pyrite flecks, irregular calcite patterns, and any dye concentration in fractures or around drill holes. Glass shows bubbles or flow lines. Dyed material often shows intense color pooling in fractures and pores.

**Acetone swab test.** Dampen a cotton bud with acetone (nail polish remover) and rub gently on an inconspicuous area. Real lapis lazuli releases no blue dye — its color is intrinsic to the lazurite mineral. Dyed howlite, dyed jasper, and dyed calcite release blue pigment immediately and visibly. Perform this only on a hidden area, as the test removes surface dye from imitations.

**Hardness check.** Genuine lapis is Mohs 5–6. A copper coin (approximately Mohs 3) will not scratch real lapis. Dyed howlite at Mohs 3.5 scratches easily with a coin. Perform hardness tests on a base or inconspicuous area only; never scratch visible surfaces.

**Weight.** Lapis has a specific gravity of 2.50–3.00 — moderately heavy for its size. Howlite is noticeably lighter. Glass can be lighter or similar depending on formulation.

## Dyed Lapis Lazuli: Enhancement vs. Substitution

Dyed lapis lazuli is a common enhancement, not an imitation in itself — the base material is genuine lapis, but color has been deepened artificially. Dye concentrates in pores, fractures, drill holes, and calcite-rich areas. A chipped edge may reveal a paler interior. Dyed lapis retains value as lapis but should be priced substantially lower than natural-color material, and treatment must be disclosed.

Dyeing becomes outright fraud only when non-lapis materials — howlite, jasper, calcite — are dyed blue and sold as lapis lazuli. That is substitution, not enhancement.

## When Lab Testing Is Needed

Visual inspection reliably identifies the most common imitations. Definitive confirmation for sophisticated cases — high-quality cobalt glass without visible bubbles, deeply penetrating stable dyes — requires laboratory methods: Raman spectroscopy (identifies lazurite, calcite, and pyrite individually), X-ray diffraction (confirms crystalline phases), or refractive index measurement. GIA issues colored stone identification reports for lapis lazuli but does not issue a standardized commercial grade.
