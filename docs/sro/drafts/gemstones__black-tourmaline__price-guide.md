# Black Tourmaline Price Guide

Black tourmaline is one of the most affordable stones in the crystal market. Raw bulk runs roughly $20–$50 per kilogram for commercial grade; individual tumbled retail pieces cost $3–$15 each depending on size and quality; faceted material is priced around $1–$27 per carat. What drives the price within those ranges, and how to avoid overpaying, is explained below.

## Why Black Tourmaline Is Affordable

Black tourmaline (schorl) forms in granites, granite pegmatites, and high-temperature hydrothermal veins, and it is by far the most common member of the tourmaline group — comprising roughly 95% of all naturally occurring tourmaline. Abundance keeps prices low. Unlike the colored tourmaline varieties (Paraiba, rubellite, indicolite) where rarity and color intensity drive carat prices into the hundreds or thousands, black tourmaline has no scarcity premium. Its primary markets are mineral specimens, crystal healing, and Vastu or astrological uses, where raw and tumbled forms dominate.

Mohs hardness of 7 makes it durable enough for everyday jewelry and handling without special care. See [the Mohs hardness scale guide](/learn/mohs-hardness-scale) for context on how hardness relates to durability and wear.

## Price Ranges by Form (September 2026)

Because black tourmaline is opaque, it is rarely priced per carat in retail. The more relevant units are per piece (tumbled or raw chunks) and per kilogram (bulk or large specimens).

| Form | Typical retail price | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Raw bulk (commercial grade) | $20–$50 per kg | Mixed size fragments, matrix material |
| Quality terminated crystals | $100–$800 per kg | Intact crystal points, good striations |
| Tumbled stones (small, 0.5–1 inch) | $1–$5 per piece | Mass market; quality varies widely |
| Tumbled stones (AA grade, 1–1.5 inch) | $5–$12 per piece | Brazil origin, reviewed September 2026 |
| Raw specimen with matrix | $10–$80+ | Priced individually by aesthetic appeal |
| Faceted / cabochon | $1–$27 per carat | Niche market; most black tourmaline is not faceted |

Prices vary by grade, seller, and region. The ranges above reflect commonly observed retail prices in September 2026; they will shift with supply conditions and seller overhead.

## What Drives the Price

### Form and processing

Raw material costs the least because it requires no processing. Tumbled stones require machine polishing — multiple stages of grading and tumbling — which adds labor and justifies a higher per-piece price than equivalent-weight raw material. Faceted black tourmaline, rare in the market, requires significant cutting and polishing of a hard mineral and commands the highest per-carat price, though still modest compared to colored tourmalines.

### Crystal integrity

For raw specimens, the quality of the striations (the defining vertical grooves), the sharpness of crystal terminations (the pointed or faceted ends), and the absence of cracks determine value. A naturally terminated black tourmaline crystal with clean striations, no breaks, and attached contrasting matrix (typically white quartz or feldspar) commands a significant premium over broken fragments or massive chunks without crystal form.

For tumbled stones, clarity of the polish, absence of chips, and color depth (jet black with no brown patches) matter most.

### Origin

Brazil (particularly Minas Gerais), Pakistan, Namibia, and Madagascar are major sources. Specimens from localities known for large, well-formed crystals — certain Minas Gerais pegmatites, Erongo in Namibia — attract collector premiums. For metaphysical use, origin is rarely priced differently at retail unless the seller specifically markets locality.

### Size and weight

Larger raw crystals are rarer than small fragments and are priced accordingly. The price-per-gram generally increases as size increases for collectible material. For tumbled stones, larger pieces command more per stone but not necessarily more per gram.

## How to Avoid Overpaying

**Know what you are buying.** A tumbled black tourmaline piece priced at $20–$40 for a 1-inch stone at a boutique crystal shop is likely a retail markup; the same quality piece is often $5–$12 at reputable online mineral sellers. Neither is necessarily wrong, but understanding the markup helps calibrate expectations.

**Check that it is actually black tourmaline.** Black obsidian, black onyx, and black glass are cheaper and are sometimes sold as black tourmaline, whether accidentally or not. The [identification guide](/gemstones/black-tourmaline/how-to-identify) covers the at-home tests (striations, hardness, streak) that distinguish genuine schorl from common lookalikes.

**Avoid novelty premiums you do not need.** "EMF protection" marketing, "charged" or "programmed" stones, and elaborate presentation boxes all add cost with no effect on the physical stone. The mineral itself is the same regardless of these additions.

**Source ethically where possible.** See the [ethical crystal sourcing guide](/learn/ethical-crystal-sourcing) for what to look for in supplier practices. Brazil and some African suppliers offer transparent supply chains; loose claims of "ethically sourced" without specifics are worth scrutinizing.
