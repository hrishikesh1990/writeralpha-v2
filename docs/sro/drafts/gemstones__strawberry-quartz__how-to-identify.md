# How to Identify Real Strawberry Quartz vs Fakes

Real Strawberry Quartz is natural quartz (SiO2) with visible iron oxide inclusions — hematite, goethite, or lepidocrocite — that create a speckled pink-to-red appearance in a clear crystal. Check for air bubbles (glass only), inspect inclusions with a loupe, assess color distribution, and apply a scratch test. Most material sold as strawberry quartz is glass or dyed quartz; genuine specimens are the minority in the market.

## What Genuine Strawberry Quartz Looks Like

Genuine strawberry quartz is transparent to translucent quartz. Its color comes entirely from internal mineral inclusions, not from the quartz host itself. A real piece shows:

- Visible red-to-pink specks, streaks, needles, or flake-like inclusions within clear quartz — these should be irregular, varied in shape, and inconsistently spaced
- A mostly transparent or lightly milky base with color concentrated in the inclusions rather than distributed evenly throughout
- Natural imperfections: internal fractures, color zoning, and uneven inclusion density are expected and actually support authenticity
- A glassy luster when polished and a conchoidal (curved, shell-like) fracture surface if broken

"Strawberry quartz" is a marketing trade name, not a formal mineralogical classification. Researchers at the GIA have identified hematite as the primary inclusion in authenticated specimens from Kazakhstan; a 2021 study in Gems & Gemology by Shu-Hong Lin, Yu-Ho Li, and Huei-Fen Chen found that some pink quartz from similar localities contains alurgite (a manganese-bearing muscovite) instead, producing a slightly different color profile.

## The Bubble Test: Fastest Way to Spot Glass

Hold the stone up to a light source and look inside. Natural quartz of any variety does not contain spherical air bubbles. If you see perfectly round bubbles inside the material, you are holding glass. This single test catches the majority of fake strawberry quartz, including the pink glass often sold as "cherry quartz" or unlabeled "strawberry quartz glass."

Glass imitations can be skillfully made, with particles of red material mixed in to mimic inclusions. Even so, the bubbles produced during glass manufacturing are nearly always present. The inclusions in glass fakes also tend to look unnaturally regular or uniform compared to natural iron oxide formations.

## Loupe Inspection: Check the Inclusions

A 10x loupe (available at any jewelers' supply store for a few dollars) is the most useful tool for distinguishing real stone from glass or dyed material.

**What to look for in genuine material:**
- Inclusions are irregular, varied in size and direction, sometimes needle-like or platelet-shaped
- Color distribution matches the inclusions: red zones sit where inclusions cluster; clear zones have no color
- You may see growth features, natural fractures, and zones of varying clarity

**Signs of glass:**
- Bubbles, even tiny ones
- Inclusions that look like suspended particles with uniform color and distribution
- Unnaturally vivid or saturated color throughout

**Signs of dyed quartz:**
- Color concentrated in surface cracks and fractures rather than distributed through inclusions in the body of the stone
- A single flat color rather than varied inclusion tones
- Running a cotton swab moistened with acetone along a hidden surface: if color transfers to the swab, the stone has been dyed

## Hardness and Temperature Tests

Real quartz has a Mohs hardness of 7. It will scratch glass (approximately Mohs 5.5) and is not scratched by a steel nail (Mohs 5.5–6.5). To test, try scratching an inconspicuous surface with a steel knife tip: quartz will not mark. If a steel key leaves a white mark, the material is softer than 7 and is not quartz.

Quartz holds cool longer than glass does when removed from a cool environment and held in the hand. This is a rough test and not definitive on its own, but a stone that warms very rapidly to body temperature is likely glass.

## Red Flags When Buying

| Feature | Likely genuine | Likely fake |
|---|---|---|
| Color | Varied red/pink inclusions in clear quartz | Solid even pink throughout |
| Inclusions | Irregular, multi-directional, naked-eye visible | Uniform, symmetrical, or absent |
| Bubbles | None | Round air bubbles visible |
| Clarity | Transparent base, inclusions visible | Opaque or uniformly colored |
| Size | Mostly small to medium pieces | Perfectly pink large towers or spheres |
| Price | Higher per piece for genuine cut gems | Extremely cheap bulk lots |

Large, perfectly pink towers or spheres are almost always glass or mislabeled material — genuine strawberry quartz rarely occurs in formations large enough for such pieces.

## Rose Quartz vs Strawberry Quartz

Strawberry quartz and rose quartz are frequently confused and sometimes deliberately swapped by sellers. Rose quartz is uniformly translucent pink with no visible inclusions; its color comes from trace amounts of titanium, iron, or manganese distributed evenly through the crystal, or from microscopic dumortierite fibers. Strawberry quartz, by contrast, is transparent with discrete visible inclusions creating a speckled effect. Held up to light, the difference is obvious: rose quartz glows evenly; strawberry quartz shows concentrated color at the inclusion sites.

## Red Aventurine and Other Lookalikes

Material sold as "red aventurine" is often dyed quartzite or quartzite with mica inclusions. Genuine red aventurine with strong aventurescence (a metallic glitter from aligned mica flakes) is distinct from strawberry quartz inclusions, which form irregular splotches and needles rather than producing a uniform glitter. If the surface shows a consistent metallic sheen, the stone is more likely aventurine than strawberry quartz.
