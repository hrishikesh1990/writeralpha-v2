# How to Identify Real Tree Agate

Real Tree Agate is opaque white chalcedony containing green dendritic (branching) inclusions formed by mineral-bearing fluids that followed tiny internal pathways as the stone crystallized. Mohs hardness of 7, a waxy to vitreous luster, and inclusions that appear to float at depth inside the stone are its key identifiers. The most common fakes and misrepresentations are dyed white chalcedony and, occasionally, glass.

## What Real Tree Agate Looks Like

Tree Agate's host body is white, cream, or pale gray microcrystalline quartz. The green inclusions branch like roots, twigs, conifers, or river tributaries. Inclusions typically appear at varying depths within the stone — some near the surface, others deeper — giving a three-dimensional quality visible as the piece tilts. The overall appearance is matte to waxy when polished, not glassy.

The inclusions are real mineral growths (typically manganese or iron oxides, chlorite-group minerals, or related silicates depending on the deposit) — they formed as fluids moved through micro-fractures in the silica. This produces branching that follows irregular, organic pathways and varies from piece to piece. No two Tree Agate specimens are identical.

## Bench Tests You Can Do Without a Lab

### Scratch test

At Mohs 7, Tree Agate will scratch glass (Mohs 5.5) and steel (around Mohs 6–6.5) easily. Run a corner of the specimen across the back of a glass tile or the flat of a steel knife blade. A genuine piece leaves a clear scratch; glass fakes (Mohs 5–6) are scratched by the stone, not the other way around. Use a corner, not a flat face, to avoid confusing powdered glass dust with a genuine scratch.

### Visual depth check

Hold the piece to a light source and tilt it slowly. In genuine Tree Agate, the inclusions exist at different depths and the branching changes apparent position as the angle changes. Flat surface printing or a thin film coating looks uniform from all angles. Inclusions that appear on the surface only, rather than inside the stone, suggest coatings or a surface treatment.

### Luster and texture

Natural chalcedony has a waxy to vitreous luster and a slightly matte feel when dry. Glass imitations tend to look brighter and more perfectly clear where the body is translucent; glass also feels distinctly colder and heavier than chalcedony of the same size.

### Temperature

Quartz-based stones feel cooler to the touch and warm slowly in the hand compared with glass or resin, which warm quickly. This is a rough guide, not a definitive test.

## How to Spot Dyed Tree Agate

Dyed pieces are the most common concern in the Tree Agate market. Natural color is heterogeneous: the green inclusions follow the branching pathways and vary in intensity; the white ground has organic variation.

Signs of dye:
- Unusually vivid, saturated, or uniform green that does not follow natural branching pathways
- Color pooled visibly in drill holes, fractures, or surface cracks
- Green on the surface only, with no depth of inclusion
- Dye wicking test: dab unobtrusive surface with a white cloth and a tiny drop of acetone (nail-polish remover); if green color transfers, the piece is dyed

Dyed Tree Agate or dyed chalcedony is not necessarily fraud when disclosed — it is a legitimate product, but the seller should state it. The concern is paying natural-stone prices for treated material without disclosure.

## Common Imitations and How to Tell Them Apart

| Material | Appearance | How to distinguish |
|---|---|---|
| Dyed white agate or chalcedony | Green-white appearance | Dye pooling in fractures; no natural depth of inclusions |
| Glass | Can mimic white-green translucency | Higher luster, warms quickly, no internal depth, may have bubbles |
| Resin or polymer | Light weight, green coloring | Noticeably lightweight, warm to touch, scratched easily by fingernail |
| Painted or coated stones | Surface color only | Color scratches off at edges; no depth under magnification |

## Is Tree Agate Commonly Faked?

Tree Agate is an affordable stone — tumbled pieces typically sell for a few dollars each — so the financial incentive to sell elaborate fakes is low. The more common issue is mislabeling: dyed chalcedony sold as natural Tree Agate, or moss agate (which has a partly translucent host body and is a different stone) sold as Tree Agate. A magnifying loupe and awareness of the stone's appearance covers most purchasing decisions.
