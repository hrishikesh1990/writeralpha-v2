# How to Identify Real Caribbean Calcite

Real Caribbean Calcite is a combination of pale blue calcite and tan-to-brown aragonite matrix, mined in Balochistan province, Pakistan, and first marketed in 2019. It has a Mohs hardness of 3, fizzes on contact with weak acid, and shows natural irregular color zoning. The main substitutes are dyed calcite, plain blue calcite without aragonite, glass, and — at the high end — Larimar.

## What Genuine Caribbean Calcite Looks Like

Caribbean Calcite is pale blue to blue-green calcite intergrown with tan, brown, or creamy white aragonite patches or streaks. The zoning is organic and irregular — no two pieces look identical. Key visual features:

- **Color distribution:** Blue areas are soft and muted, not vivid or neon. The tan-brown aragonite is present in most genuine pieces as distinct patches or veining.
- **Luster:** Waxy to vitreous when polished; slightly silky across aragonite areas.
- **Translucency:** Calcite areas are often slightly translucent when held to strong light; the overall stone is mostly opaque.
- **Surface texture:** Polished pieces may show very fine pinhole pits where softer zones meet the surface — this is normal for a Mohs 3 carbonate.

## Hardness Test

Caribbean Calcite rates Mohs 3. A copper coin (about 3 on the Mohs scale) will scratch it or be on the boundary; a steel knife blade (about 5.5) scratches it easily. To test on an inconspicuous spot:

1. Use a copper coin and press firmly across a hidden surface.
2. Wipe away any powder and look for a scratch in the stone (not just a copper mark).
3. If the stone scratches easily, that is consistent with calcite; if it does not scratch at all, the material is harder and is not Caribbean Calcite.

Do not perform the scratch test on display faces. Test only on a base or hidden edge.

## Acid Test

Genuine calcite fizzes visibly when a drop of weak acid contacts it. This is the most reliable physical confirmation of calcium carbonate content.

1. Place the stone on a tissue or dry surface.
2. Apply a single drop of plain white vinegar to an inconspicuous spot.
3. Watch for fizzing (small CO2 bubbles forming at the contact point).
4. Rinse the spot immediately with a dry cloth — do not let vinegar sit on the surface.

A positive reaction (fizzing) confirms calcium carbonate. The absence of any reaction suggests the material is not calcite — it may be glass, dyed resin, or a different mineral.

## Comparison with Common Substitutes

| Material | Key difference from Caribbean Calcite |
|---|---|
| Dyed calcite | Color is unnaturally even; pigment may pool in cracks or look brighter than natural blue calcite; still fizzes with acid |
| Plain blue calcite | Lacks tan/brown aragonite matrix; overall more uniform color |
| Glass | Does not fizz with acid; Mohs ~5.5 so a coin will not scratch it; feels heavier and colder |
| Larimar | Pectolite, Mohs 4.5–5; white-blue cloud patterns, no acid fizz; found only in Dominican Republic; significantly more expensive |
| Dyed resin / synthetic | Does not fizz with acid; may feel lighter or show bubbles inside; surface may feel slightly tacky |
| Amazonite | Feldspar, Mohs 6–6.5; green-blue without brown matrix; does not fizz |

## Origin and Documentation

All commercially available Caribbean Calcite originates from Balochistan, Pakistan. If a seller states a different country of origin, ask for documentation. This does not mean the stone is automatically fake — new sources could theoretically be found — but it warrants scrutiny. A reputable seller should be able to describe the stone as a soft carbonate material (not Larimar, not blue apatite), and should disclose any dyeing, stabilization, coating, or filling.

Heavily saturated bright blue pieces with minimal brown matrix should prompt a question about enhancement. Natural Caribbean Calcite blue is soft and muted; strong sky-blue or teal tones may indicate dye treatment.
