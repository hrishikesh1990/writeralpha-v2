# How to Identify Real Peach Moonstone vs Fakes and Glass

Real peach moonstone shows adularescence — a soft glow that floats just below the surface and shifts as the viewing angle changes. If the glow is static, looks like a surface coating, or appears equally bright at every angle, the stone is likely glass or dyed material. The test takes seconds: tilt the stone slowly under a single light and watch whether the sheen drifts through the interior.

## What Adularescence Looks Like in Genuine Peach Moonstone

Adularescence is caused by the stone's internal structure: alternating microscopic layers of orthoclase and albite feldspar scatter light between them, producing a billowing, three-dimensional glow. In peach moonstone, the body color is a warm peach to champagne tone, and the adularescence — when present — is typically white to silvery, appearing to hover within the stone.

The key test: move the stone slowly under a single lamp. The glowing area should migrate across the surface as the angle changes. In a high-quality specimen the sheen is broad and centered. In commercial-grade material, the sheen may be narrow or offset toward one edge, which is a cutting limitation rather than a sign of fakery. What distinguishes fake from real is whether the glow moves at all.

Glass and opal glass imitations produce a static surface sheen or a surface iridescence that does not drift through the stone's interior.

## Step-by-Step At-Home Checks

### 1. The adularescence movement test

Hold the stone under a desk lamp or phone flashlight. Tilt it slowly through a 90-degree arc. In genuine moonstone, the glowing area moves smoothly. In glass with a surface coating, the effect either stays fixed or disappears entirely at certain angles.

### 2. Temperature test

Real [peach moonstone](/gemstones/peach-moonstone), like all natural mineral stones, feels noticeably cool to the touch and warms slowly in the hand. Glass also starts cool but may warm faster. This test is indicative, not definitive — it works best as a quick first filter.

### 3. Inclusion check (with a loupe if available)

Genuine moonstone almost always contains inclusions visible under 10x magnification: tension cracks called "centipede" inclusions (short, parallel cracks that resemble insect legs), twinning planes, or faint cloud-like internal layers. Perfectly clean, inclusion-free material at low price is a warning sign for glass. Glass typically contains gas bubbles — tiny spherical inclusions — rather than the angular, parallel cracks seen in feldspar.

A 10x jeweler's loupe costs a few dollars and is the single most useful identification tool for home use. Look near the surface under oblique light.

### 4. Hardness reference

Peach moonstone has Mohs hardness 6–6.5. A copper coin (Mohs 3) will not scratch it. A steel knife (Mohs about 5.5–6) may or may not scratch it depending on the steel. Window glass (Mohs 5.5) will not scratch moonstone — moonstone should scratch glass. Do not test this on the face of a display-quality stone; use the girdle edge if you test at all.

Glass imitations (Mohs 5.5) can be scratched by moonstone but not vice versa — if the suspected stone is scratched by a piece of common glass, it is not moonstone.

### 5. Price as a signal

Peach moonstone at commercial quality retails for roughly $5–$50 per carat for loose stones (September 2026). Tumbled or shaped stones in the crystal market typically sell for $2–$20 per piece depending on size and quality. Very cheap material sold in large quantities — particularly at prices well below $2 per tumbled stone — should prompt closer inspection. ## Common Imitations and How to Spot Them

| Material | How it mimics moonstone | Key difference |
|---|---|---|
| Opal glass / adularescent glass | Surface iridescence or coating | Effect is surface-level and static; no interior glow movement |
| Milky synthetic spinel | Can show some adularescence | Usually more uniform body color; inclusions differ |
| Dyed quartz | Peach color | No adularescence; quartz has Mohs 7 (harder than moonstone) |
| Chalcedony | Translucent, waxy appearance | No adularescence; different luster and feel |
| Opalite (glass) | Peach-toned glass | Static blue sheen visible only from one angle; feels lighter |

## When to Seek Lab Identification

At-home checks distinguish obvious glass from genuine moonstone in most cases. If you are paying a significant price for a stone — above $50 per piece, or buying for investment — a gemological laboratory identification from GIA, IGI, or a local gem lab provides a definitive determination. Labs report refractive index, specific gravity, and spectral data that are unambiguous.
