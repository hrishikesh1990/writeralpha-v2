# Black Moonstone Price Guide

Black Moonstone tumbled stones and palm stones typically sell for $5 to $40 per piece in retail markets as of September 2026. Cut cabochons for jewelry run roughly $2 to $15 per carat depending on size, clarity, and sheen quality. Pieces with a strong, well-centered glow command the upper end; flat, opaque material with minimal sheen trades near the floor.

## Typical Retail Price Ranges

| Form | Typical retail price (September 2026) |
|------|---------------------------------------|
| Small tumbled stone (2–4 cm) | $5 – $15 per piece |
| Medium palm stone (5–7 cm) | $10 – $30 per piece |
| Large specimen or sphere (8 cm+) | $25 – $60+ per piece |
| Cabochon for jewelry (per carat) | $2 – $15 per carat |
| Bracelet (8 mm beads) | $15 – $40 |
| Pendant set in silver | $20 – $70 |

Prices vary significantly by seller, country of origin, and how heavily the stone is marketed as "premium." The ranges above reflect commonly available material, not high-end gem-quality specimens.

## What Drives Black Moonstone Prices

**Sheen quality.** The floating glow — adularescence — is the defining quality factor. A stone where the glow appears centered, strong, and clearly moves as the stone tilts commands a premium. Flat, weak, or one-directional sheen reduces the price substantially.

**Color and body tone.** Black Moonstone with a clean dark gray-to-black body color and a silver or white sheen is the most commercially desirable form. Pieces with patches of brown, orange, or salmon color may appeal to some buyers but trade at variable prices depending on how the mix is presented. Attractive color zoning in rough material is prized differently than surface staining.

**Clarity of the base material.** Visible cracks, fractures, and heavy inclusions reduce value, particularly in faceted or cabochon material. Inclusions do not always show at polished surfaces but become apparent under tilted light or magnification.

**Size.** Larger pieces showing a consistent sheen across the whole surface are rarer and more expensive per gram than small tumbled stones.

**Origin.** Black Moonstone from Madagascar, India, and occasionally Tanzania is the most common commercial material. Origin alone does not strongly drive retail prices at the mass market level, but artisan sellers may premium-price material with documented origin.

**Cutting and finishing quality.** A well-domed cabochon places the sheen at its optical maximum. Poorly cut flat cabs suppress the adularescence. High-polish finish is expected; a low-polish tumble sells for less than a polished palm stone of the same size.

## Trade Name Risks and Overpaying

The name "Black Moonstone" is applied in the trade to several different dark feldspar materials — including microcline, labradorite, and larvikite — as well as to dyed glass and dyed black stones. A piece of larvikite or dark labradorite sold as Black Moonstone is not necessarily fraudulent (both are genuine feldspars with legitimate trade histories), but misidentification of dyed glass or onyx as natural feldspar is worth detecting.

The best protection is understanding what genuine material looks like before buying. See [how to identify Black Moonstone](/gemstones/black-moonstone/how-to-identify) for the physical tests that separate genuine feldspar from substitutes.

## Durability and Value Retention

Black Moonstone at Mohs 6 to 6.5 is moderately hard but has pronounced cleavage that makes it vulnerable to chipping on impact. This affects how well pieces hold up in jewelry — a chipped ring stone loses value and cannot be fully restored without recutting. See [Mohs hardness scale](/learn/mohs-hardness-scale) for how this hardness compares to other gemstones and everyday abrasives.

For jewelry investment, consider settings that protect edges — bezels rather than prongs — and avoid using Black Moonstone in rings that will experience daily knocks.

## How to Avoid Overpaying

- Compare prices across multiple sellers before purchasing. The same grade of tumbled stone varies two to three times in price across platforms.
- Ask for natural-light photos or video showing the sheen moving. A still photo under flat overhead light can make a weak sheen look good and a good sheen look flat.
- Request disclosure of any coating, dye, or treatment. Coated stones should sell for less than untreated natural material.
- Be skeptical of prices dramatically higher than the ranges above unless accompanied by a laboratory certificate or detailed provenance.
- For sourcing that also addresses labor and environmental conditions in mining, see [ethical crystal sourcing](/learn/ethical-crystal-sourcing).
