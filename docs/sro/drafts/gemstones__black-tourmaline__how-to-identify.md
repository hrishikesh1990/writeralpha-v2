# How to Identify Real Black Tourmaline

Real black tourmaline (schorl) has three reliable diagnostic features: vertical striations running along the length of the crystal, a triangular or rounded-triangular cross-section, and a Mohs hardness of 7 that allows it to scratch glass. Common substitutes — black obsidian, black glass, black onyx, and hornblende — fail at least one of these tests. The checks below do not require equipment beyond a steel knife, a glass object, and an unglazed ceramic surface.

## What Real Black Tourmaline Looks Like

Black tourmaline belongs to the trigonal crystal system and grows as prismatic crystals. Its confirmed physical properties from Mindat and Gemdat are:

- **Colour:** Bluish-black to black, sometimes brownish-black. Completely opaque in normal light.
- **Lustre:** Vitreous (glass-like) to resinous on fresh crystal faces; duller or resinous on broken surfaces.
- **Striations:** Parallel vertical grooves running along the length of the crystal — the most recognizable feature of the tourmaline group.
- **Cross-section shape:** Rounded triangular or roughly six-sided. This is characteristic of all tourmalines; no other common mineral shares this combination with the striated prismatic habit.
- **Streak:** White to pale gray. Despite its very dark color, black tourmaline leaves a light streak on an unglazed ceramic surface.
- **Hardness:** 7 on the Mohs scale. It easily scratches glass and cannot be scratched by a steel knife.
- **Specific gravity:** 3.18–3.22 — noticeably dense for its size.
- **Cleavage:** Very poor to absent. The stone fractures unevenly rather than splitting cleanly.

## At-Home Tests

### Striations check

Pick up the specimen and examine the long faces of the crystal under good light, or use a hand lens. Genuine schorl has deep, parallel grooves running lengthwise along the crystal. These are not superficial scratches; they are part of the crystal structure. They should be clearly visible without magnification on a raw crystal.

On tumbled or polished pieces, striations are often polished down. Tumbled black tourmaline may feel faintly rough rather than perfectly smooth because of the stone's hardness and fracture pattern. A suspiciously glassy, perfectly smooth tumbled piece with no texture at all may be obsidian or glass.

### Hardness test (scratches glass)

Drag the specimen firmly across a glass surface — the back of a phone screen protector, a glass jar, or a spare glass tile. Genuine black tourmaline at Mohs 7 will scratch glass easily. The glass surface will be marked; the tourmaline will not be.

If the glass is unmarked, or if the "stone" leaves a black powder (which is the stone being abraded rather than the glass being scratched), the specimen is likely softer than glass — possible obsidian, glass, or another mineral.

### Streak test

Drag the specimen across the unglazed base of a ceramic mug or the back of an unglazed tile. Genuine black tourmaline leaves a white to pale gray streak. If the streak is red-brown, the specimen may be hematite. If there is no streak at all (because the ceramic is softer than the stone and the stone is leaving no mark), the result is inconclusive; try a different ceramic surface.

### Weight check

Hold a similar-sized piece of clear quartz or common glass in one hand and the black tourmaline specimen in the other. Black tourmaline's specific gravity of 3.18–3.22 makes it noticeably denser. Obsidian (SG ~2.4) and glass (SG ~2.5) feel markedly lighter for the same volume.

### Cross-section shape

If the crystal has a visible end face, examine its shape. Genuine tourmaline cross-sections are rounded-triangular or roughly three-to-six-sided. Round cross-sections, perfectly square cross-sections, or irregular blob-like ends suggest the specimen is not tourmaline.

## Common Lookalikes

| Material | Key differences from black tourmaline |
|---|---|
| Black obsidian | Volcanic glass; conchoidal (shell-like) fracture; glassy, smooth surface; no striations; Mohs 5–5.5 (scratched by steel knife) |
| Black glass | Similar to obsidian; may show bubbles inside; no crystal form; lighter weight |
| Black onyx | Chalcedony variety; smooth and often banded; no striations; Mohs 6.5–7 (may scratch glass, so hardness alone is insufficient) |
| Hornblende | Black amphibole; two visible cleavage directions at 56° and 124°; Mohs 5–6; no striations |
| Black hematite | Metallic or sub-metallic lustre; red-brown streak; distinctly heavier (SG ~5.3) |
| Dyed howlite | Much softer (Mohs 3.5); leaves a white streak; can be scratched with a fingernail |

Black tourmaline is abundant and inexpensive, so intentional fakery with synthetic stone is uncommon in the mainstream crystal market. The more common situation is a naturally occurring lookalike — especially obsidian — being sold, knowingly or not, as black tourmaline. ## Professional Verification

At-home tests give strong indicators but not certainty. If you have invested significantly in a specimen, a gemologist can confirm identity by measuring refractive index, specific gravity, and crystal optics precisely.
