# Black Obsidian Price Guide

Black obsidian is one of the most affordable stones in the crystal and gemstone market. Its abundance as a volcanic glass found across multiple continents keeps prices low. Tumbled stones typically sell for $1 to $5 per piece at retail, beaded bracelets for $10 to $35, and spheres for $10 to $60 depending on diameter. Large carved pieces and premium-quality specimens cost more.

## Why Black Obsidian Is Affordable

Black obsidian is not a scarce material. It forms when silica-rich lava cools rapidly, and significant deposits exist in the United States (Oregon, California, Wyoming, Nevada, New Mexico), Mexico, Argentina, Iceland, Japan, and many other regions. Because the raw material is widely available, the cost of finished pieces is driven more by cutting, polishing, and shipping labor than by raw material scarcity.

Obsidian has a Mohs hardness of 5 to 5.5, which makes it relatively easy to cut and shape compared to harder stones, keeping manufacturing costs moderate.

|---|---|---|
| Tumbled stone (small, ~2–3 cm) | $1–$5 per piece | Very common; widely available |

| Large slab or display piece | $30–$150+ | Varies widely by weight and finish |

These ranges reflect standard retail pricing and will vary by seller, country of origin, quality of polish, and platform (in-store vs. online marketplace).

## What Drives Price Within This Range

**Form and labor**: A sphere requires more skill and time to produce than a tumbled stone. Carved animals, skulls, or decorative shapes command a premium for the carving labor. Jewelry-quality settings in silver or gold add significantly to the total price.

**Size**: All else equal, larger pieces cost more simply because they require more raw material and longer polishing times.

**Quality of polish**: A high-quality polish that shows a deep mirror-like black finish commands a premium over a matte or imperfectly finished piece. The glassy luster of obsidian, when well-polished, is one of its most desirable visual qualities.

**Origin**: Obsidian from specific notable localities — such as Obsidian Cliff in Yellowstone, or specific Mexican deposits — may be marketed at higher prices. Most commercially sold black obsidian does not disclose exact origin, however.

**Rainbow or sheen obsidian**: These varieties, which show iridescence caused by layers of magnetite microcrystals or hedenbergite nanoparticles, command a premium over plain black obsidian.

## How to Avoid Overpaying

Black obsidian should be one of the most affordable stones you can buy. If a seller is asking significantly above the ranges above for standard tumbled stones or bracelets — and claiming unusual rarity, special energy, or premium provenance — those claims warrant scrutiny.

Conversely, extremely cheap pieces (especially in unusual colors like vivid blue or red) are almost certainly not genuine obsidian. See [how to identify Black Obsidian](/gemstones/black-obsidian/how-to-identify) for a full guide to distinguishing real obsidian from glass imitations.

**Buy from sellers who:**
- Disclose the material honestly (volcanic glass, origin country if known)
- Offer consistent quality across their product listings
- Have verifiable customer reviews on independent platforms

Because obsidian is a glass, its durability in rings and bracelets is limited — it chips easily on impact. This is relevant to value: an obsidian piece set in a ring will not last as long as a harder gemstone. For more on hardness and durability, see [Mohs hardness scale](/learn/mohs-hardness-scale).
