# How to Identify Real Aquamarine — Tests and Telltale Signs

Real aquamarine is a blue to blue-green beryl with a Mohs hardness of 7.5 to 8, a specific gravity of 2.72, and a refractive index of 1.577 to 1.583. These properties — especially its strong dichroism and characteristically eye-clean transparency — distinguish it from most common imitations. At-home tests can rule out glass and some simulants; definitive laboratory identification confirms the rest.

## What Real Aquamarine Looks Like

Aquamarine's color ranges from very pale blue-green to a medium saturated blue. The preferred color in the gem trade is a clean blue without gray or green interference. Most natural faceted aquamarine is eye-clean — meaning no inclusions are visible to the naked eye — which is unusual among colored gemstones and is one of aquamarine's defining features.

Under a loupe or microscope, characteristic inclusions in natural aquamarine include:

- Long, slender hollow tubes running parallel to the crystal's long axis (the c-axis)
- Two-phase fluid inclusions (liquid and gas bubbles)
- Thin healing fractures, sometimes called "fingerprints"
- Occasionally, mineral inclusions of muscovite, quartz, or tourmaline

Lab-grown (hydrothermal synthetic) beryl can also be blue and is chemically identical to natural aquamarine. It typically shows irregular angular growth patterns inside, distinct from the natural inclusion types listed above, but separating natural from synthetic confidently requires a gemologist with spectroscopic equipment. For the broader question of natural versus lab-grown, see [lab-created vs natural gemstones](/compare/lab-created-vs-natural-gemstones).

## At-Home Tests That Help

**Hardness.** Aquamarine has a Mohs hardness of 7.5 to 8. Common glass is approximately 5.5, so a real aquamarine will scratch glass cleanly. Use a glass tile or the back of a ceramic plate — the aquamarine should cut a visible line while remaining unscratched. This does not distinguish aquamarine from other hard simulants such as blue topaz (Mohs 8) or synthetic spinel (Mohs 8).

**Coolness to the touch.** Genuine mineral gemstones feel noticeably cooler to the touch than glass or plastic for a few seconds when first picked up. Glass warms to hand temperature faster. This is a rough test, not a definitive one, as it depends on the piece's size and ambient temperature.

**Heft.** Aquamarine has a specific gravity of 2.72 — somewhat lighter than blue topaz (3.5–3.6) and heavier than glass of the same size. A piece of blue topaz and a same-size piece of aquamarine will feel noticeably different in weight. Without a reference stone, this test is less useful.

**Bubbles under magnification.** Glass simulants almost always contain spherical gas bubbles visible under 10× magnification. Natural gemstones have mineral inclusions and two-phase inclusions, not perfect spheres. If you see round bubbles, the stone is almost certainly glass.

## Common Simulants and How They Differ

| Simulant | Hardness | Key difference from aquamarine |
|---|---|---|
| Glass | ~5.5 | Spherical gas bubbles; softer; warms faster |
| Blue topaz | 8 | Higher SG (feels heavier); different RI; no tube inclusions |
| Blue synthetic spinel | 8 | Curved growth striae; no dichroism like aquamarine |
| Dyed blue chalcedony | 6.5–7 | Microcrystalline texture; color often patchy under magnification |
| Maxixe-type beryl | 7.5–8 | Same hardness as aquamarine; deep blue color fades in light — a diagnostic sign |

**Maxixe-type beryl** deserves special mention. It is also a beryl, produced by irradiation to create a deep, saturated blue. Unlike heat-treated aquamarine (which is color-stable), Maxixe color fades with prolonged light exposure. If a very deep-blue stone sold as aquamarine noticeably lightens in color over time, it is likely Maxixe-type.

## Treatments You Should Know About

Most natural aquamarine sold commercially has been heat-treated at 400–450°C to remove unwanted greenish tints and achieve a purer blue. This treatment is:

- **Permanent** — it does not fade or reverse under normal conditions
- **Widely accepted** — disclosure is expected but the treatment does not significantly reduce value at commercial grades
- **Undetectable without spectroscopy** — consumer-level tests cannot confirm whether a stone was heated

Fracture filling (clarity enhancement with resin or glass) is less common in aquamarine than in emerald, but does occur in lower-grade material. Fracture-filled stones should be cleaned only with warm soapy water; ultrasonic cleaning can dislodge the filler.

## When to Get Laboratory Verification

At-home tests narrow the field but cannot definitively identify aquamarine or confirm its origin. A gemological laboratory report from GIA or a reputable equivalent will confirm species, treatment status, and whether the stone is natural or synthetic. For stones above a few hundred dollars, a laboratory report is worth the cost. It also provides documentation relevant to insurance and resale.
