# How to Identify Real Amethyst: Tests and Red Flags

Real amethyst is a purple variety of quartz (SiO2) with a Mohs hardness of 7, a vitreous (glassy) luster, and a refractive index of 1.544–1.553. The most common imitations are glass, dyed agate or quartz, and synthetic (lab-grown) amethyst. Glass can be identified at home; synthetic amethyst is much harder to distinguish from natural and often requires laboratory testing. This guide covers what to look for and what each test can and cannot tell you.

## What Real Amethyst Looks Like

Natural amethyst is transparent to translucent with a clean, glass-like surface sheen. Its purple color — caused by iron (Fe3+) impurities activating radiation-induced color centers in the quartz structure — often shows subtle variations: natural stones commonly display angular or straight-edged color zoning, with areas of lighter and darker purple, or colorless to slightly violetish-blue zones alongside purple areas. Perfectly uniform, edge-to-edge even color throughout a large stone is more characteristic of synthetic material or dyed product.

Genuine faceted amethyst should feel cool to the touch initially (quartz is a poor heat conductor compared to glass or plastic) and will warm slowly in the hand. This is a rough guide only, not a definitive test.

## Common Imitations and How to Recognize Them

### Glass

Glass is the most common material passed off as amethyst in inexpensive beads and tumbled pieces. Key differences:

- **Bubbles.** Glass may contain small spherical gas bubbles visible under magnification or a loupe. Natural quartz does not contain round bubbles — it may have liquid inclusions, fractures, or mineral inclusions, but not gas spheres.
- **Hardness.** Glass has a Mohs hardness of approximately 5.5. Real amethyst (Mohs 7) will scratch glass but not the other way around. To test: attempt to scratch a piece of glass with a sharp edge of the suspect stone. If the stone scratches glass without being scratched itself, it is at least as hard as quartz. This is a rough test that can damage the sample and the glass; use it cautiously on an inconspicuous area.
- **Thermal conductivity.** Glass warms in the hand faster than quartz. Hold a suspect piece for 30 seconds: quartz stays cooler longer.
- **Swirl marks.** Molded glass may show subtle flow lines or swirl marks in its interior, unlike the cleaner internal appearance of quartz.

### Dyed quartz or agate

Lower-quality white quartz or agate can be dyed purple and sold as amethyst. Signs:
- Color often concentrates along surface fractures or grain boundaries rather than being distributed evenly throughout the crystal.
- Under magnification, dye pooling or uneven color distribution at cracks is visible.
- Very vivid, even, saturated color in a low-priced specimen is a red flag.

### Synthetic amethyst

Synthetic amethyst is laboratory-grown quartz with the same chemical composition and physical properties as natural amethyst. It cannot be identified by hardness, specific gravity, refractive index, or appearance alone. Key indicators require magnification or laboratory equipment:

- **Twinning pattern.** GIA research (published in Gems & Gemology, 1986) found that natural amethyst is almost always multiply twinned on the Brazil law. Under a polariscope, this produces interrupted spectral rings. Synthetic amethyst is typically an untwinned single crystal, showing undisturbed spectral color bands. This test requires a polariscope and practice; it is not a consumer-level test.
- **Color zoning pattern.** Natural amethyst shows angular or straight color zones, sometimes with colorless-to-violetish zones adjacent to purple. Synthetic amethyst typically has only light and dark purple zones, or no zoning at all.
- **Inclusions.** Natural amethyst may contain fluid inclusions (liquid-filled cavities), mineral inclusions (calcite, chlorides, barite), or natural growth features. Synthetic amethyst lacks these natural inclusions; gem-quality synthetic is often strikingly clean.
- **Infrared spectroscopy.** Laboratory IR spectroscopy can detect bands associated with synthetic growth conditions, though the 3543 cm⁻¹ band alone is not a definitive test; combined examination of spectra, twinning, and inclusions is needed for unambiguous identification (Balitsky et al., Gems & Gemology, 2004).

For synthetic vs. natural questions more broadly, see [lab-created vs natural gemstones](/compare/lab-created-vs-natural-gemstones).

## Treatments to Know About

### Heat treatment

Large quantities of pale or dark amethyst are heat-treated to lighten the color or to convert it to yellow citrine. Heat-treated amethyst has the same physical properties as untreated material and is not "fake" — it is still quartz — but should be disclosed and priced accordingly. Under a polariscope, heat-treated amethyst still shows Brazil law twinning (because it started as natural amethyst), which distinguishes it from synthetic material. Under magnification, hematite inclusions with surrounding cracks can sometimes indicate heating.

GIA notes that heat treatment cannot make pale amethyst darker; it can only lighten very dark material or remove brownish inclusions.

### "Green amethyst" (prasiolite)

Material sold as "green amethyst" is actually prasiolite — a green variety of quartz, usually produced by heating natural amethyst or smoky quartz. It is a legitimate, distinct gemstone, not amethyst. If you are specifically buying amethyst, note that purple is its defining color; green stones labeled "amethyst" are a different product.

## Practical Buying Guidance

- **Price is a signal.** At-market pricing for amethyst is modest — see [amethyst price guide](/gemstones/amethyst/price-guide) for current ranges. A stone priced far below comparable retail for its stated quality deserves scrutiny; so does one priced far above it.
- **Ask for disclosure.** Any seller of natural amethyst should be able to state whether the stone has been heat-treated and whether it is natural or synthetic.
- **Buy from established sources.** For fine material or stones at significant price points, a laboratory report from GIA or an equivalent lab documents treatment status and confirms natural origin. For general guidance on buying crystals online, see [are online crystals real](/learn/are-online-crystals-real).
- **Use magnification.** A 10x loupe is a standard gemological tool and will reveal most glass imitations (bubbles, swirl marks) and surface dye concentration in heavily dyed material.

Amethyst's physical properties — Mohs 7, vitreous luster, transparent to translucent purple quartz — mean that any reputable seller can describe exactly what you are getting. Uncertainty about these basics is itself a red flag.
