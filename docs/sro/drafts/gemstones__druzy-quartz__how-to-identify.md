# How to Identify Real Druzy Quartz — Fakes and Treatments

Real Druzy Quartz consists of quartz crystal points — six-sided prisms — growing directly from a mineral matrix such as agate or chalcedony. The most common problem is not entirely fake druzy but real quartz that has been dyed, coated, or assembled in ways that are not disclosed. Here is what to look for at each step.

## What Real Druzy Quartz Actually Is

Druzy is a term describing the structure — a dense layer of tiny crystals lining a cavity or coating a surface — not a separate mineral species. The crystals are quartz (SiO2, Mohs 7), and the host matrix is typically agate, chalcedony, or occasionally volcanic rock. Natural formation occurs when silica-rich fluid enters a cavity and cools or evaporates rapidly, producing many small crystal nucleation points rather than a few large crystals.

Understanding this helps set expectations: natural druzy in its untreated form tends toward earth tones — white, gray, cream, tan, brown, and iron-stained orange or red. Any vivid blue, rainbow iridescence, metallic gold, or neon color indicates treatment. Treatment does not make a stone fake, but it should be disclosed. ## Step-by-Step Identification

### 1. Confirm the sparkling layer is actual crystals

Under a loupe or magnifying glass (10x is sufficient for most purposes), look at the surface. Genuine quartz druzy points show:

- **Six-sided prism geometry**: Each crystal tip is a pointed prism, even if very small. They may be so fine that they resemble sugar, but magnification reveals the faceted geometry.
- **Variable orientation**: Natural crystals grow outward from the matrix at slightly different angles. The surface has a chaotic directional sparkle.
- **Pointed terminations**: Each crystal ends in a point, not a flat surface or rounded edge.

Imitation materials look different:

- **Glitter**: Flat, reflective flakes with no depth; sparkle disappears when the viewing angle changes.
- **Glass fragments**: Irregular broken surfaces with no crystal geometry; may show conchoidal fracture marks.
- **Glued crystal chips**: Chips sit in a visible adhesive layer; the base may be uniform resin or metal rather than a mineral matrix.
- **Plastic**: Lightweight, warm to the touch; does not feel cold like mineral quartz.

### 2. Check whether the crystals grow from the matrix or sit on it

Genuine druzy shows continuous crystal growth into a mineral base — chalcedony, agate, or rock. Break lines (if visible on a specimen edge) show crystals transitioning into the host material without an adhesive interface.

Assembled fakes or glued chips show a distinct boundary: crystals sitting in a smooth clear adhesive layer that may have bubbles, or crystal material that can flex or peel slightly from a backing.

### 3. Assess the color honestly

Natural quartz druzy colors include: white, colorless, gray, cream, tan, brown, pale purple (from iron or manganese), and orange-red (from iron oxide staining). These occur naturally and require no treatment.

Colors that almost always indicate treatment:

- Electric or cobalt blue
- Rainbow or interference iridescence
- Metallic gold, silver, or copper
- Magenta or hot pink
- Black (may be dyed matrix)

The most common coating method is Chemical Vapor Deposition (CVD), in which titanium or another metal is deposited on the crystal surface in a vacuum chamber, creating a thin film that produces interference colors. CVD-treated druzy is widely sold; the issue is when it is sold without disclosure of the coating. On genuine CVD-coated pieces, the metallic sheen sits evenly over the crystal points. On cheap imitations, paint or spray settles into grooves, flattens the surface texture, and reduces the sparkle — the stone looks dull rather than iridescent at multiple angles.

### 4. Test hardness — carefully

Quartz ranks 7 on the Mohs scale and will scratch glass (Mohs 5.5). If you must test, find an inconspicuous area away from the crystal surface — the edge of the matrix — and attempt to scratch a piece of glass. A streak indicates harder-than-glass material. Be aware that scratch testing destroys whatever you test; never scratch across crystal points.

Glass imitations will not scratch glass as cleanly. However, hardness testing alone cannot distinguish natural quartz from synthetic quartz or from other Mohs-7 minerals.

### 5. Check the price

Price is one of the most practical signals. Natural druzy quartz cabochons retail from roughly $5 to $40 each for standard sizes; natural geode halves from $5 to $200 depending on size and quality; treated or coated commercial pieces at similar or higher prices due to processing. A listing offering druzy at well below these ranges with vague descriptions is worth scrutinizing. See [Druzy Quartz price guide](/gemstones/druzy-quartz/price-guide) for current retail evidence.

## Common Misleading Practices

The most frequent identification problems in the druzy market, per trade sources, are:

- Calling dyed agate substrate "naturally blue" or "rare blue druzy"
- Selling CVD-coated quartz as "rainbow druzy" without noting the metallic treatment
- Describing glued crystal chips as a natural druse
- Omitting resin or adhesive backing from descriptions
- Using heavily filtered or edited product photos that exaggerate color
- Labeling ordinary commercial druzy as "rare" or "museum-quality"
- Confusing calcite druzy (softer, Mohs 3) with quartz druzy (Mohs 7)

None of these treatments makes the underlying quartz worthless, but undisclosed treatments are misrepresentation. Ask sellers directly: Has this been dyed? Does it have a surface coating?
