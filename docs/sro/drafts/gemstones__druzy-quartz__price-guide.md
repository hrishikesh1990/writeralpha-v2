# Druzy Quartz Price Guide — What to Expect in 2026

Druzy Quartz is widely available and generally affordable. Small natural pieces and standard cabochons typically retail between $5 and $40; natural geode halves between $5 and $200; larger display clusters from $30 to several hundred dollars. Treatment type, crystal quality, matrix, and size are the main price drivers — not rarity.

## Why Druzy Quartz Is Typically Affordable

Quartz is one of the most abundant minerals on Earth. Druzy formations — crystal-lined cavities in agate, chalcedony, and related silicate rock — occur in Brazil, India, China, the United States, and several other countries. Supply is consistent, which keeps prices accessible compared to rarer gemstones.

The wide price range within druzy reflects quality variation, form factor, and the presence and type of treatment, not geological scarcity. A treated, iridescent commercial cabochon and an exceptional large natural amethyst geode are both called "druzy quartz," but their prices reflect very different production processes and material qualities.

## Price Ranges by Form (September 2026)

The following retail ranges are based on evidence from referenced sources checked in September 2026. Prices vary by grade, seller, and market channel.

| Form | Typical retail range |
|---|---|
| Small natural specimens and simple geode halves | $5 – $30 |
| Commercial cabochons (standard sizes) | $5 – $40 |
| Dyed or coated commercial cabochons | $5 – $40 (treatment adds processing cost, not geological value) |
| Larger natural geodes and display clusters | $30 – $200 |
| Designer or custom-cut scenic cabochons | $50 – $200+ |
| Exceptional amethyst or color-banded large geodes | $200 – several thousand |

Per-carat pricing is less useful for display specimens because the host matrix — which contributes most of the weight in a geode — adds little to the crystal quality. Wholesale per-carat figures (from trade sources) range from roughly $0.15–$3 per carat for cabochons and $0.15–$1.30 per carat for loose specimens, reflecting pre-retail pricing rather than what a buyer pays at retail.

## What Drives Price Within the Range

**Crystal quality**: Evenly sized, uniformly distributed crystal points that fully cover the matrix surface command higher prices than uneven or patchy coverage. Trade graders sometimes distinguish AA grade (variable crystal height, incomplete coverage) from AAA grade (uniform small crystals, full coverage).

**Crystal size**: Finer, more uniform crystals generally indicate more nucleation sites and slower, more even growth — this is considered higher quality for cabochons. For geodes and display pieces, larger individual crystals within a uniformly covered cavity are valued differently.

**Color**: Natural colors (white, gray, cream, tan, iron-orange) are valued for authenticity. Treated colors (metallic iridescence, electric blue, rainbow) command prices based on aesthetic appeal and coating quality, not geological rarity. A well-executed CVD titanium coating can add to retail price while actually reducing natural mineral value.

**Matrix and substrate**: A clean agate matrix with visible banding, a colorful chalcedony base, or an unusual host mineral increases desirability for collectors. A plain gray rock matrix does not.

**Size**: Larger specimens cost more. Weight alone does not drive price — a heavy matrix geode with sparse crystals costs less than a smaller, fully-covered piece.

**Locality**: Brazilian agate geodes dominate supply. Indian material is also common. Unusual localities may command modest premiums among collectors.

## How Treatments Affect Value

Treatments are extremely common in the druzy market. Dye, CVD metallic coating, electroplating, resin backing, and adhesive assembly are standard commercial practices and are not inherently problematic when disclosed. They are a problem when undisclosed, because the buyer pays for natural rarity that does not exist.

Undisclosed treatment is the main source of overpaying in this market. A $30 druzy cabochon described as "rare natural blue" may be ordinary white or gray quartz over dyed agate, coated with titanium, and backed with resin — a commercial product worth its retail price as a decorative item but not as a rare natural specimen.

For how to spot dyed, coated, or assembled material before purchasing, see [how to identify Druzy Quartz](/gemstones/druzy-quartz/how-to-identify).

## How to Avoid Overpaying

- Ask the seller directly whether the piece has been dyed, coated, or has resin backing. A trustworthy seller answers clearly.
- Compare prices across two or three sellers for the same form and quality before committing.
- Be skeptical of "rare," "museum-quality," or "collector grade" labels applied to ordinary commercial druzy at elevated prices.
- Photographs are often enhanced; request natural-light photos if you are buying online.
- Understand that durability is moderate — crystal points are brittle (Mohs 7, but physically fragile tips). This affects wear-form choices. For durability context, see [Mohs hardness scale](/learn/mohs-hardness-scale).
- For guidance on ethical purchasing and supply chain transparency, see [ethical crystal sourcing](/learn/ethical-crystal-sourcing).
