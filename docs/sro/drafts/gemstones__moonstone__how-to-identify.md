# How to Identify Real Moonstone vs Fakes and Imitations

Real moonstone is a feldspar mineral whose defining feature — adularescence — is a soft, billowing glow that appears to float beneath the stone's surface and shifts as the stone moves. Opalite glass, the most common imitation, shows only a surface sheen rather than an internal floating light, and gives itself away under close inspection. The tests below progress from what you can do by eye to what requires a jeweler's tools.

## What You Are Looking For: Adularescence vs Surface Sheen

The single most important identification step is understanding what genuine adularescence looks like. In real moonstone, the glow originates from within — it appears to come from below the surface and to move as a cloud or wave as you tilt the stone. The effect is produced by light scattering between alternating internal layers of orthoclase and albite feldspar.

An imitation that uses a reflective backing, a coating, or uniformly translucent glass shows a sheen only on the surface — more like a gloss on the stone rather than a light inside it. Opalite, the most widely sold imitation, shows a milky blue-white body under reflected light and a warm orange or yellow glow along thin edges under transmitted light (hold it up to a light source). Genuine moonstone does not produce that orange transmitted-light effect.

## The Adularescence Movement Test

Place the stone under one small, diffused light source (a desk lamp works well). Slowly rotate the stone. Observe:

- **Genuine moonstone:** The glow moves across or within the stone as you rotate it. The effect is consistent with one main sheen color — white, silver, or blue — that billows smoothly. A poorly cut genuine moonstone may show the effect only through a narrow angle.
- **Opalite or glass with a backing:** The reflected sheen may appear to shift, but it moves with the surface rather than from within. Opalite frequently shows multiple color shifts (yellow, blue, pink) on the same stone at the same time — a property genuine moonstone does not have.

## Loupe Inspection (10x Magnification)

A 10x jeweler's loupe reveals features that confirm or rule out natural feldspar:

| Feature to look for | Real moonstone | Opalite or glass |
|---|---|---|
| Centipede inclusions | Present in many specimens — short, parallel tension cracks resembling a centipede | Absent |
| Internal layering | Faint parallel planes visible in some orientations | Absent; material is uniformly smooth |
| Gas bubbles | Absent | Often present in cast glass |
| Cleavage planes | Two sets visible in cross-section | Absent |
| Mold seams | Absent | Sometimes visible on glass cabochons |

Centipede inclusions — named for their multi-legged appearance under magnification — are characteristic of orthoclase moonstone and, while their absence does not rule out genuine material, their presence strongly supports it.

Also inspect the back and edge of the stone. Assembled doublets (a thin moonstone slice glued beneath a quartz cap) show a visible join plane or glue layer at the girdle, especially under angled light. Coated stones sometimes show coating wear at thin edges or drill holes.

## Weight and Temperature

Real moonstone has a specific gravity of 2.54 to 2.66. Opalite glass tends to feel noticeably lighter for the same visual size. This requires direct comparison rather than assessment in isolation, but if a stone feels unusually light for its apparent volume, glass is more likely.

Genuine feldspar also retains cool temperature longer against the skin than plastic, though this test cannot distinguish moonstone from glass reliably on its own.

## Professional Tests for High-Value Stones

When buying fine blue moonstone or any stone at significant price, the following tests at a gemologist or gem lab provide definitive answers:

- **Refractive index:** Moonstone reads 1.518 to 1.526. Glass is variable (often around 1.5 but inconsistent); synthetic spinel reads around 1.72. A refractometer reading settles most questions in seconds.
- **Specific gravity:** 2.54 to 2.66 for moonstone, confirmed by hydrostatic weighing.
- **Raman spectroscopy:** Distinguishes feldspar (orthoclase or labradorite) from glass, quartz, spinel, and polymer without damaging the stone.
- **Polariscope:** Confirms anisotropic (doubly refractive) crystalline behavior, ruling out glass, which is amorphous.

## Common Imitations and How to Spot Them

**Opalite:** Not a mineral — it is manufactured glass. Distinguished by its orange/warm transmitted-light glow, lighter weight, surface-only sheen, and perfect uniformity under magnification. It is extremely common in online and market-stall crystal sales.

**Rainbow moonstone:** This is technically a trade name for adularescent [labradorite](/gemstones/labradorite), not traditional orthoclase moonstone. It is genuine feldspar showing genuine adularescence and is not a fake, but it is a different mineral. Raman spectroscopy can confirm labradorite identity.

**Fiber-optic glass:** Sometimes sold as "cat's eye moonstone." Shows a narrow, fixed bright line rather than a moving adularescent cloud. The line appears highly regular and does not shift the way natural chatoyancy does. Under magnification, a bundled-fiber structure is visible.

**Pale opal:** Can superficially resemble white moonstone. Opal is amorphous (no cleavage), has a waxy rather than vitreous luster, and shows no adularescence.

**Assembled doublets:** A thin moonstone base with a quartz cap. The cap is harder and more scratch-resistant, which can make the stone feel more durable than solid moonstone. Inspect the join plane at the girdle.

## Scratch Tests: Not Recommended

A fingernail (hardness ~2.5) cannot scratch moonstone. A copper coin (~3) cannot scratch it. Even a steel knife blade (~5.5) is borderline. The reverse — checking whether moonstone scratches glass (~5.5) — is also inconclusive since glass and moonstone overlap in hardness range. Scratch tests also physically damage the stone and should not be used for identification. Use the adularescence movement test and loupe inspection instead.
