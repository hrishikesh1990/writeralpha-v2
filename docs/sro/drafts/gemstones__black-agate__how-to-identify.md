# How to Identify Real Black Agate

Most black agate sold in the crystal market is genuine chalcedony — the mineral is real — but it has been dyed rather than found naturally black. True natural black agate exists but is uncommon. The more important separations to make are agate versus glass and agate versus plastic. Both are straightforward with simple at-home tests. Distinguishing natural from dyed agate requires gemological equipment, but there are visual clues.

## What You Are Actually Testing For

There are three distinct questions when examining a black stone sold as agate:

1. **Is it agate (chalcedony) at all, or glass or plastic?** This is the most important test.
2. **Is the color natural or dyed?** Dyed is not fake — it is genuine chalcedony with a color treatment — but it affects value and should be disclosed.
3. **Is it a different mineral entirely?** Black onyx, black obsidian, and some black jaspers are sold interchangeably with black agate. They are all related silica minerals; the distinction matters mainly to collectors and buyers paying a premium for a specific stone.

## Agate vs. Glass: At-Home Tests

### Temperature test

Hold the stone for a few seconds. Genuine agate (and most mineral stones) feels noticeably cooler than air temperature and warms slowly in the hand. Glass can behave similarly, so this test has limited value on its own. Plastic, by contrast, warms quickly and feels closer to body temperature from the start.

### Scratch test

Black agate has a Mohs hardness of 6.5 to 7. A steel knife blade or file (Mohs approximately 5.5) will not scratch the surface of genuine agate. Genuine agate will readily scratch a piece of glass. If your stone scratches easily with a steel implement, it is not agate. Perform this test in an inconspicuous spot, as any scratch leaves a mark.

### Luster and surface appearance

Chalcedony has a waxy to greasy luster when polished — a slightly soft-looking surface rather than a brilliant, glassy shine. Glass has a more intensely glassy, reflective luster. Under close inspection, glass often shows molded edges, a perfectly smooth interior surface, or small spherical bubbles when viewed in strong light at an angle. Agate will not have bubbles.

### Weight

Agate has a specific gravity of approximately 2.58 to 2.64. A piece of glass of identical volume will have a similar weight, which is why weight alone is not definitive. Plastic (SG approximately 1.0 to 1.5) is noticeably lighter than stone of the same apparent size — a plastic bead can feel hollow or insubstantial next to a genuine agate bead.

### Fracture appearance

If you have access to a broken or unpolished edge, genuine agate shows a conchoidal (shell-like) fracture — smooth, curved breakage. This is not a test you would perform on a stone you own unless it was already broken.

## Dyed vs. Natural Black: What to Look For

Natural black agate gets its color from fine-grained carbonaceous matter or dispersed iron-manganese oxide minerals. Commercial dyed agate gets its color from the sugar-acid carbonization treatment applied to pale chalcedony.

Visual clues for dyed material:
- **Dye concentration in cracks or pores**: Under a loupe or 10x magnifier, dyed stones often show darker concentrations along micro-fractures or porous zones, as the dye wicks into openings.
- **Uniformly perfect black color**: Natural black agate may have subtle tonal variation, banding, or areas of different intensity. Very uniform, intense black with no visible banding can suggest treatment.
- **Unpolished surfaces or drill holes**: Check the interior of bead holes or any unpolished area — dye often appears concentrated at edges relative to the interior.

These are indicators, not proof. Spectroscopic analysis by a trained gemologist is the only reliable method for confirming treatment. If you need certainty on a high-value piece, consult a gemological laboratory.
