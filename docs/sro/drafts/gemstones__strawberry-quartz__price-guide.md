# Strawberry Quartz Price Guide: What It Costs and Why

Genuine Strawberry Quartz retails at $4–10 per carat for cabochons and $7–20 per carat for faceted stones (GemSelect, September 2026). Tumbled stones retail at roughly $2–6 each. These prices apply only to natural quartz with real iron oxide inclusions; glass imitations dominate the market and sell for far less.

## Why Genuine Strawberry Quartz Costs More Than It First Appears

Strawberry quartz is a marketing trade name for natural quartz with inclusions of hematite, goethite, or lepidocrocite. Genuine specimens come from a limited number of deposits — principally Kazakhstan, Chihuahua in Mexico, Minas Gerais in Brazil, and Madagascar. Supply of high-quality material is genuinely constrained, which means the crystal market is heavily weighted toward imitations.

The result is a confusing market where a tumbled piece sold as "strawberry quartz" for $1.49 is almost certainly glass or dyed quartz, while a verified natural specimen with good clarity and visible inclusions commands a meaningfully higher price. Understanding what you are buying is the first step to understanding price.

Prices vary by seller, origin country, grade, and how accurately the material is described. The ranges above reflect genuine natural strawberry quartz; glass imitations are often sold at $1–3 for bulk lots.

## What Drives the Price of Genuine Material

**Inclusion quality and distribution.** The most desirable pieces have well-distributed iron oxide inclusions visible to the naked eye, with color that ranges from pink to burgundy across the same piece. Inclusions that are too sparse leave the stone appearing mostly colorless; inclusions so dense that the stone becomes opaque reduce transparency, which many buyers prefer in this material.

**Clarity.** Transparent pieces where the inclusions are clearly visible inside the crystal command a premium over heavily included or milky material. High-clarity strawberry quartz is rare enough that it is typically sold as individual pieces rather than in bulk.

**Cut and finish.** Faceted stones with symmetrical cuts require skilled lapidary work and command higher per-carat prices than tumbled stones. Cabochons with a smooth domed surface and evenly displayed inclusions are valued between rough and faceted.

**Origin.** Kazakhstan material is considered reference quality for this stone. Mexican specimens from Chihuahua are collector favorites, particularly terminated crystals. Brazilian material is more often cut for jewelry.

**Whether it is actually genuine.** This is the most important price driver. A piece sold cheaply as strawberry quartz is more likely to be glass or dyed material. See [how to identify Strawberry Quartz](/gemstones/strawberry-quartz/how-to-identify) for tests to apply before purchasing.

## How to Avoid Overpaying (or Underpaying for Fakes)

Overpaying happens when a buyer pays genuine-stone prices for glass. Underpaying — in the sense of getting what you paid for — is fine if you want a decorative pink glass bead for a few dollars and that is what is being sold. The problem is misrepresentation.

- Buy from sellers who can identify the origin and describe the inclusions (not just "pink quartz crystal")
- Ask for close-up photos of the specific piece you are buying, not stock images; genuine material will show inclusions clearly
- Avoid bulk listings of large formations (towers, spheres) at very low per-unit prices — genuine strawberry quartz rarely occurs in such formations
- Compare prices: faceted genuine material below $3/ct should prompt scrutiny

Mohs hardness 7 means the stone is durable for everyday jewelry without special treatment — for how hardness affects value and wearability, see [Mohs hardness scale](/learn/mohs-hardness-scale). ## Collector and Specialty Pieces

Collector-grade strawberry quartz — terminated crystal points with clear quartz and well-defined acicular red hematite inclusions from Chihuahua, Mexico, or Kazakhstan — can command prices significantly above the per-carat retail ranges above. These specimens are sold individually at mineral shows and specialist dealers. No reliable current pricing data was found for this tier of material; expect prices to be negotiated individually based on size, locality, and condition.
