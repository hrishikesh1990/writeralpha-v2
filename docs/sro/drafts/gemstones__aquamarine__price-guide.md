# Aquamarine Price Guide — What It Costs and Why

Aquamarine retail prices in 2026 range from around $20 per carat for very pale commercial stones to over $1,000 per carat for exceptional saturated blue material. Most attractive, eye-clean stones suitable for everyday jewelry fall between roughly $80 and $500 per carat. The primary driver of price is color intensity; clarity, cut, carat weight, origin, and treatment status also play roles.

## Price Ranges by Quality (Retail, September 2026)

The following ranges are based on retail evidence checked September 2026. They cover loose faceted natural aquamarine in the US market. Individual stones fall outside these ranges depending on seller, certification, and specific characteristics. Prices vary by grade and seller.

| Quality tier | Typical characteristics | Approximate retail price |
|---|---|---|
| Commercial | Very pale blue or blue-green, modest cut, possible windowing | $20–$80 per carat |
| Good | Light-to-medium blue, eye-clean, attractive commercial cut | $80–$250 per carat |
| Fine | Medium to medium-dark blue, strong saturation, eye-clean, well-cut | $250–$600 per carat |
| Exceptional | Vivid saturated blue, excellent tone balance, superior cut | $600–$1,000+ per carat |
| Top Santa Maria color | Intense, deeply saturated blue from Brazil's Santa Maria de Itabira mine | $1,000–$2,000+ per carat |

A February 2026 gem trade report noted that top-range Santa Maria-color aquamarines were exceeding $2,000 per carat in specialist transactions — a price point that would have been unusual three or four years earlier. Prices at the top end of the market have risen significantly while mid-range commercial prices have remained relatively stable.

**Important:** per-carat price does not scale steeply with size the way it does for ruby or sapphire. Because very large aquamarine crystals exist, a 10-carat eye-clean stone may cost only modestly more per carat than a 2-carat stone of similar quality.

## What Drives the Price

**Color** is the single largest factor. Aquamarine color is evaluated on hue (blue to greenish blue), tone (light to dark), and saturation (intensity of color). The most valuable stones show a pure, moderately dark blue with no gray or brown component. The Santa Maria designation — named for a mine in Minas Gerais, Brazil — is used in the trade to describe stones with the most prized intense blue, but the term is not standardized and can be applied loosely by sellers.

**Clarity.** Aquamarine is held to a higher clarity standard than most colored gemstones because it is typically transparent and eye-clean. Milky, heavily included, or haziness-affected stones are significantly less valuable. Most commercial faceted aquamarine is eye-clean.

**Cut.** A well-proportioned cut maximizes brilliance and reveals color depth. Poorly windowed stones — where color appears washed out when viewed from above — are less desirable and priced accordingly. Aquamarine's light color and moderate birefringence (0.005–0.009) reward precise cutting.

**Treatment.** The vast majority of commercial aquamarine has been heat-treated to remove greenish tints. This treatment is permanent and widely accepted; it does not significantly discount value. Unheated natural blue material commands a slight premium in collector circles but is difficult to verify without laboratory spectroscopy.

**Origin.** Brazilian Santa Maria material carries the highest premium. Pakistani aquamarine can also be intensely blue. Madagascar and Nigerian material is generally available at more accessible prices for similar quality levels.

**Carat weight.** Larger stones of the same quality cost somewhat more per carat, but the premium is modest compared to diamonds or rubies. A 5-carat fine-quality stone might be priced at $350–$700 per carat; a comparable 1-carat stone at $250–$500.

## Prices for Rough, Tumbled, and Crystal Forms

Faceted gemstones command the highest prices. For crystal practice, buyers often choose:

- **Tumbled stones:** small polished pieces, typically commercial-grade color, commonly available for $5–$30 per piece depending on size and color depth.
- **Raw crystal points or sections:** wide range, from a few dollars for small pale fragments to hundreds for large, clear, well-formed pieces with good color. Pricing depends heavily on visual quality.
- **Crystal clusters or matrix specimens:** priced as collector minerals, not per carat. Prices vary widely based on aesthetic appeal, locality, and size.

For crystal practice purposes, the exact color grade matters far less than for jewelry; a pale commercial stone functions the same way in tradition. For context on durability and why it matters for value, see [Mohs hardness scale](/learn/mohs-hardness-scale). Aquamarine's hardness of 7.5–8 makes it suitable for rings and daily-wear jewelry, which supports its value relative to softer stones.

## How to Avoid Overpaying

**Buy from sellers who disclose treatment.** Reputable sellers will state whether a stone has been heat-treated or fracture-filled. Undisclosed fracture filling in aquamarine is uncommon but does occur.

**Check for laboratory reports on significant purchases.** For stones above $300–$400, a GIA or equivalent laboratory report confirming species and treatment status is cost-justified.

**Compare color in neutral light.** Aquamarine looks its best in daylight or daylight-equivalent LED light. Warm incandescent light washes out the blue. Assess color in both conditions before buying.

**Know simulant prices.** If a stone is priced at $200 per carat and described as exceptional deep-blue aquamarine, verify it is not blue topaz (which can look similar and costs far less). See [how to identify aquamarine](/gemstones/aquamarine/how-to-identify) for distinguishing tests.
