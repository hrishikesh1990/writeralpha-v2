# How to Identify Real Red Tiger's Eye

Real Red Tiger's Eye is a fibrous quartz aggregate with a distinctive moving band of light called chatoyancy. To identify it, look for that silky optical effect shifting across the surface as you rotate the piece, confirm the fibrous parallel structure visible in the stone, and check hardness. Dyed imitations, glass, and plastic all fail one or more of these tests.

## What Genuine Red Tiger's Eye Looks Like

Red Tiger's Eye is a variety of tiger's eye in which the iron-bearing mineral fibers have been altered to iron oxide (hematite) — either through natural geological heating or, in most commercial material, through deliberate heat treatment. The result is a stone with:

- **Deep red to red-brown color**, sometimes with dark banding or near-black zones. The color is not uniform flat red; it has depth and variation along the fiber direction.
- **Chatoyancy**: a narrow band of bright, silky light that moves across the surface as the stone is tilted. In high-quality material this is sharp and centered; in lower-grade material it is diffuse.
- **Visible fibrous structure**: when held up to a light source and examined from the side, you can see the subparallel fibers running through the stone. These give it the silky texture.
- **Vitreous to silky lustre** on a polished surface.

## Key Tests You Can Do at Home

### Check for chatoyancy

Hold the stone under a single point light source (a lamp, not overhead diffuse lighting) and rotate it slowly. A genuine piece will show a single bright band of light sliding across the surface — the cat's eye effect. Glass can sometimes be produced with chatoyancy, but it typically shows an unnatural brightness and a more perfectly geometrical band. In genuine tiger's eye the band has a slight softness and travels with the fiber direction.

### Inspect the fiber structure

Look at the stone under a loupe or magnifying glass. In real Red Tiger's Eye, you will see fine parallel fibers running in one direction. These fibers are what create the chatoyancy. Glass has no fibrous structure; it will appear uniform and glassy throughout. Plastic imitations similarly lack any fibrous grain.

### Hardness test

Quartz has a Mohs hardness of 7, which means it will scratch glass (approximately 5.5) and most metals but will not be scratched by them in return. A pocket knife blade (around Mohs 5.5) should not be able to scratch the surface of genuine Red Tiger's Eye. This test is best done on a concealed edge. Note: hardness testing can damage the surface, so use it only when other tests are inconclusive and on an area not visible in normal use.

### Temperature test

Real stone feels noticeably cooler to the touch than plastic or resin and warms more slowly in your hand. Glass also starts cool but tends to reach body temperature faster than a dense stone. This is not a definitive test but can quickly rule out plastic imitations.

### Weight

Dense stone feels heavier than plastic for the same volume. If a piece feels unusually light, that is a reason to look more closely.

## What You Are Most Likely to Encounter

**Dyed material:** Some low-grade or light-colored tiger's eye is dyed red rather than heat-treated. Dyed stones often show color bleeding at cracks or chip edges, particularly when inspected under magnification. The color may also appear more uniformly saturated — a flat, consistent red — rather than the varied tones of naturally oxidized or properly heat-treated material. Dyed tiger's eye is still genuine tiger's eye, but it should be disclosed and priced accordingly.

**Heat-treated yellow tiger's eye sold as natural:** Most red tiger's eye on the market has been heat-treated. Heat treatment is a standard, widely practiced process in the gem trade and is not considered fraudulent when disclosed. Many reputable sellers list "treated" or "heated" in their product descriptions. Heated Red Tiger's Eye is genuine tiger's eye; the treatment produces the same iron oxide chemistry that occurs in naturally red specimens.

**Glass or synthetic chatoyant glass:** Cat's eye glass is made deliberately and can mimic the optical effect. It lacks fibrous structure, is heavier than plastic but may feel slightly different from stone, and does not pass the hardness test — a knife will mark glass more easily than it marks quartz.

**Plastic or resin imitations:** The lightest and cheapest imitations. Plastic is warm to the touch, lightweight, and yields to a needle or knife blade without resistance. It also typically lacks the depth and fiber variation of stone.

## What the Price Tells You

Genuine Red Tiger's Eye is an abundant, affordable gemstone. If a price is dramatically lower than the typical retail range — below roughly $3–5 for a tumbled stone, or under $8 for a bracelet — the piece is likely glass, plastic, or very heavily dyed low-grade material. Conversely, a high price is not by itself a sign of authenticity without visible quality evidence. ## Lab-Grown vs. Natural

There is no commercially produced synthetic tiger's eye; the stone's fibrous formation process is not practically reproducible in a lab setting at commercial scale. What is sold as "synthetic" or "glass" tiger's eye in the market is glass with chatoyancy, not a chemically identical synthetic. For the distinction between lab-created and natural gemstones more broadly, see [lab-created vs. natural gemstones](/compare/lab-created-vs-natural-gemstones).
