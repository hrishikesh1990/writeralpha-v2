# How to Identify Real Carnelian — Tests and Red Flags

Real carnelian is almost always heat-treated or, in many cases, dyed agate — this is normal and commercially accepted. What counts as "fake" in a problematic sense is glass, plastic, or synthetic resin sold as carnelian. Here is how to distinguish each category using practical checks and what to know before buying.

## What You Are Comparing Against

Carnelian is a variety of chalcedony (microcrystalline SiO2) with a Mohs hardness of 6.5 to 7, specific gravity of 2.58 to 2.64, and a characteristic translucency caused by its fibrous structure. Its orange-to-red color comes from microscopic iron oxide inclusions distributed through the stone.

According to Gemdat (citing Walter Schumann, Gemstones of the World, 2001):
- Carnelian's color can be enhanced by heating: dull yellow material becomes carnelian-orange or red.
- Most carnelians sold today are dyed agates that have then been heat-treated.

This means heat treatment is the norm in the trade, not a deception. When retailers disclose their stones as "natural carnelian" without further qualification, heat treatment is almost certainly involved. True untreated carnelian with natural color is rarer and more expensive — see [carnelian price guide](/gemstones/carnelian/price-guide) for context. For a broader look at how to evaluate online crystal purchases, see [are online crystals real](/learn/are-online-crystals-real).

## The Key Distinction: Natural Color Distribution vs. Dye Banding

The most practical visual test for carnelian is to hold the stone to a strong light source (a lamp or bright daylight, not a phone torch):

- **Natural or heat-treated carnelian** shows a **cloudy, diffuse color distribution** — the color is uneven, sometimes with pale or darker patches that blend gradually.
- **Dyed agate sold as carnelian** often shows **parallel banding** — stripes of color following the stone's natural agate layers. This is because the dye soaks along the banding rather than distributing evenly.

Dyed agate is not necessarily fraudulent if it is disclosed as such, but it should be priced accordingly (lower than natural carnelian). If a seller describes dyed banded material as natural carnelian, that is a misrepresentation. Check [carnelian meaning](/gemstones/carnelian/meaning) for what natural carnelian looks like.

## Glass and Plastic Imitations

Glass and plastic are the most significant fakes to watch for, particularly in very cheap pieces.

| Test | Real Carnelian | Glass | Plastic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Temperature | Stays cool at room temperature initially | Also cool | Warms quickly to hand |
| Weight | Noticeably heavier than it looks | Similar weight to carnelian | Noticeably lighter |
| Surface | Waxy to vitreous luster; slight texture | Very high gloss; perfectly smooth | May show mold lines or seams |
| Bubbles | None visible | May show internal bubbles | May show bubbles or flow marks |
| Hardness (scratch test) | Steel knife blade (Mohs ~5.5) will not scratch carnelian | Glass is Mohs ~5.5; knife may or may not scratch | Knife scratches plastic easily |
| Light transmission | Warm, diffuse glow with internal depth | Flat, uniform glow; may show bubbles | May appear uniformly colored without depth |

**Note on scratch testing**: This test can damage both the tested item and your knife. It is most useful for identifying plastic (easy to scratch) rather than distinguishing carnelian from glass with confidence. A gemologist can measure refractive index (carnelian: 1.535–1.539) and specific gravity (2.58–2.64) for definitive identification.

## Practical Buying Checks

1. **Ask about treatments**: A reputable seller will confirm whether the material is dyed, heat-treated, or both. This should be disclosed, not hidden.
2. **Check the price**: Very cheap carnelian (well under $1 per piece for polished tumbles) may be glass or plastic. Carnelian is affordable but not free — see the [carnelian price guide](/gemstones/carnelian/price-guide).
3. **Examine the color in light**: Use the light-transmission test described above. Natural and heat-treated carnelian will show cloudy internal color; striped banding suggests dyed agate.
4. **Hold it**: Genuine stone (any mineral) holds cold initially. Plastic warms to the touch in seconds.
5. **Look at multiple pieces**: In a batch, natural carnelian will show color variation between pieces. Identical, perfectly uniform orange pieces in bulk suggest glass or heavily processed material.

## Lab Testing

If authenticity matters for a high-value piece, a gemmological laboratory can confirm:
- Refractive index (distinguishes chalcedony from glass, identifies species)
- Specific gravity (confirms stone identity)
- Spectroscopic analysis (can detect dyes and distinguish natural from heated material)
