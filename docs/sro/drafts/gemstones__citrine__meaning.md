# Citrine Meaning, Properties, and Traditional Uses

Citrine is a yellow to orange variety of macrocrystalline quartz (SiO₂) with a Mohs hardness of 7. Its color comes from traces of ferric iron (Fe³⁺) incorporated during crystal growth. In crystal tradition, citrine is most widely associated with prosperity, confidence, and joy — a combination that has earned it the popular label "the merchant's stone." The full physical profile is at [citrine](/gemstones/citrine).

## What Citrine Is — The Physical Stone

Citrine belongs to the quartz group, sharing its chemical formula (SiO₂) with amethyst, smoky quartz, and clear quartz. What distinguishes citrine is its color: pale lemon yellow to deep brownish-orange, produced by ferric iron impurities in the crystal lattice. The mineral has a trigonal crystal system, vitreous (glassy) lustre, and transparent to translucent appearance in most gem-quality pieces. Its specific gravity is approximately 2.65 and it has no cleavage, fracturing conchoidally instead.

**A note on the market.** The majority of citrine sold today — including most orange-colored stones marketed as "Madeira citrine" or "Rio Grande citrine" — is heat-treated [amethyst](/gemstones/amethyst) or [smoky quartz](/gemstones/smoky-quartz). Heating amethyst to roughly 470–560°C converts its purple color to yellow or orange through a change in how iron is incorporated in the lattice. Heat-treated citrine is a legitimate commercial product and is not "fake," but it is distinct from natural citrine. Natural, untreated citrine is rarer and typically paler in color. For guidance on telling them apart, see [how to identify citrine](/gemstones/citrine/how-to-identify).

For a broader explanation of how gemstones, crystals, and minerals differ as categories, see [gemstone vs crystal vs mineral](/learn/gemstone-vs-crystal-vs-mineral).

## The Name and Its Origins

The name citrine comes from the French word for lemon, *citron*, reflecting the stone's characteristic yellow color. The name has been applied to yellow quartz for centuries, though the modern gem trade's use of it stabilized in the early twentieth century. Roman writers described yellow quartz used in jewelry, though they did not always distinguish it clearly from topaz — confusion that persisted into early modern gem literature.

## Traditional and Cultural Associations

Crystal tradition describes citrine as the "merchant's stone," a label tied to the belief that keeping citrine in a cash register, wallet, or business space encourages prosperity and attracts customers. The practical origin of this association is undocumented in academic sources, but it is one of the most consistent descriptions across modern crystal practice texts.

Beyond prosperity, citrine is traditionally linked to:

- **Joy and optimism.** Citrine's warm yellow and orange tones are associated with sunshine and uplifted mood. Practitioners describe it as a stone for dispelling negativity and encouraging a positive outlook.
- **Confidence and personal power.** In chakra systems, citrine is associated with the solar plexus chakra (Manipura), the energy center traditionally linked to self-esteem, willpower, and motivation.
- **Creativity.** Writers, artists, and entrepreneurs appear frequently as the described audience for citrine in crystal practice literature, drawn by its traditional association with focused, generative energy.
- **Abundance in a broad sense.** While "the merchant's stone" points to financial abundance, practitioners also apply citrine to abundance of creative output, social confidence, and opportunities.

Citrine's traditional associations with money and prosperity are explored in the [crystals for money](/crystals/crystals-for-money) guide, and its confidence associations appear in [crystals for confidence](/crystals/crystals-for-confidence).

## Astrological and Elemental Associations

In Western astrological tradition, citrine is most commonly linked to Aries, Leo, and Gemini — see the [Aries gemstone page](/gemstones/zodiac/aries) for the full context of that pairing. The fire element and the Sun are the planetary and elemental associations most frequently cited in crystal practice literature, consistent with the stone's warm color and energizing traditional character. In Vedic astrology, citrine is sometimes used as an accessible alternative to yellow sapphire for those working with the Sun's influences.

Citrine is listed as an alternate birthstone for November alongside topaz, an association recognized by some (though not all) gemological authorities.

## How Citrine Is Used in Practice

Practitioners use citrine in several ways:

- **Carried or worn.** Keeping citrine in a pocket, purse, or wallet is the most common application tied to the prosperity tradition.
- **Placed in a workspace.** Desks, studios, and cash registers are the most frequently cited placements, following the merchant's stone tradition.
- **Used in meditation.** Holding citrine during meditation is described as a way to work with solar plexus energy and set intentions around confidence or abundance.
- **Combined with other stones.** Pairing citrine with complementary stones is explored in [citrine combinations](/gemstones/citrine/combinations).
- **Paired with affirmations.** Speaking intentions aloud while holding citrine is a common practice; see [citrine affirmations](/gemstones/citrine/affirmations) for examples.
