# How to Identify Real Larvikite

Real larvikite is identified by its silver-blue schiller — an internal shimmer produced by light diffracting off layered feldspar crystals within the rock, not a surface coating or dye. Genuine material is also a coarse-grained rock, meaning individual crystals are visible to the naked eye. Glass fakes and dyed imitations lack this internal structure.

## What Makes Larvikite Distinctive

Larvikite is an igneous rock quarried primarily near Larvik, Norway. It is composed predominantly of anorthoclase feldspar (typically 90% or more of the rock), with minor amounts of pyroxene, amphibole, and other accessory minerals.

The stone's signature visual property is its **schiller effect** — sometimes called labradorescence because it resembles the optical effect in labradorite. When you tilt a polished piece of larvikite under light, the dark grey-black surface produces a silver-blue flash that moves across the stone. This effect comes from light diffracting between alternating thin layers of feldspar and plagioclase within the individual crystals. It is a property of the internal mineral structure, not of the surface.

Other key physical characteristics:

- **Mohs hardness 6 to 6.5** — will be scratched by a steel file or quartz, but not easily by a fingernail or copper coin
- **Coarse grain** — crystals are large enough to see with the naked eye; the surface appears granular rather than smooth at close range
- **Color** — dark grey to near-black ground, sometimes with brownish or greenish overtones depending on the variety; the schiller adds a blue-silver overlay
- **Specific gravity 2.59** — heavier than glass of the same size
- **Pearly luster** on polished surfaces
- **No cleavage planes visible** at the scale of a tumbled stone or carving

## Step-by-Step Identification

**Step 1: Look for schiller in different lighting.**
Hold the stone under a single-point light source (a lamp, window, or phone torch) and tilt it slowly. Genuine larvikite produces a moving silver-blue flash that originates from inside the stone. The flash shifts as the angle changes. If the stone shows only a uniform surface sheen or reflectivity with no internal depth, it may be glass or another material.

**Step 2: Examine grain structure.**
Look at the surface closely, ideally with a loupe or magnifying glass. In real larvikite, you can see individual crystals — patches of different mineral phases with slightly different orientations. Glass has no grain structure: it looks uniformly smooth and homogeneous at the surface even under magnification.

**Step 3: Feel the weight.**
Larvikite's specific gravity of 2.59 means a polished sphere or tumbled stone should feel distinctly heavier than a piece of glass of the same size. Glass has a specific gravity around 2.5, so the difference is subtle but perceptible with practice.

**Step 4: Check the temperature response.**
Like most stone minerals, larvikite feels cool to the touch initially and warms slowly with your hand. Glass also feels cool, so this test is less diagnostic on its own, but plastic imitations warm more quickly.

**Step 5: Look at price in relation to type.**
Larvikite is a relatively common architectural rock quarried in significant quantities. Tumbled stones and spheres are widely available at modest prices. A piece sold at a very high price — hundreds of dollars for a standard tumbled stone — warrants scrutiny. See [larvikite price guide](/gemstones/larvikite/price-guide) for current retail context.

## Glass Imitations

Glass can be manufactured in dark grey-black tones and can even be given an iridescent surface coating that produces a vaguely schiller-like effect. Key differences from genuine larvikite:

- Glass schiller is a **surface effect** (a coating); it does not shift with the same three-dimensional depth as larvikite's internal schiller
- Glass has **no grain structure** under magnification
- Glass **chips differently** — the edges of a chip are conchoidal (curved and shell-like), whereas larvikite fractures along grain boundaries and produces rougher, more angular breakage
- Glass may have **bubbles** visible under magnification

## Dyed or Coated Imitations

Larvikite itself is not typically dyed, as its natural color and schiller are its selling point. However, other dark rocks — basalt, gabbro, black tourmaline matrix — may occasionally be sold as larvikite. Basalt and gabbro lack the characteristic schiller of larvikite's feldspar content. If a dark stone has no blue-silver flash when tilted, it is likely not larvikite.

Some polished stones sold under trade names like "Blue Pearl Granite" may be from other sources than Norway and may have different mineral compositions. The schiller test is the most reliable field check.

## Labradorite vs. Larvikite

[Labradorite](/gemstones/labradorite) and larvikite are related: both are feldspar-rich rocks displaying schiller effects. The difference is primarily in appearance: labradorite typically shows a broader, more colorful schiller (including gold, green, and orange tones) on an individual mineral surface, while larvikite's schiller is more uniformly silver-blue and seen across the dark matrix of the rock. Labradorite cabochons are usually cut from a single transparent to translucent mineral; larvikite is always opaque and visibly coarse-grained.
