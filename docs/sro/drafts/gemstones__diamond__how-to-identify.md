# How to Identify Real Diamond vs. Fakes and Imitations

Real diamond is pure crystalline carbon with a Mohs hardness of 10, a refractive index of 2.417, and exceptionally high thermal conductivity. Common imitations include cubic zirconia (CZ), moissanite, white sapphire, white topaz, and glass. No single at-home test is conclusive; a combination of tests — or professional gemological analysis — gives the most reliable result.

## What Makes Diamond Physically Distinct

Diamond (chemical formula: C) forms under extreme heat and pressure deep in the Earth's mantle. Its carbon atoms bond in a three-dimensional cubic lattice that makes it the hardest natural substance known, rated 10 on the Mohs scale — the only material that can scratch another diamond. Key measured properties:

| Property | Diamond | Cubic Zirconia | Moissanite | Glass |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mohs hardness | 10 | 8–8.5 | 9.25 | 5–6 |
| Refractive index | 2.417 | 2.15 | 2.65–2.69 | 1.5 |
| Specific gravity | 3.52 | 5.6–5.9 | 3.22 | 2.5 |
| Optical character | Singly refractive | Singly refractive | Doubly refractive | Amorphous |

CZ is significantly denser than diamond: a CZ stone of identical dimensions will weigh noticeably more. Moissanite is doubly refractive, meaning a facet edge viewed through the stone appears doubled under a loupe — diamond does not show this doubling.

## At-Home Tests Worth Trying

### Thermal conductivity (fog test)

Diamond conducts heat more efficiently than almost any other material. Breathe on the stone so a thin layer of condensation forms, then watch how quickly the fog clears. On a diamond, fog disperses almost instantly — within one second — because the stone conducts the warmth away. On glass or CZ, the fog lingers for two or more seconds. This test works better for loose stones than stones set in jewelry, and moissanite also passes because it too has high thermal conductivity.

### The loupe and doubling test

Use a 10× jeweler's loupe. Look through the table (top flat facet) toward the back facets at an angle. In moissanite, the doubled facet edges are visible as a ghosting effect. Diamond shows no such doubling. This test reliably separates diamond from moissanite but not from CZ.

### Specific gravity (for loose stones)

A jeweler's scale and a cup of water allow a quick density check. Diamonds have an SG of 3.52. CZ is 5.6–5.9 — a CZ stone of the same diameter as a 1 ct diamond will weigh roughly 1.6–1.7 ct on a scale. If a stone labeled "1 ct" weighs much more when compared against a reference, suspect CZ.

### UV fluorescence

Roughly 25–35% of natural diamonds fluoresce blue under shortwave UV light; a smaller proportion show other colors or no reaction. This is an indicator, not a test — non-fluorescent genuine diamonds exist, and some simulants also fluoresce — but consistent blue fluorescence is consistent with diamond.

## What At-Home Tests Cannot Determine

Home tests cannot distinguish a natural diamond from a laboratory-grown diamond. Lab-grown diamonds are chemically, optically, and physically identical to natural diamonds (both are pure carbon, Mohs 10, RI 2.417) and share all the physical properties above. The GIA notes that distinguishing lab-grown from natural diamonds typically requires advanced spectroscopic equipment. For that determination, see [lab-created vs. natural gemstones](/compare/lab-created-vs-natural-gemstones).

## Professional Testing

A trained gemologist with a thermal probe (diamond tester) can distinguish diamond from most simulants in seconds. Moissanite-specific testers are needed to separate moissanite from diamond because both pass thermal probes designed for older-generation testers. For high-value purchases, GIA certification is the most reliable documentation: the grading report includes the stone's unique measurements, grade, and, since 2007, a notation if the diamond is laboratory-grown.

Avoid relying solely on a scratch test: scratching glass with an unset stone only confirms hardness above Mohs 5–6 (which also includes sapphire and many other stones), and scratching can damage both the setting and soft surfaces.

## Common Imitations and Their Tells

**Cubic zirconia** is the most common diamond simulant. It is heavier, slightly warmer to the touch before handling (lower thermal conductivity), and flawless to the naked eye — real diamonds rarely are. It also shows a slight orange flash (fire) under incandescent light.

**Moissanite** was once rare but is now lab-grown and widely sold. Its RI (2.65–2.69) gives stronger fire and brilliance than diamond; some buyers find the sparkle appears "too perfect." The doubling test under a loupe is the fastest check.

**White sapphire** is hard (Mohs 9) but much less brilliant than diamond; it often looks glassy and lacks fire.

**Glass** is soft (Mohs 5–6), scratches easily, and fogs for a noticeably long time.
