# Opalite Price Guide: What It Costs and Why

Opalite is synthetic glass produced in volume at low cost, making it one of the most affordable stones in crystal practice. Tumbled stones retail for roughly $1 to $5 each; simple jewelry runs $2 to $25. Price drivers are size, cut complexity, and surface treatments. The main way buyers overpay is purchasing opalite sold as natural opal or moonstone.

## What Opalite Is

Opalite is a trade name for synthetic opalescent glass, not a natural mineral. It is manufactured from glass, sometimes with added dolomite or resin, and has a Mohs hardness of approximately 5.5 to 6. It is produced primarily in China and sold globally. Because it is man-made and produced in quantity, raw material costs are low and consistent — there is no geological rarity driving its price. For identification guidance and how to tell it from natural opal or moonstone, see [how to identify opalite](/gemstones/opalite/how-to-identify). The full property profile is at the [opalite](/gemstones/opalite) hub page.

## What Drives Opalite's Price

### Size

Larger pieces command higher prices. A single tumbled opalite stone under 2 cm typically costs $1–$2; a 4 cm piece may run $4–$6. Carved spheres and figures scale further with size, but remain affordable compared to equivalent sizes in natural semi-precious stones.

### Clarity and air bubbles

Opalite is glass, and the manufacturing process sometimes leaves spherical air bubbles trapped inside. Pieces with visible bubbles are considered lower quality and are priced accordingly. Clear, bubble-free pieces with a consistent opalescent glow command slightly higher prices in retail settings. Examine pieces in good light before purchasing.

### Surface treatment

Some opalite pieces receive additional surface treatments: aura coatings (metallic vacuum deposition that adds rainbow surface iridescence), dye additions, or specialty colorations such as pink or purple opalite. These treated pieces may carry a modest premium — typically $1–$5 more per piece for aura-treated items — but should be clearly labeled as treated. Standard untreated opalite glass has no surface coating; its color is internal.

### Cut and form

Tumbled stones require minimal processing and are the least expensive form. Cabochon cuts for jewelry settings require more labor. Carved shapes — hearts, skulls, spheres, animal figures — add both labor cost and material waste, pushing prices toward the upper end of the range. Cut does not affect value the way it does for precious stones: opalite's lack of cleavage means it is workable in many shapes without the structural risk that affects, say, topaz or calcite.

### Seller markup

Crystal retail stores and metaphysical shops often mark up tumbled stones substantially above wholesale cost. The same piece that costs $1.50 at a bulk mineral supplier may sell for $4–$6 in a retail crystal shop. This is a normal retail margin, not a sign of misrepresentation. Buying from wholesale suppliers or in bulk reduces this margin significantly. For guidance on ethical sourcing, see [ethical crystal sourcing](/learn/ethical-crystal-sourcing).

## The Main Overpayment Risk: Mislabeling

The single most common way buyers overpay for opalite is purchasing it while believing it is natural opal or moonstone. Natural precious opal sells for $10 to several hundred dollars per carat depending on quality; natural moonstone retails from $5 to $50+ per carat. Opalite glass at those prices is a significant overpayment.

Sellers occasionally list opalite as "opal," "sea opal," or "opal moonstone" without clarifying that it is man-made glass. The test: if a piece is priced like opalite but labeled as a natural stone, ask the seller directly whether it is natural or man-made. Genuine dealers will confirm readily.

For the physical tests that distinguish opalite glass from natural opal and moonstone, see [how to identify opalite](/gemstones/opalite/how-to-identify). For the durability context that affects long-term value at Mohs 5.5–6, see [Mohs hardness scale](/learn/mohs-hardness-scale).

## Is Opalite Worth Buying?

Opalite is worth its price for what it is: an attractive, durable-enough decorative glass with established use in crystal practice. It is not worth paying natural-opal or moonstone prices for. If the appeal is the appearance and the traditional associations in crystal practice, opalite delivers both at a fraction of the cost of the natural stones it resembles. If the appeal is natural mineral origin or geological rarity, opalite does not meet that criterion — it is engineered glass.
