# How to Identify Real Hematite and Spot Fakes

The most reliable test for hematite is the streak: drag the specimen across an unglazed ceramic tile (a streak plate) and genuine hematite always leaves a red to reddish-brown powder, even if the stone itself looks silver or gray. No other silver-gray metallic mineral produces this red streak, making it the single most useful field identification.

## The Streak Test: Most Reliable

Hematite's streak is consistently red to reddish-brown regardless of which variety is being tested. Specular hematite (silver, shiny, micaceous), botryoidal kidney ore (rounded, dark gray), and earthy red ochre are all the same mineral — Fe₂O₃ — and all leave red powder on a streak plate. When performing the test, brush away any loose debris fragments first: dense metallic hematite can crumble slightly, leaving large glittery fragments alongside the true powder. Shake or lightly brush these free, then read the embedded powder remaining on the plate's textured surface.

Magnetite is commonly confused with hematite because both have dark metallic appearances. Magnetite produces a **black** streak. A black streak rules out hematite identification.

## The Magnet Test: Identifies the Most Common Fake

Real hematite (Fe₂O₃) is not strongly magnetic. A genuine specimen may show very slight attraction to a strong neodymium magnet if it contains magnetite as an impurity, but it will not jump to a common magnet or hold one firmly. If a piece of "hematite" is strongly attracted to a refrigerator magnet — or if the magnet sticks firmly and is difficult to pull away — the piece is almost certainly not genuine hematite.

**"Magnetic hematite"** sold in gift shops, tourist markets, and some crystal retailers is a man-made material, typically sintered barium ferrite or strontium ferrite. It has a different chemical composition from hematite and no mineralogical relationship to it. Purchasing these products for decorative or personal reasons is not a problem, but they should not be marketed as the natural mineral. The magnet test is the fastest way to tell the difference.

## Weight and Hardness

Genuine hematite is notably heavy: its specific gravity is 5.0 to 5.3, roughly twice the density of the same volume of quartz. A piece that feels unexpectedly light for its apparent size warrants further testing. Glass imitations and resin pieces will feel considerably lighter.

Hematite's Mohs hardness is 5 to 6.5. It will scratch window glass (approximately Mohs 5.5) and cannot be scratched by a copper coin (approximately Mohs 3). A piece that is easily scratched with a fingernail (Mohs 2.5) is not hematite. Hard, heavy, metallic pieces that produce a red streak are very likely genuine.

## Appearance Clues

Hematite's luster ranges from metallic to submetallic, and in earthy varieties it can appear dull or matte. Its color spans gray-black to silver in metallic forms and red to brown in earthy or powdered forms. Hematite has no cleavage and breaks with an uneven to subconchoidal fracture. Be more skeptical of a piece that shows:

- An iridescent rainbow sheen across the whole surface (common on synthetic ferrite, not typical of natural hematite)
- A perfectly uniform, plastic-looking finish with no variation in texture
- Very light weight for its apparent volume
- A black streak rather than red

Hematite is found in many forms including tabular crystals, rosettes, massive botryoidal kidney ore, columnar or fibrous masses, and earthy aggregates. Each form looks different, but all produce the diagnostic red streak.

## Dyed, Treated, and Synthetic Material

Natural hematite is rarely dyed — its natural gray-black or silver color is its appeal, and its red streak comes from the mineral itself, not a coating. Treated "hematite" jewelry more often refers to haematine or hematitine, a synthetic sintered material used for beads and cabochons. Haematine behaves similarly to magnetic hematite in tests: it is often magnetic and may not produce a clean red streak. The label "hematine" on packaging is an honest disclosure; "hematite" on packaging for a strongly magnetic bead is not.
