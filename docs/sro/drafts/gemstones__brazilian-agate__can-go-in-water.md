# Can Brazilian Agate Go in Water?

Brazilian Agate can go in water. As a variety of chalcedony (SiO2, Mohs hardness 6.5–7), it is chemically inert and does not dissolve, rust, or react with water. The main practical exception is dyed material: the vivid blues, reds, greens, and purples common in retail Brazilian Agate are almost always the result of dyeing, and some dyed pieces can transfer color when soaked. A quick pre-check tells you which category yours is in.

## Why Brazilian Agate Is Generally Water-Safe

Agate is a microcrystalline form of quartz (silicon dioxide, SiO2). Silicon dioxide is one of the most stable minerals in the geological record. It does not react with liquid water at room temperature, does not corrode, and does not leach harmful compounds. With a Mohs hardness of 6.5–7, agate is harder than most tableware glass and will not soften, swell, or crumble when wet.

This places Brazilian Agate firmly in the "water-safe" category by standard mineralogical criteria. The relevant risk factors that would disqualify a stone — high solubility (selenite, halite), reactive metal content (pyrite, hematite), or very low hardness (calcite, gypsum) — are absent here.

## The Dye Exception: What to Check Before Soaking

Dyeing is standard practice in the Brazilian Agate trade. The deeply saturated colors seen at retail are produced by soaking porous agate nodules in metal-salt solutions and heat-treating the result. This is openly disclosed at the wholesale level and is not considered fraud — it is an industry norm.

The dyes are generally stable under dry contact and normal wear. Prolonged soaking is where some pieces, particularly those with less thorough finishing, can release color into the water or onto adjacent surfaces.

**How to test your stone before soaking:**
1. Dampen a white cotton swab or cloth.
2. Rub a small area on the back or a less visible part of the stone.
3. If color transfers to the swab, treat the stone as dye-sensitive and limit water contact to quick rinses under 30 seconds, followed by immediate thorough drying.
4. If no color transfers, the dye is well-fixed and brief soaking (up to 15–20 minutes) is safe.

Natural-color Brazilian Agate — grey, white, pale beige, or light brown — contains no dye and can be rinsed or soaked without this concern.

## Salt Water

Salt water is safe for the mineral itself, as SiO2 does not react with dissolved salt. The same dye-bleed caution applies: if the stone passes the swab test, brief saltwater contact is not harmful. Prolonged soaking in saltwater (hours or days) is not recommended for any polished agate, as it can gradually dull a high-polished surface over multiple exposures.

For more context on salt and crystals, see [salt-safe crystals](/learn/salt-safe-crystals).

## Sunlight After Washing

Drying Brazilian Agate in direct sunlight after rinsing is safe for the mineral. Brief sunlight exposure (under an hour) poses no structural risk to chalcedony. However, dyed material in prolonged direct sun may fade over months to years — this applies to sunbathing habits, not to a single drying session. For guidance on sun exposure during recharging, see [sun-safe crystals](/learn/sun-safe-crystals).

## Practical Cleaning with Water

To clean Brazilian Agate with water:
1. Use cool or lukewarm water — avoid hot water, as sudden temperature changes can stress polished surfaces.
2. Use a soft brush or cloth to remove surface dust and oils.
3. Rinse briefly under running water.
4. Dry promptly with a soft cloth and allow to air-dry completely before storing.

Avoid ultrasonic cleaners. They can amplify any existing micro-fractures and, in polished display pieces, may loosen adhesive settings.
