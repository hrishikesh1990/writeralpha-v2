# Blue Onyx Price Guide — What It Costs and Why

Blue Onyx is an accessible, mid-to-budget gemstone. Most tumbled stones and bead bracelets retail in the range of $2–$15 per piece; small to medium cabochons for jewelry are typically $1–$10 per piece depending on size and finish; decorative carvings and larger display pieces vary widely, from around $20 for simple shapes to several hundred dollars for museum-quality material from Argentina. Prices checked September 2026.

## What Blue Onyx Actually Is (and Why It Matters for Price)

Blue Onyx is a dyed banded chalcedony (SiO₂, Mohs 6.5–7). Most commercial material is chalcedony or banded agate that has been dyed blue through a standard gem-trade process. The base material is abundant and inexpensive; the value comes from polish quality, size, carving complexity, and — for rarer natural-color pieces — origin and color depth.

Because dyeing is standard and widely disclosed in reputable gem trade, dyed Blue Onyx is not considered inferior to other affordable ornamental stones. The key is disclosure: a seller who states "dyed" is being honest; one who implies rarity or premium provenance for ordinary commercial Blue Onyx may not be.

## What Drives the Price

**Treatment and disclosure** — Dyed Blue Onyx is priced as an ornamental chalcedony. Natural-color Blue Onyx (where the color arises from the Tyndall effect in fine-grained chalcedony, mainly from Argentina) commands a premium when correctly identified, but is rarely found at mass-market retail without disclosure issues.

**Size and weight** — Larger pieces require more raw material and more carving time. Price scales roughly with size.

**Polish and surface quality** — High-gloss polish on an evenly banded piece is worth more than a roughly tumbled stone with visible tool marks.

**Banding uniformity** — Crisp, parallel blue-and-white banding is considered more desirable than muddled or partially obscured bands.

**Origin** — The highest-quality Blue Onyx material is traditionally associated with Argentina. Pieces accurately attributed to Argentinian deposits may carry a premium. Most mass-market pieces come from Mexico, India, Brazil, or the United States.

**Form** — Cabochons and beads are priced on the supply chain of mass production. Carved pieces involve handwork and are priced accordingly.

## How to Avoid Overpaying

1. **Expect dyeing disclosure.** Most Blue Onyx at retail price points is dyed. A seller claiming "natural color" without lab documentation for a $5 tumbled stone is likely marketing rather than accurate.
2. **Compare like for like.** A $3 tumbled stone from one seller and a $30 tumbled stone from another may be the same material. Check size, finish, and whether any special claim justifies the difference.
3. **Be skeptical of metaphysical price premiums.** Labels such as "high-vibration," "activated," or "Reiki-charged" do not change the mineralogy and do not justify a higher price relative to the same physical stone.
4. **Buy from sellers who disclose treatment.** Treatment disclosure is a sign of professional practice. Sellers who do not mention treatment are not necessarily selling fakes, but they are giving you less information to evaluate.
