# How to Identify Real Yellow Calcite

Real Yellow Calcite has a Mohs hardness of 3, produces a white streak, fizzes when a drop of weak acid touches it, and shows perfect rhombohedral cleavage when broken. These four properties, used together, distinguish genuine yellow calcite from glass imitations, dyed or misrepresented stones, and yellow quartz minerals such as citrine. No single test is definitive in isolation; apply at least three before drawing a conclusion.

## The Four Key Physical Tests

### 1. Hardness test

Yellow Calcite sits at Mohs hardness 3 — one of the defining minerals of the scale. A steel coin (copper-plated zinc, Mohs ~3.5) will scratch it. A fingernail (Mohs ~2.5) will not scratch it. A piece of glass or a steel knife (Mohs ~5.5) will scratch it easily.

**Procedure:** Find an inconspicuous spot, preferably on the base of the piece. Press the corner of a steel coin firmly across the surface and look for a visible groove. If you see a groove, the material is softer than steel — consistent with calcite. If the coin slides off without scratching, the material is harder than steel, which rules out calcite.

**Caution:** A powdery residue without an actual groove means the coin abraded itself on a harder material; this is not a scratch on the stone. Blow the powder away and look for a groove to confirm.

### 2. Acid test

Calcite's most reliable identifier is its reaction with dilute acids. Even weak household vinegar (acetic acid, ~5%) will cause a gentle fizz on a freshly broken calcite surface. Stronger acid (dilute HCl, used in professional settings) fizzes vigorously.

**Procedure:** Place a drop of white vinegar on an inconspicuous spot or the base of the stone. Genuine calcite will show small bubbles — carbon dioxide released as the carbonate reacts with acid. The reaction may be subtle on polished surfaces and more obvious on raw or broken surfaces.

**Note:** Many carbonate minerals will fizz with acid. The acid test alone does not distinguish yellow calcite from other carbonates; combine it with the hardness and cleavage tests.

### 3. Cleavage and streak

Break or examine any chipped edges. Genuine calcite breaks with perfect flat faces at approximately 75° angles — rhombohedral cleavage in three directions. The cleavage faces will be reflective and flat rather than irregular.

Streak test: Drag the stone across an unglazed porcelain tile (the back of a bathroom tile). Calcite produces a white streak regardless of its body color. A yellow streak suggests a different mineral.

### 4. Specific gravity (advanced)

Genuine calcite has a specific gravity of approximately 2.71. Weigh the stone in air, then suspended in water, and calculate the ratio. This requires a scale capable of measuring to at least one decimal place and a container of water. It is impractical for most buyers but useful for larger pieces where investment is higher.

## Distinguishing Yellow Calcite from Common Imitations

### Glass

Glass is harder than calcite (Mohs ~5.5) and will not be scratched by a steel coin under normal pressure. Glass also shows conchoidal (shell-like) fracture rather than flat cleavage faces, and will not react with acid. Glass feels noticeably cooler to the touch than calcite for the first few seconds of handling.

### Citrine (yellow quartz)

[Citrine](/gemstones/citrine) is often confused with yellow calcite because of color similarity. Citrine is quartz (SiO2) with Mohs hardness 7 — much harder. A steel coin will not scratch it. Quartz does not react with vinegar. Quartz has no cleavage (it fractures conchoidally). If a "yellow calcite" piece passes a hardness test at Mohs 7, it is probably citrine or another quartz variety.

### Dyed calcite

Some calcite is artificially dyed to produce more saturated colors. To check for dye: apply a small amount of acetone (nail varnish remover) to a cotton bud and rub an inconspicuous spot. If color transfers to the cotton, the stone has been surface-dyed. Natural color in calcite is structural and will not transfer. Note that an acetone-negative result does not rule out all dyeing methods; some use penetrating dyes that are harder to detect without laboratory analysis.

### Selenite and other soft white/yellow minerals

Selenite (gypsum, Mohs 2) is softer than calcite and can be scratched with a fingernail. It does not react with acid as vigorously as calcite. Its surface often shows a silky, fibrous texture quite different from the waxy to vitreous luster of calcite.

## Visual Signals That Do Not Prove Authenticity

Color uniformity, surface polish, and weight alone are not reliable identifiers. Uneven color distribution — patchy yellows, white inclusions, or minor color variation — is normal in natural calcite and can be a positive indicator that the stone is genuine rather than a uniformly colored imitation. Very uniform, saturated yellow color throughout a piece is more consistent with dyed material or glass.

A very low price does not necessarily mean a fake; yellow calcite is an abundant mineral and genuinely inexpensive. See the [yellow calcite price guide](/gemstones/yellow-calcite/price-guide) for expected retail ranges. A very high price is not a reliable indicator of authenticity either.
