# Amethyst Price Guide: What It Costs and Why

Amethyst is one of the most affordable major colored gemstones. Commercial faceted material can retail for as little as $3 to $15 per carat, while fine saturated material with strong color and precision cutting reaches $20 to $60 per carat or more. Exceptional top-color pieces ("Deep Siberian" reddish-purple) are found at $100 to $250 per carat and above. Prices checked September 2026; they vary by grade, seller, and market conditions.

## What Drives Amethyst's Price

Unlike rare gemstones, amethyst is abundant — large deposits exist in Brazil, Uruguay, and Zambia — so large size alone does not significantly raise the per-carat price. Color quality is the dominant value factor, followed by cut quality and clarity.

### Color

GIA identifies strong reddish-purple to pure purple with no visible color zoning as the finest amethyst color. The trade term "Deep Siberian" refers to the most prized tone: a deep, slightly reddish purple with strong saturation and no pale windows. As color becomes paler or more washed-out, value drops substantially. Brownish or bronze tints also reduce desirability significantly. Visible zoning — patches of alternating light and dark purple — lowers value in faceted stones, where buyers expect even color.

### Cut

Commercial amethyst is produced in standard calibrated shapes at low cost. Precision custom cutting, which requires more labor and produces stronger brilliance and more even color distribution in the face-up view, can multiply the per-carat price of the same rough material several times over.

### Clarity

Most faceted amethyst on the market is expected to be eye-clean (no inclusions visible to the unaided eye). African material — particularly from Zambia — often has more inclusions but can command a premium for its distinctive raspberry-purple color. Large fractures, cloudy regions, or inclusions that break up transparency reduce both visual quality and price.

### Treatment

Heat treatment is used to lighten over-dark amethyst or to convert it to citrine. Treated material should be disclosed and is typically priced lower than equivalent untreated stones. Synthetic amethyst (lab-grown quartz with identical chemistry to natural material) is common in the trade and sells at a substantial discount to natural material; it should be disclosed. For how to tell natural from treated or synthetic material, see [how to identify amethyst](/gemstones/amethyst/how-to-identify).

### Origin

Amethyst from Zambia and Uruguay tends to show deeper, more saturated color than typical Brazilian material. Origin alone rarely drives a dramatic price premium for amethyst (unlike, say, ruby or sapphire), but it can matter at the fine end of the market.

## Indicative Retail Price Ranges (September 2026)

Prices vary by seller, form factor, and grade. The table below shows indicative ranges for faceted natural amethyst; verify current dealer prices before purchasing.

| Grade | Approximate retail (per carat) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Pale / commercial | $3–$15 | Often light-toned or visibly zoned |
| Medium color, eye-clean | $5–$30 | The most widely available tier |
| Strong saturated purple | $20–$60 | Good cut, minimal zoning |
| Fine reddish-purple / top color | $50–$150 | Even color, superior cutting |
| Exceptional "Deep Siberian" | $100–$250+ | Top reddish-purple, precision cut |
| Precision custom or designer cuts | $30–$100+ at same color grade | Labor premium on top of stone value |

Tumbled stones and raw specimens are typically sold by piece rather than by carat and range from roughly $2 to $15 per piece for common commercial material, depending on size and color. Crystal clusters and geodes are priced by specimen and vary widely; there is no meaningful per-carat benchmark for these forms.

## Why Large Size Barely Raises the Price

For most rare gemstones, price per carat rises steeply with size because large clean crystals are uncommon. Amethyst is the exception: large, clean quartz crystals form naturally and are readily available. A 20-carat amethyst in commercial color may cost only marginally more per carat than a 2-carat stone of the same quality. This makes amethyst excellent value for statement pieces and large jewelry.

## How to Avoid Overpaying

- **Pay for color, not size.** A small, deeply saturated stone will outperform a large, pale one both aesthetically and in value retention.
- **Ask about treatment.** Untreated natural amethyst should be stated as such. Heat-treated material and synthetic amethyst must be disclosed and should be priced accordingly.
- **Compare similar grades.** Seek out multiple sellers offering stones in the same color and clarity range before committing.
- **Request documentation on fine stones.** For stones priced above $50 per carat, a laboratory report from GIA or a comparable lab confirms treatment status and natural origin.
- **Understand the difference between forms.** Commercial calibrated cuts are inexpensive and widely available. High prices for common commercial material without exceptional color or cut are difficult to justify.
