# Agate Price Guide — What It Costs and Why

Common agate is one of the most affordable gemstones in the world: a tumbled stone costs $1 to $5 at retail, and polished slabs run $10 to $80. Exceptional specimens and premium varieties — fire agate, Botswana agate with fine banding, large geodes — reach hundreds or thousands of dollars. Price is driven primarily by variety, banding quality, size, and polish.

## Retail Price Ranges by Form (September 2026)

Prices below are drawn from retail market data and vary by seller, origin, and grade. They are per-piece unless noted.

| Form | Typical retail range |
|---|---|
| Tumbled stone (common agate) | $1–$5 per stone |
| Polished slab or slice | $10–$80 |
| Botswana agate tumbled | $2–$8 per stone |
| Botswana agate polished slab | $10–$40 |
| Blue lace agate tumbled | $3–$10 per stone |
| Blue lace agate cabochon | $15–$80 |
| Moss agate tumbled | $2–$8 per stone |
| Moss agate polished | $10–$50 |
| Fire agate (weak fire, rough) | $5–$20 per piece |
| Fire agate (good fire, cabochon) | $50–$300 |
| Fire agate (exceptional, multi-color) | $300–$3,000+ |
| Flower agate tumbled | $5–$20 per stone |
| Grape agate small cluster | $5–$15 |
| Large display geode or slab | $50–$1,000+ depending on size |

These ranges reflect retail, not wholesale. Wholesale pricing for common agate tumbled material runs considerably lower per piece when bought in bulk.

## What Drives Agate Prices

### Variety

Not all agates are equal. Common banded agate from South American or African deposits is abundant and inexpensive. Rarer varieties command premiums:

- **Fire agate** is iridescent and found primarily in the Southwestern United States and Mexico. The iridescence comes from thin layers of limonite within the stone, and exceptional examples with strong, multi-color fire are scarce.
- **Blue lace agate** comes primarily from Namibia. Demand has increased in recent years while supply from the main historical deposits has tightened, pushing prices upward.
- **Flower agate** (from Madagascar) and **grape agate** (from Indonesia) are newer discoveries and command higher prices partly due to novelty.

### Banding quality

Agate is valued in part for the visual impact of its banding. Stones with sharp contrast between bands, unusual geometric patterns (fortification patterns, plume inclusions, scenic landscapes) command premiums over uniformly banded or plain material.

### Size and weight

Larger pieces cost more. A polished agate slab that weighs 2 kg commands a higher price not only because of material volume but because large, crack-free agate slices are harder to find than small tumbled pieces.

### Polish and cut

A well-polished surface highlights banding and translucency. Cabochons (dome-cut) show agate's waxy luster well and are used in jewelry. Free-form polished pieces and matched bookend pairs carry value for display. Rough, unpolished material costs less per gram but requires lapidary work to realize its visual potential.

### Treatment

Much commercial agate is dyed to produce colors that do not occur naturally — vivid blue, bright green, cherry red. Dyed material is still genuine agate but is generally worth less than naturally colored material of equivalent banding quality. Natural color agate commands a premium among collectors. Ask sellers explicitly whether material is dyed. See [how to identify agate](/gemstones/agate/how-to-identify) for visual signs of dyeing.

### Origin

Certain origins carry collector premiums. Brazilian Lake Superior agate, Fairburn agate from South Dakota, and sagenite agate from specific localities attract collectors willing to pay multiples of the standard price for documented provenance.

## How to Avoid Overpaying

1. **Know the form and grade.** A $30 "agate slice" and a $2 tumbled stone are both real agate — the price reflects size and finish, not quality fraud.
2. **Ask whether it is dyed.** Natural-color pieces are worth more. If a seller cannot or will not say, assume it may be dyed.
3. **Buy common varieties from volume sellers.** Tumbled agate is a commodity; paying $15 for a tumbled stone that costs $2 at a mineral show is avoidable.
4. **Pay premiums only for documented rarity.** Fire agate with documented locality, blue lace agate with natural provenance, and large collector-grade specimens have legitimate reasons to cost more.
5. **Check the return policy.** Reputable sellers accept returns if material is misrepresented.
