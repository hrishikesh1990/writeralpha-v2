# How to Identify Real Emerald vs Fakes and Imitations

Real emerald is a chromium-bearing beryl that is almost always visibly included, usually oil-treated, and carries specific optical properties measurable with gemological tools. The most reliable identification is a laboratory certificate from GIA or a similar recognized laboratory. Without a certificate, a combination of inclusion observation under magnification, optical testing, and awareness of what imitations look like is the most practical approach for a non-specialist.

## What "Real" Means for Emerald

Before testing, it helps to define the categories:

- **Natural emerald**: Grown in the earth, mined, cut. Inclusions expected. Usually fracture-filled.
- **Synthetic (lab-grown) emerald**: Chemically and optically identical to natural, but grown in a laboratory. Not a fake — but not mined. Worth significantly less per carat than natural.
- **Imitations**: Materials that resemble emerald but are chemically different — green glass, green tourmaline, tsavorite garnet, green quartz, green cubic zirconia, assembled doublets or triplets.
- **Treated natural emerald**: Natural stone that has been fracture-filled with oil or resin to improve appearance. Routine in the trade; not a "fake," but the treatment level affects value.

For how pricing differs across these categories, see [emerald price guide](/gemstones/emerald/price-guide). For broader context on lab-created vs natural stones, see [lab-created vs natural gemstones](/compare/lab-created-vs-natural-gemstones).

## Step 1 — Look at the Inclusions

Natural emerald is a GIA Type III gemstone, meaning inclusions are expected and normal. Virtually all natural emeralds have a characteristic internal landscape — called the "jardin" (French for garden) — visible under magnification.

**What to look for:**
- Under a 10x loupe, natural emerald will show a complex of fractures, two-phase inclusions (liquid + gas bubble), or three-phase inclusions (liquid + gas + solid crystal) in Colombian stones.
- Inclusions may also include crystals of other minerals, growth tubes, and healed fractures.
- Very few natural emeralds are eye-clean.

**Red flags:**
- A stone that is completely clean under 10x magnification is more likely glass, synthetic, or another material. Eye-clean natural emerald does exist but is extremely rare and commands top prices.
- Green glass is internally clean or shows only rounded gas bubbles, not crystalline inclusions.
- Doublets (two pieces of material glued together) may show a visible join line at the girdle, often filled with a green adhesive.

## Step 2 — Check Optical Properties

A gemological refractometer measures refractive index (RI). Natural emerald has an RI of approximately 1.577 to 1.583 (GIA) and shows birefringence (it splits light into two rays). Key distinctions:

| Material | Refractive Index | Birefringence |
|---|---|---|
| Natural emerald | 1.566 – 1.600 | 0.004 – 0.010 |
| Synthetic emerald (flux/hydrothermal) | ~1.560 – 1.565 | 0.003 – 0.004 |
| Green glass | ~1.50 – 1.70 (varies) | None (isotropic) |
| Tsavorite garnet | ~1.739 – 1.744 | None (isotropic) |
| Green tourmaline | 1.624 – 1.644 | 0.018 – 0.030 |

Glass has no birefringence because it is amorphous. If a stone shows no birefringence, it is not emerald.

Specific gravity (SG) also helps: natural emerald SG is 2.67 to 2.78. Synthetic emeralds run slightly lower (flux synthetics ~2.65–2.66; hydrothermal ~2.67–2.69). These tests require equipment and are best performed by a gemologist.

## Step 3 — Observe Color and Pleochroism

Natural emerald shows pleochroism: it appears slightly different colors from different viewing angles — typically bluish green and yellowish green. Viewing through a dichroscope or simply rotating a faceted stone under a point light source can reveal this effect.

The characteristic color of fine emerald is described as a slightly bluish, pure green of medium to medium-dark tone. Very blue or very yellow greens are less likely to be high-quality natural emerald, though they may still be natural stone of lower grade.

## Step 4 — Consider the Treatment

Approximately 90 percent of natural emeralds are fracture-filled. Under magnification, this filling may show as a slightly different luster or an irregular surface within fissures. Some laboratories distinguish between "no oil," "minor oil," "moderate oil," and "significant oil" treatments — disclosure of treatment is standard practice among reputable sellers.

A stone sold as emerald with no mention of treatment, at a price well below market, is either synthetic, significantly included, or not emerald at all.

## Step 5 — Get a Laboratory Certificate

The definitive method is a certificate from GIA, Gübelin, SSEF, or another major gemological laboratory. A certificate confirms:
- Species and variety (natural emerald vs synthetic vs imitation)
- Treatment type and degree
- Geographic origin (for significant stones)

For any emerald purchase above a few hundred dollars, a certificate is strongly advisable. See [are online crystals real](/learn/are-online-crystals-real) for broader guidance on sourcing.

## Common Imitations at a Glance

| Imitation | How to Spot It |
|---|---|
| Green glass | No inclusions or only gas bubbles; no birefringence; may show mold marks |
| Green synthetic spinel | Different RI; no pleochroism |
| Tsavorite garnet | Higher RI (~1.74); no birefringence; no pleochroism |
| Green tourmaline | Stronger birefringence; different inclusions |
| Doublet / triplet | Join line visible at girdle; layer structure under magnification |
| Dyed green beryl | UV fluorescence may differ; treatment not disclosed |
