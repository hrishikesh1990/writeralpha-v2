# How to Tell Real Moldavite from Fakes

Genuine moldavite is natural impact glass formed 15 million years ago — and so are many convincing fakes, which are also green glass. Standard rules about "bubbles mean fake" do not apply here. The reliable tests are surface texture, internal inclusions under magnification, density, price, and provenance. No single test is conclusive; the strongest identification uses several together.

## What Real Moldavite Looks Like

### Surface texture

Natural moldavite spent millions of years buried in acidic soil, which etched its exterior into a chaotic pattern of ridges, pits, grooves, and channels — described in gemmology as vermiform (worm-like) sculpture. This pattern is non-repeating: no two areas look identical. Under a 10x loupe, the surface resembles a forest floor — intricate and random.

Molded glass fakes are poured into a mold and either left smooth or given a shallow, repeating texture. The clearest signs of a fake surface are: a uniform shiny or wet-looking appearance; bumps or ridges that repeat in a pattern; mold-seam lines running across the piece; and a generally softer, rounder topography that lacks the sharp, irregular depth of genuine acid etching.

Note: some genuine moldavite is faceted or polished for jewelry settings. A polished piece lacks the natural exterior, so for faceted stones rely on internal features and provenance rather than surface texture.

### Color

Genuine moldavite ranges from pale yellowish-green through olive and bottle-green to brownish-green. The color is often uneven when backlit — darker in thicker areas, lighter where thin. Imitations tend toward a vivid, uniform, almost neon or emerald green that looks more appealing but does not match the natural range. A uniformly bright green color warrants closer examination.

### Internal inclusions under magnification

Moldavite's most diagnostic internal feature is **lechatelierite**: fine threadlike or wire-like inclusions of pure high-temperature silica glass visible under a 10x loupe or microscope. Lechatelierite is created when existing quartz grains are flash-melted during the meteorite impact and re-solidified. It appears as wavy, contorted, optically distinct strands within the moldavite glass.

Gas bubbles are also present in genuine moldavite and are not a sign of fakery — moldavite is glass, so bubbles are expected. What matters is their character: genuine bubbles tend to be elongated, flattened, or irregular, frozen mid-flow as the glass cooled rapidly in flight. Factory glass produces round, spherical, uniformly distributed bubbles. An interior that is completely clear and featureless is a warning sign: genuine moldavite almost always shows some internal structure.

Internal flow lines (schlieren — curved, streaky variations in the glass) are also common in genuine pieces. Flow lines can appear in manufactured glass as well, so they are supporting rather than diagnostic evidence.

## Density and Weight

Genuine moldavite has a specific gravity of approximately 2.32 to 2.38 (Gemdat). Many common glass imitations, particularly those using lead glass to achieve moldavite's green color, have higher density (2.9 or more) and feel noticeably heavier than genuine material of the same size. Resin imitations may feel lighter and slightly warm to the touch. A rough at-home check: if a piece feels notably heavier than you'd expect for its volume, or warms extremely slowly in your hand, density may be off.

Accurate density measurement requires weighing in air and in water with a precision scale — home methods are approximate and unreliable for faceted pieces. Professional gemological testing is needed for a definitive result.

## Price as an Identification Signal

Genuine moldavite is expensive. At retail (September 2026), standard raw specimens from trusted dealers range from approximately $20 to $80 per gram; premium etched pieces from highly regarded localities such as Besednice command more. Faceted stones sell for several hundred dollars per piece. A price below $8 to $10 per gram should be treated as a strong indicator of imitation material.

## Provenance and Seller Practices

Genuine moldavite comes from the Czech Republic — primarily southern Bohemia (Besednice, Chlum, Slavče) and Moravia, with minor occurrences in adjacent parts of Germany and Austria. Any stone described as "African moldavite," "Indian moldavite," or from any location outside Central Europe's Ries strewn field is, by definition, not moldavite.

Trustworthy dealers photograph each piece individually, sell by gram weight, and state a specific Czech locality. Certificates help but are only as reliable as the issuer — sellers of fake moldavite also produce fake certificates. An independent gemological laboratory report with the specimen's photograph and measurable identifiers (weight, dimensions) is the strongest provenance document.

The duplicate test is a practical free check: if a seller's listings show two "unique" specimens with the same outline and surface pattern, they came from the same mold.

## Laboratory Testing

For high-value pieces, laboratory analysis provides definitive answers. Useful techniques include:

- **FTIR spectroscopy**: produces a spectral fingerprint that can be compared with authenticated reference specimens; non-destructive on most samples.
- **Density measurement**: straightforward and accurate in a laboratory.
- **Chemical analysis** (LA-ICP-MS, electron microprobe, X-ray fluorescence): compares elemental composition with known natural moldavite; the most reliable origin evidence because manufactured glass cannot replicate the complete elemental signature of a 15-million-year-old impact event.

## What Cannot Reliably Distinguish Real from Fake

- **Bubbles alone**: both genuine and fake moldavite can have bubbles; bubble shape helps but is not decisive.
- **Surface texture alone**: acid etching and careful molding can imitate natural surfaces convincingly.
- **UV light**: neither genuine moldavite nor most common imitations fluoresce under UV; this test does not help.
- **Refractive index alone**: natural moldavite (RI 1.48–1.54) overlaps with common manufactured glasses.
