# How to Identify Real Hematoid Quartz

Real Hematoid Quartz is a variety of quartz (SiO2, Mohs hardness 7) containing visible iron oxide inclusions — primarily hematite (Fe2O3) — that create red, orange, or golden zones within or on the surface of the crystal. The key identifiers are quartz hardness, vitreous luster, and clearly visible iron inclusions with organic variation in their distribution.

## What Genuine Hematoid Quartz Looks Like

Hematoid Quartz can appear as:

- **Transparent to translucent quartz** with red or orange phantom zones, streaks, or scattered flecks of hematite inside the crystal body
- **Opaque or semi-opaque quartz** pervasively stained reddish-brown or golden by fine iron oxide throughout
- **Harlequin quartz**, a trade name for specimens with bold, distinct red spots or streaks on a clear background
- **Fire quartz**, a trade name emphasizing the vivid red-orange coloration

In all cases, the quartz host and the iron inclusions should both be visible as distinct components, not a uniform single color throughout the entire piece.

A natural specimen shows organic variation: the inclusions are unevenly distributed, may concentrate toward certain growth zones, and create different patterns in different areas of the same stone. ## The Four Main Tests

### 1. Hardness test (Mohs 7)

Quartz scratches glass and ordinary steel. Take a piece of glass (or a steel nail) and attempt to scratch the surface of the specimen. A genuine quartz crystal will leave a scratch on the glass; the quartz surface itself will be unscratched. Glass itself is approximately Mohs 5.5 and cannot scratch quartz.

**What it catches:** Glass imitations (Mohs ~5.5) will not scratch glass as effectively and will show scratches from the quartz. Dyed howlite or magnesite (Mohs 3–4), sometimes sold as colored crystals, will be scratched easily by a steel nail.

Apply the test to an inconspicuous spot on a rough or natural part of the piece. Do not scratch polished display faces.

### 2. Visual inspection of inclusions

Under a 10x jeweler's loupe, genuine hematite inclusions appear as:
- Red, orange, or brown mineral zones with irregular, natural boundaries
- Phantom outlines or platelets — flat, layered formations following the crystal's growth stages
- Fine, scattered flecks with variation in density across different zones

**What it catches:**
- **Glass imitations** show round or elongated gas bubbles, flow lines, and no mineral inclusions of any kind.
- **Dyed stones** (dyed agate, quartz, or howlite) show dye pooling in cracks and grain boundaries rather than internal inclusions. The color often looks too even and concentrates at surface fractures.
- **Heat-treated specimens** sold as natural may show unnaturally uniform red coloring without distinct hematite zones. Some sellers heat low-quality material to intensify red coloration; natural specimens show organic, zone-by-zone variation.

### 3. Temperature test

Natural quartz feels noticeably cool to the touch when first held, even at room temperature. Glass objects warm up faster in the hand than natural stone. This test is rough and not definitive, but genuine quartz pieces larger than a few centimeters will feel cool for several seconds after you pick them up. Resin or plastic imitations warm quickly.

### 4. Weight (specific gravity ~2.65)

Quartz has a specific gravity of approximately 2.65. Glass is similar (~2.5), so this test does not separate glass from quartz well, but it does catch plastic or resin imitations, which are noticeably lighter. A genuine piece should feel appropriately heavy for its size.

## Distinguishing Hematoid Quartz from Related Stones

| Stone | How it differs from Hematoid Quartz |
|---|---|
| Strawberry Quartz | Pink inclusions from lepidocrocite (not hematite); finer, more evenly distributed; paler pink not red-orange |
| Tangerine Quartz | Iron coating is on the surface only, not internal inclusions; the coating can sometimes be rubbed or polished off |
| Red Jasper | Opaque microcrystalline quartz with uniform red throughout; no distinct clear quartz and separate inclusion zones |
| Carnelian | Orange-red chalcedony (microcrystalline quartz); translucent but without distinct hematite inclusions |

## Where Price Is an Identification Signal

Genuine Hematoid Quartz from Madagascar or Brazil is inexpensive relative to rarer gems but not free.
