# Can Green Opal Go in Water?

Green Opal can tolerate a brief rinse under cool running water for cleaning purposes, but prolonged soaking is not advisable. The stone is a hydrated silica mineraloid with naturally high water content, and repeated or extended water exposure — especially hot water, salt water, or chemical cleaners — can cause surface crazing (fine fracture lines) or gradual degradation. Keep any water contact short and dry the stone promptly.

## What Green Opal Is Made Of

[Green Opal](/gemstones/green-opal) is an amorphous hydrated silica mineraloid with the formula SiO2·nH2O. Unlike crystalline quartz, it has no ordered lattice structure and contains between 3 and 10 percent water by weight distributed throughout its matrix. This high water content is what gives opal its characteristic properties — and also what makes water exposure complicated.

The surface of opal is hydrophilic: it readily forms hydrogen bonds with water molecules. Under brief, controlled contact, this is harmless. But prolonged soaking allows water to penetrate micro-fissures in the stone, and thermal or chemical changes during that process can cause the structure to craze — developing a network of fine cracks on the surface.

Its Mohs hardness is 5.5 to 6, and toughness is very poor to fair, meaning the stone is also physically fragile. It will not dissolve in plain water, but the combination of low toughness and water sensitivity means it rewards careful handling.

## What Is Safe

**Brief cool rinse**: Holding Green Opal under cool or room-temperature running water for one to two minutes to remove dust or surface grime is generally safe. Dry the stone immediately and completely with a soft cloth. The GIA recommends warm, soapy water and a soft cloth for cleaning opal jewelry — the same principle applies: short contact, gentle action, prompt drying.

**Occasional light cleaning**: Wiping the surface with a damp cloth is lower-risk than running water and is suitable for regular light cleaning.

## What to Avoid

| Contact type | Risk |
|---|---|
| Prolonged soaking (hours or overnight) | Can promote surface crazing on repeated exposure |
| Hot or boiling water | Heat causes opal to fracture; sudden temperature changes are especially damaging |
| Salt water | More chemically aggressive than plain water; can etch or dull the surface |
| Saltwater soaking for "energetic cleansing" | Combines the risks of extended soaking and salt damage |
| Chlorinated pool or spa water | Chemicals can attack the opal surface over time |
| Chemical cleaners (bleach, ammonia) | Opal is attacked by caustic alkaline solutions |

Salt is a commonly suggested crystal care tool, but it is not safe for Green Opal. For a broader guide on which stones are salt-safe, see [salt-safe crystals](/learn/salt-safe-crystals).

## Does Green Opal Absorb Water Over Time?

Green Opal already contains water in its structure. What changes with soaking is not that the stone gains beneficial hydration but that water enters surface cracks and can alter the structure upon drying. Some very porous opal specimens may temporarily appear more vivid when wet (a phenomenon called water opal) and then caze as they dry. If a Green Opal specimen looks dramatically different wet versus dry, treat it as higher-risk for water contact.

## Desiccation: The Opposite Problem

Opal's water content also means it can suffer from being too dry. Long-term storage in very low humidity — near heat vents, in unventilated display cases in centrally heated homes — can cause some opal specimens to lose internal water and develop surface cracks over months or years. Store Green Opal away from intense heat sources and direct sunlight for extended periods. Brief morning sun for energetic practice carries minimal risk; hours of direct sun does not.

For sun exposure guidance, see [sun-safe crystals](/learn/sun-safe-crystals).

## Summary

Green Opal is not a water-safe stone for extended soaking, but it is not so fragile that a short rinse causes harm. The practical rule: brief, cool water contact followed by immediate drying is acceptable for physical cleaning. Salt water, hot water, and prolonged soaking are all risks that compound with each exposure.
