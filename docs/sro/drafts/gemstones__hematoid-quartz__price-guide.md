# Hematoid Quartz Price Guide

Hematoid Quartz is an affordable stone. Tumbled and small rough pieces retail for roughly $3–15 per piece; polished points and towers range from $10–60 depending on size and quality; large display specimens and spheres run $50–150+; exceptional phantom or harlequin specimens from collector dealers can reach $200–300. Prices checked at US retail in September 2026.

## What Hematoid Quartz Typically Costs

The table below reflects retail prices found across multiple US crystal sellers in September 2026. Prices vary by seller, origin, and individual piece quality.

| Form | Typical size | Retail price range |
|---|---|---|
| Small rough piece (1–2 in.) | 25–50 g | $2–8 per piece |
| Tumbled / polished slab | 1.25–2.5 in. | $5–15 per piece |
| Small tower / generator | 3–5 in. | $10–25 |
| Medium freeform / point | 3–5 in., 0.5–1 lb | $18–60 |
| Large display freeform | 4–6 in., 1+ lb | $50–150 |
| Sphere (3+ in.) | 3–4 in. diameter | $100–200 |
| Collector phantom or harlequin specimen | Varies | $150–300 |

These are retail consumer prices. Wholesale prices for bulk orders run significantly lower.

## What Drives the Price

**Quality of inclusions.** The most important factor for Hematoid Quartz is the character of the hematite inclusions. Bold, distinct red phantom zones or harlequin spots on a clear quartz host command higher prices than diffuse, brownish staining throughout an opaque piece. Vivid red-orange coloration in a transparent host is the premium end of the market.

**Clarity of the quartz host.** Transparent or translucent quartz hosting well-defined inclusions is worth more than muddy, opaque, or heavily fractured material.

**Form and finish.** Polished points, towers, and freeforms cost more than raw rough material of equivalent size, reflecting the labor in shaping and polishing. Natural crystal points (terminated without cutting) that display both the quartz crystal form and the inclusions cleanly are valued for their naturalness.

**Size.** Like most minerals, larger pieces cost more. However, size alone without inclusion quality does not push the price into collector territory.

**Origin.** Specimens from Brazil (Minas Gerais) and Madagascar are the main commercial sources. Madagascar material is the most commonly available at retail. Brazilian material, particularly from specific zones known for vivid inclusions, can command a modest premium with established dealers.

**Treatment.** Some sellers heat low-quality material to intensify the red color. Heat-treated pieces typically show uniformly red zones without distinct hematite inclusions. Natural, untreated specimens show organic variation. Treatment is not always disclosed; see [how to identify Hematoid Quartz](/gemstones/hematoid-quartz/how-to-identify) to recognize natural versus treated material.

## How to Avoid Overpaying

**Compare by piece, not by label.** The name "fire quartz" or "harlequin quartz" is a trade term, not a separate mineral. The same physical material may be sold at different prices under different names. Evaluate the piece itself — inclusion clarity, quartz transparency, size, finish — against the price.

**Verify the hardness.** At Mohs 7, genuine quartz scratches glass. Glass imitations (Mohs ~5.5) do not. A hardness test on an inconspicuous edge costs nothing and catches the most common substitution. For more on the Mohs scale, see [Mohs hardness scale](/learn/mohs-hardness-scale).

**Buy from sellers who disclose origin and treatment.** Reputable crystal sellers state whether material has been heat-treated and where it came from. Absence of this information is not a reason to refuse a purchase, but presence of it is a quality signal. For guidance on ethical sourcing, see [ethical crystal sourcing](/learn/ethical-crystal-sourcing).

**Very low retail prices are a flag.** Genuine Hematoid Quartz is inexpensive but not negligible. Tumbled pieces sold at $0.50–1 each in high-volume retail are worth inspecting under magnification to confirm they are correctly identified and not glass or dyed agate.

**Large price premiums for "rare" varieties need scrutiny.** Hematoid Quartz is not rare; it is commercially mined in multiple countries. Sellers claiming exceptional rarity to justify prices well above the ranges above should be asked to explain specifically what quality characteristic they are pricing.
