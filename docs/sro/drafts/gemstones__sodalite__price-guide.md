# Sodalite Price Guide — What to Pay and Why

Sodalite is one of the most affordable royal-blue ornamental stones available. Tumbled stones typically cost $2–10 each at retail; standard cabochons run $1–8 per carat; large polished palm stones and carvings sell for $10–60. The rare color-changing variety, hackmanite, can reach $50–200 per carat for documented specimens. Prices checked September 2026; they vary by quality grade, origin, and seller.

## Typical Retail Price Ranges (September 2026)

| Form | Typical retail range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Tumbled stones (small, 1–3 cm) | $2–8 each | Grade and color depth vary; B-grade common in wholesale lots |
| Tumbled stones (large, 4–6 cm) | $5–15 each | Deeper color and fewer calcite patches command more |
| Cabochons | $1–8 per carat | Standard range; intense color, good polish at the higher end |
| Loose faceted stones | $2–20 per carat | Uncommon; sodalite is usually cut as cabochon due to opacity |
| Palm stones / worry stones | $10–30 each | Size and color uniformity drive pricing |
| Carvings and spheres | $15–60+ | Artisan labor adds to cost; size matters |
| Raw rough specimens | $5–20 per kg (wholesale) | Collector-quality rough with good color: more |
| Hackmanite (color-changing variety) | $50–200 per carat | Rare; fluorescence intensity and documented tenebrescence affect price |
| Jewelry (set in silver) | $15–100+ per piece | Setting metal, design complexity, and brand all add cost |

Sodalite is categorized as a budget to mid-range ornamental stone. It is typically priced well below lapis lazuli of comparable appearance — lapis at similar quality grades commonly sells for $10–150 per carat, roughly ten to twenty times sodalite's baseline price. If a seller prices sodalite at lapis-level rates, request verification of mineral identity. See [how to identify sodalite](/gemstones/sodalite/how-to-identify) for the physical tests that distinguish the two.

## What Drives the Price

**Color depth and uniformity.** Deep, saturated royal blue with minimal calcite veining commands the highest prices. Stones with more white or grey calcite content are less valuable, though the white veining is natural and characteristic; heavy veining indicates a lower grade, not a fake.

**Cut and finish.** Well-domed cabochons with a clean, even polish sell at a premium over roughly finished or asymmetric cuts. Tumbled stones with dull or pitted surfaces are discount grade.

**Origin.** Canadian sodalite — particularly material from the Bancroft, Ontario region or with documented Princess Blue provenance — is marketed at a premium over commercial Brazilian or Namibian sodalite because of its historical association and consistent color. In practice, color and finish are more meaningful quality indicators than origin alone for most buyers.

**Size.** Larger stones cost more in absolute terms; price per carat may decrease slightly at very large sizes in wholesale contexts but this effect is less pronounced than in premium gems.

**Treatment status.** Untreated natural sodalite is the standard; dyed sodalite exists and should be disclosed and priced lower than natural-color material. A seller should be able to confirm whether color has been enhanced. For ethical sourcing context, see [ethical crystal sourcing](/learn/ethical-crystal-sourcing).

**Hardness and durability context.** At Mohs 5.5–6, sodalite is durable enough for pendants, earrings, and beads but softer than quartz. It chips and scratches in rough wear, which affects long-term value for jewelry pieces. For context on how hardness affects gem value and care, see [Mohs hardness scale](/learn/mohs-hardness-scale).

## How to Avoid Overpaying

**Know the baseline price.** If a retailer is charging more than $15 per carat for standard blue sodalite without an exceptional reason (hackmanite status, exceptional provenance, very large size, high-quality artisan cutting), the pricing is above market.

**Check for dye.** Heavily saturated, uniformly blue sodalite with no calcite variation may be dyed. Request disclosure from the seller and, if uncertain, perform an acetone swab test on an inconspicuous surface. Natural-color material should not transfer blue onto the swab.

**Compare the weight.** Sodalite is a light stone (specific gravity 2.27–2.33). If a piece feels very heavy for its size, it may be lapis lazuli rather than sodalite — which could mean the seller is pricing sodalite but selling something more valuable, or the reverse.

**Ask about origin.** For any purchase over $50, origin documentation adds credibility and some resale value.

**Buy from sellers who label clearly.** A reputable seller distinguishes sodalite from lapis lazuli and from hackmanite. Interchangeable labeling, vague descriptions, or refusal to identify the exact mineral are warning signs.
