# How to Identify Real Amazonite

Real Amazonite is a blue-green to greenish-blue variety of microcline feldspar. Its combination of color, a distinctive cross-hatch pattern, two-directional cleavage, a hardness of 6 to 6.5, and pearly luster on flat faces makes it identifiable to an experienced eye — and separable from its most common imitators (jade, turquoise, aventurine, and dyed green glass) through a few simple checks.

## What Genuine Amazonite Looks Like

Amazonite's color ranges from pale pastel blue-green to vivid turquoise-green, caused by trace lead (and in some specimens, iron) in the crystal structure. The most prized color is a strong, even blue-green with minimal white streaking, but the majority of amazonite on the market shows some variation: white veining, patches of cream or gray, or a gridiron (cross-hatch) pattern caused by two intergrown feldspar phases.

Key visual features of genuine Amazonite:

- **Color**: Blue-green to green; rarely pure blue or pure green. Color is typically muted rather than fluorescent or electric.
- **Pattern**: Many specimens show a visible cross-hatch or grid-like pattern at the surface — parallel lines intersecting at close to right angles. This is characteristic of the feldspar intergrowth and difficult to replicate convincingly in dyed glass or resin.
- **White inclusions**: Streaks, veins, or patches of white feldspar or quartz running through the body are common and normal. Absolutely uniform, inclusion-free material may be dyed.
- **Translucency**: Most amazonite is translucent to opaque. Very rarely is it semitransparent. If a stone claiming to be amazonite is clearly transparent, it is not amazonite.

For more on the stone's identity and traditional meaning, see the [Amazonite meaning page](/gemstones/amazonite/meaning).

## Hands-On Tests

### Hardness Test

Amazonite has a Mohs hardness of 6 to 6.5. It cannot be scratched by a steel knife or a copper coin (Mohs 3–3.5 and 5–6 respectively, depending on alloy), but it can be scratched by a piece of quartz (Mohs 7). If a stone that looks like amazonite is easily scratched by a coin, it may be a soft simulant such as chrysocolla or low-grade dyed glass. Test on an inconspicuous spot.

### Cleavage Inspection

Amazonite has two directions of perfect cleavage that intersect at close to a right angle. On a tumbled or polished piece, look for flat, slightly reflective areas that catch light differently from the rest of the surface — these are cleavage faces. On rough material, the cleavage planes are usually very obvious. The pearly luster on cleavage faces is diagnostic. Most glass simulants lack cleavage and show a smooth, uniform luster across all surfaces.

### Temperature

Real minerals generally feel cold to the touch and warm slowly. Glass also starts cool but warms rapidly in the hand. Dyed resin or plastic warms almost immediately. Hold the stone for five seconds — if it is slow to warm, this is consistent with genuine mineral material (though not conclusive alone).

### Specific Gravity

Amazonite has a specific gravity of about 2.6 to 2.8. This is within the feldspar range and is significantly lower than jade (jadeite: 3.2–3.4; nephrite: 2.9–3.1). If you have access to a basic water-displacement measurement, an unusually heavy stone for its size is probably not amazonite.

## Common Simulants and How to Tell Them Apart

| Stone | Color overlap | How to distinguish |
|---|---|---|
| Jade (jadeite or nephrite) | Blue-green to green | Jade is harder (6.5–7), denser, lacks cleavage in the same way; jadeite has a glassy luster without the gridiron pattern |
| Turquoise | Blue-green | Turquoise is softer (5–6), more porous, has a waxy luster and no cleavage planes; often shows brown matrix |
| Aventurine (green) | Green, sometimes blue-green | Aventurine quartz has a glittery sparkle (aventurescence) from mica flakes; lacks amazonite's gridiron pattern |
| Dyed green glass | Blue-green | Glass is amorphous, lacks cleavage, is uniformly transparent, and may show bubbles under magnification |
| Chalcedony or dyed agate | Blue-green | Harder than amazonite (7), waxy to silky luster, lacks cleavage, often shows banding |
| Chrysocolla | Blue-green | Much softer (2–4), often copper-associated, matte surface, no cleavage |

## Is There Lab-Grown Amazonite?

No. As of current trade knowledge, there is no synthetic or lab-grown amazonite on the market. The concern with amazonite is mislabeling — being sold as a pricier stone — rather than synthetic substitution. Some vendors have historically used the name "Amazon jade," which is a trade misnomer and does not indicate a synthetic; it simply attempts to suggest the stone is jade. For a broader discussion of lab-created vs natural gemstones, see [lab-created vs natural gemstones](/compare/lab-created-vs-natural-gemstones).

## Treatments to Watch For

Amazonite occasionally receives treatments that should be disclosed:

- **Dyeing**: Pale or unevenly colored material is sometimes dyed to intensify or even out the blue-green color. Dye may be visible at surface cracks or grain boundaries under magnification. Unusually vivid, perfectly even color with no white streaking warrants scrutiny.
- **Waxing or oiling**: Common on many opaque stones; improves surface appearance. This is a minor treatment widely accepted in the trade.
- **Resin impregnation**: Lower-grade rough with fractures is occasionally stabilized with clear resin before cutting. The stone may feel slightly warmer to the touch and will not show the characteristic cold response.
