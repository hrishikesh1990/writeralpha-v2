# Amazonite Price Guide — How Much It Costs and Why

Amazonite is an affordable colored gemstone. Retail prices range from under $5 per carat for low-saturation or heavily streaked material to around $15 to $30 per carat for vivid, well-cut, high-quality stones — based on retail surveys from early 2024. Tumbled stones and beads sell for considerably less per piece. Price is driven primarily by color intensity, followed by clarity, cut quality, and size.

## Price Ranges

The table below summarizes approximate retail price ranges observed in 2024. Amazonite does not have a standardized grading system equivalent to the GIA system for diamonds or colored gemstones, so prices vary between sellers and by market channel.

| Grade | Price per carat (approx.) | Typical form |
|---|---|---|
| Low quality — pale, heavily streaked, or uneven color | $1 – $5 | Tumbled rough, ungraded beads |
| Average quality — reasonable color, visible white inclusions | $5 – $15 | Tumbled stones, standard beads, simple cabochons |
| High quality — vivid, even blue-green, few inclusions | $15 – $30 | Faceted or precisely cut cabochons, collector gems |

*Source: retail survey, January 2024. Prices checked September 2026 may differ. Always compare multiple reputable sellers.*

For small tumbled stones sold singly (typically 5–15 carats), you can expect to pay anywhere from $1 to $8 per stone depending on quality and seller. Beaded bracelets made from standard-grade amazonite commonly retail for $10 to $25. Collector-quality mineral specimens from Colorado's Pikes Peak region — particularly those featuring large vivid crystals alongside smoky quartz — can sell for several hundred dollars or more.

## What Drives the Price of Amazonite

### Color

Color is the dominant value factor. The most desirable amazonite shows a strong, even, saturated blue-green with minimal white or cream streaking. Color should be consistent throughout the stone rather than zoned or patchy. Pale, pastel, or heavily veined material is abundant and carries the lowest prices; vivid, clean, uniformly colored stones are rarer and command a premium.

### Clarity and Inclusions

Unlike transparent gemstones where inclusions affect light transmission, amazonite is translucent to opaque, so clarity is assessed by how much the white feldspar matrix or other inclusions interrupt the color. A small amount of white patterning is natural and expected — the characteristic gridiron pattern is not a defect. Large patches of white or cream material that break up the color are priced lower.

### Cut Quality

A well-executed cabochon or bead that shows the stone's color to best advantage is worth more than a roughly shaped piece. Amazonite's cleavage (two perfect directions at close to 90 degrees) makes precision cutting challenging; a cutter who avoids cleavage-related fractures and centers the best color produces more valuable material.

### Size

Larger stones of consistent color are rarer and more expensive per carat, both because maintaining color evenness across a larger surface is harder and because fewer pieces of that size reach the lapidary market.

### Origin

Colorado material — especially from the Pikes Peak batholith — is among the most sought-after and tends to command a premium over material from other localities. Russian (Ilmen Mountains), Brazilian, and Malagasy material is also well-regarded. Origin is not always verified by sellers in the lower price tiers.

### Form

Loose gemstones (faceted or cabochon) are priced per carat. Tumbled stones, beads, and raw rough sell by the piece or by weight in bulk lots. Jewelry settings add labor costs. Collector-quality matrix specimens are priced by the piece based on size, locality, and aesthetic qualities rather than per carat.

## How to Avoid Overpaying

**Know what you are buying.** Amazonite is not an expensive stone; if a seller prices it alongside sapphire or tourmaline at equivalent per-carat rates, be skeptical. Vivid high-quality amazonite at $30 per carat is at the top of the market. Prices above that range warrant asking why — is the seller offering a known Colorado locality, a particularly large clean stone, or professional certification?

**Check for treatments.** Unusually saturated, perfectly even color with no white markings may indicate dyeing. Dyed material is not inherently wrong to buy, but it should be disclosed and priced accordingly. See [how to identify Amazonite](/gemstones/amazonite/how-to-identify) for more on spotting treatments.

**Compare channels.** Crystal retail stores, lapidary shows, and online marketplaces vary considerably. Rough material from a mineral show is almost always cheaper per gram than finished tumbled stones from a crystal boutique. For guidance on ethical sourcing considerations, see [ethical crystal sourcing](/learn/ethical-crystal-sourcing).

**Durability affects long-term value.** Amazonite's Mohs hardness of 6 to 6.5 and its two perfect cleavage directions mean it is more prone to scratching and chipping than harder stones. Paying more for an amazonite set in a ring for daily wear may not be a good investment if the stone chips within a year.
