# How to Identify Real White Quartz

Real White Quartz (milky quartz) is silicon dioxide (SiO2) with a Mohs hardness of 7, vitreous lustre, conchoidal fracture, and no cleavage. Its white opacity comes from microscopic fluid inclusions distributed through the crystal. The most reliable at-home tests are hardness and lustre — glass scratches at Mohs 5.5 and plastic scratches at Mohs 2, making both identifiable against genuine quartz.

## Physical Properties That Define Real White Quartz

| Property | White Quartz |
|---|---|
| Formula | SiO2 |
| Mohs hardness | 7 |
| Specific gravity | 2.65–2.66 |
| Lustre | Vitreous (glass-like) |
| Fracture | Conchoidal |
| Cleavage | None |
| Crystal system | Trigonal |
| Colour cause | Light scattering by microscopic fluid inclusions |
| Transparency | Translucent to opaque |

The white colour is natural: it results from light scattering by trapped microscopic fluid inclusions, not from dye, paint, or coating. Genuine milky quartz has this colouration throughout the body of the stone.

## Common Imitations and How to Spot Them

### Glass

White or frosted glass is the most common imitation. Key differences:

**Hardness.** Glass has a Mohs hardness of approximately 5 to 5.5. White Quartz at Mohs 7 will scratch glass, but glass will not scratch White Quartz. Drag a corner of the suspected piece firmly across a piece of known glass — if it leaves a clear scratch, the stone is at least as hard as Mohs 5.5 and likely harder. Clean the mark first: sometimes the softer material leaves a powdery residue on the harder surface that looks like a scratch but wipes away.

**Air bubbles.** Glass often contains spherical air bubbles visible under a magnifying glass. Natural quartz inclusions are irregular in shape, not spherical.

**Temperature.** Quartz stays cool in the hand for longer than glass because of its different thermal properties. The difference is noticeable on first contact but is a preliminary indicator only, not a definitive test.

### Plastic

Plastic imitations are lighter than quartz, warm up immediately on contact (quartz stays cool), and scratch very easily — a fingernail (Mohs approximately 2.5) will scratch most plastics. A hardness test eliminates plastic quickly.

### Dyed or coated material

Natural milky quartz has no dye. If a stone marketed as White Quartz shows colour concentrated around cracks, surface pits, or edges — or if any colour rubs off on a cloth — it has been surface-treated or dyed. Genuine milky quartz has uniform white colouration through the body of the stone.

### Calcite

White calcite is sometimes confused with milky quartz in tumbled or polished form. Calcite has a Mohs hardness of 3 and perfect rhombohedral cleavage — it breaks into rhombus-shaped fragments rather than the conchoidal fracture of quartz. A copper coin (Mohs approximately 3) will scratch calcite easily; it will not scratch White Quartz. Calcite also reacts with dilute acid (fizzes); quartz does not.

### Selenite and satin spar

White selenite or satin spar (gypsum) is sometimes confused with white quartz in raw or fibrous form. Selenite is Mohs 2 and can be scratched with a fingernail. It has a silky rather than vitreous lustre, and fibrous varieties display a visible parallel fiber structure.

## At-Home Identification Steps

1. **Visual inspection.** Look for vitreous (bright, glass-like) lustre. Milky quartz has a milky, slightly translucent quality in thin areas and natural irregular inclusions — not spherical bubbles.
2. **Hardness test.** Try to scratch a piece of window glass with a corner of the stone. If it leaves a clear scratch with minimal pressure, hardness is at least 6 and likely 7. This is the most diagnostic single test.
3. **Weight check.** Hold the stone. Quartz at specific gravity 2.65 feels moderately heavy and solid. Plastic feels noticeably light for its size; glass can feel similar to quartz.
4. **Temperature on contact.** Natural quartz feels distinctly cool for several seconds when first picked up. Plastic warms up nearly immediately.
5. **Examine any broken surfaces.** Quartz shows smooth, curved conchoidal fracture (like chipped glass, with a shell-like surface). Calcite breaks in flat planes.

## Practical Buying Notes

Price can be an indirect signal: White Quartz is one of the most abundant and affordable minerals available. Extremely low prices on pieces that look unusually perfect or uniform may indicate glass or plastic.
