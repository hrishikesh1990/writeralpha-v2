# How to Identify Real Sunstone — Fakes, Glass, and Treated Stones

Two imitations dominate the Sunstone market: goldstone (a manufactured glass) and copper-diffused andesine (a feldspar treated to mimic Oregon Sunstone's copper color). Both are identifiable under a 10x loupe with practice. A handful of physical tests — hardness, inclusions, color distribution — separate genuine material from either imitation without laboratory equipment.

## Know What Genuine Sunstone Looks Like

Sunstone is a feldspar mineral. Depending on the variety, the base chemistry is either potassium aluminosilicate (orthoclase) or a sodium-calcium aluminosilicate solid solution (oligoclase or labradorite). GIA records Mohs hardness at 6.0 to 7.2, specific gravity between 2.58 and 2.70 by variety, and refractive index from 1.518 to 1.568.

Genuine Sunstone's defining optical property is aventurescence: flat, reflective platelets of hematite, goethite, or copper aligned along crystallographic planes inside the stone produce a metallic glitter when the stone is tilted in light. Oregon Sunstone, a labradorite variety with native copper inclusions, is the most prized form and comes in colorless, champagne, pale yellow, orange, pink, red, green, and blue-green — with color caused by copper concentration and platelet size, not surface treatment.

Under magnification, genuine Oregon Sunstone shows copper platelets as irregular hexagonal or triangular metallic inclusions scattered through the stone, varying in size and orientation. Bicolor and tricolor stones show color zoning through the body of the stone, not concentrated at the surface.

For full physical properties, see the [Sunstone hub page](/gemstones/sunstone). For what Sunstone typically costs — a useful calibration signal — see the [price guide](/gemstones/sunstone/price-guide).

## Identifying Goldstone (Manufactured Glass)

Goldstone is a copper-flecked glass invented in 17th-century Venice. It is sometimes sold under the name "Sunstone" or "goldstone sunstone" to uninformed buyers. Under a 10x loupe, goldstone is unmistakable:

- The copper flakes are perfectly ordered in a dense, regular grid, all the same size and all the same hexagonal shape, with no variation in orientation or clustering.
- Tiny gas bubbles are visible within the glass matrix — a hallmark of manufactured glass not found in crystalline minerals.
- The substrate is glassy rather than crystalline; it will feel slightly warmer in the hand than a cool mineral and will look uniformly homogenous under magnification.

Goldstone has a specific gravity around 2.5, slightly lower than feldspar (2.58–2.70). A hardness test using a steel knife (hardness ~5.5) will scratch glass easily, whereas a genuine Sunstone at Mohs 6+ will resist scratching by steel. Test on an inconspicuous edge, not a polished face. Goldstone is not toxic and is a legitimate decorative material in its own right — the problem is misrepresentation as a natural mineral.

## Identifying Copper-Diffused Andesine

The more sophisticated imitation is copper-diffused andesine or labradorite — pale feldspar subjected to high-temperature copper diffusion treatment to produce a red or red-green color on and near the surface. This material entered the market around 2008 and has been the subject of multiple GIA gemological investigations.

Diagnostics without laboratory equipment:

- **Color concentrated at the rim**: In diffusion-treated stones, color is densest near the surface and fades toward the center. In natural Oregon Sunstone, color is typically richer in areas with the densest copper inclusion, concentrated toward the interior rather than the rim.
- **Absence of oriented copper platelets**: Under magnification, diffusion-treated stones do not show the distinct hexagonal copper platelets of natural Oregon Sunstone. The copper is diffused atomically rather than precipitated as visible platelets, so the schiller effect is often absent or weaker.
- **Price anomaly**: Fine red or red-green Oregon Sunstone commands $450 to $1,700 per carat at retail (September 2026). Material offered as "red andesine from Tibet" or "Tibetan sunstone" at significantly lower prices should be assumed treated until proven otherwise.

Laboratory confirmation requires UV fluorescence testing: copper-diffused plagioclase shows strong fluorescence under 320 nm UV excitation (due to residual Cu+ ions in the lattice), whereas natural Oregon Sunstone does not show this characteristic. This test is available from GIA and other accredited gemological laboratories.

## Practical At-Home Tests

| Test | Genuine Sunstone | Goldstone | Treated Andesine |
|---|---|---|---|
| Loupe inclusions | Irregular copper platelets, varied size | Regular grid, same-size flakes, gas bubbles | Absent or surface-only |
| Color distribution | Through the body | Uniform (glass) | Concentrated at rim |
| Steel knife scratch | Resists (Mohs 6+) | Scratched (glass ~5.5) | Resists (feldspar) |
| Temperature | Cool, slow to warm | Slightly warmer (glass) | Cool |
| Schiller | Directional flash in light | Omnidirectional glitter | Weak or absent |

## Buying Safely

For Oregon Sunstone specifically, material with documented provenance from named Oregon mines (Ponderosa, Spectrum, Dust Devil) sold by dealers who disclose treatment status is the safest purchase. The site's guide to [evaluating online crystal purchases](/learn/are-online-crystals-real) covers the broader buying context. For the distinction between natural and lab-grown material (a separate question from fake vs. real), see [lab-created vs. natural gemstones](/compare/lab-created-vs-natural-gemstones).
