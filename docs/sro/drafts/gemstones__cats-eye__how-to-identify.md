# How to Identify Real Cat's Eye Gemstone

Real Cat's Eye chrysoberyl has a sharp, well-defined chatoyant line that moves cleanly across the dome as the stone tilts, a Mohs hardness of 8.5 (harder than a steel file), and a noticeably cool, dense feel. Glass and most imitations are softer, lighter, and show a broader or stationary eye. For certain identification, a gemological laboratory test is the only reliable method, but several field checks narrow the field considerably.

## Understanding What You Are Looking For

"Cat's eye" in gem trade usage always refers to chrysoberyl (cymophane, BeAl2O4) unless another mineral name is attached. If a seller describes a stone simply as "cat's eye" without qualification, it should be chrysoberyl. Materials that are sometimes sold or mislabeled as cat's eye include:

- **Cat's eye glass (fiber-optic glass):** The most common imitation. It is synthetic, cheap, and widely available. The eye is very regular and often multi-colored.
- **Cat's eye quartz:** A genuine mineral (Mohs 7) but far less valuable than chrysoberyl. Must be sold as "cat's eye quartz" or "quartz cat's eye."
- **Cat's eye tourmaline, apatite, or scapolite:** Genuine but different mineral species, each with different hardness and value. All must be qualified by mineral name.
- **Synthetic chrysoberyl:** Lab-created with the same chemistry and properties as natural; distinguished only by laboratory testing.
- **Composite or assembled stones:** A chatoyant layer bonded onto a glass or lesser mineral base.

## Visual Checks

**Eye sharpness and behavior.** Hold the stone under a single-point light source (a penlight or a bare LED bulb) and tilt it. In genuine chrysoberyl with strong chatoyancy, the cat's eye line should be narrow, bright, and move cleanly across the dome as you rotate the stone. In fiber-optic glass, the eye is often very narrow and perfectly consistent, sometimes showing multiple parallel bands or a rainbow effect — these are signs of glass. In genuine stones, the line may be slightly less perfect but has a characteristic depth and movement that glass does not replicate.

**The "milk and honey" effect.** When light hits from above, one half of the dome of a fine chrysoberyl cat's eye appears rich and golden ("honey") while the other half appears pale and milky. Rotate the stone 180 degrees and the two halves switch. This is specific to chrysoberyl with good natural chatoyancy; glass typically shows a uniform appearance.

**Color.** Genuine chrysoberyl cat's eye ranges from honey-golden yellow through greenish-yellow to brownish-green. Bright blue, red, or vivid multi-colored "cat's eye" stones sold at low prices are almost certainly glass or composite material.

**Transparency and inclusions.** Cat's eye chrysoberyl is translucent to opaque due to its fine parallel inclusions. A stone that appears perfectly transparent with a chatoyant effect is unusual; under magnification, genuine stones typically show fine parallel needle-like inclusions responsible for the eye.

## Physical Tests

**Hardness test.** Chrysoberyl at Mohs 8.5 cannot be scratched by a standard steel file (approximately Mohs 6.5) or a piece of quartz (Mohs 7). If you can scratch the stone's surface with a steel file, it is not chrysoberyl — it may be glass (Mohs 5–6), quartz cat's eye (Mohs 7), or another softer material. Test on an inconspicuous area and clean away any residue before reading the result; what looks like a scratch is sometimes just powdered residue from the file. Note: hardness tests leave marks; use only if needed and acceptable.

**Temperature.** Natural gemstones feel cool to the touch and warm slowly in the hand. Glass warms quickly. Hold the stone against your inner wrist: genuine chrysoberyl will feel noticeably cool and take several seconds to warm. This is a rough screening test, not definitive.

**Weight.** Chrysoberyl has a specific gravity of 3.70 to 3.78 — denser than most glass (about 2.5) and much denser than quartz (2.65). A genuine cat's eye stone of a given size will feel noticeably heavier than a glass stone of the same size. This is easiest to evaluate by comparing two similarly sized stones side by side.

## Gemological Tests

For definitive identification, a gemologist with a refractometer, specific gravity measurement, and microscopy can distinguish chrysoberyl from all common imitations. The refractive index of chrysoberyl (1.746–1.763) is distinctive and measurable. Spectroscopic analysis (including Raman spectroscopy, as described in Rybnikova et al. 2023) can identify the rutile inclusions responsible for chatoyancy in genuine stones and distinguish natural from synthetic chrysoberyl.

For high-value purchases (above $500 total), a report from GIA, Gübelin Lab, SSEF, or AGL is the only way to confirm natural origin, species, and treatment status.

## Buying Safely Online

When buying Cat's Eye online, apply these checks:
- Ask the seller to state the mineral species (should be "chrysoberyl" or "cymophane").
- Request multiple photos or video under a single-point light source to evaluate the eye's behavior.
- For significant purchases, buy only from sellers who offer laboratory certification or accept returns for independent appraisal.
