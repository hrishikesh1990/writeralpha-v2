# How to Identify Real Obsidian — Tests and Common Fakes

Real obsidian is a volcanic glass with a Mohs hardness of 5–5.5, conchoidal fracture that produces smooth curved breaks with razor-sharp edges, and natural flow patterns visible in the body of the stone. The most common substitutes are industrial slag glass, man-made coloured glass, and resin. Here is how to tell them apart without specialist equipment.

## What Makes Obsidian Distinctive

Obsidian forms when silica-rich lava cools so fast that crystals cannot grow. The result is a natural glass with:

- **Conchoidal fracture:** When obsidian breaks, it produces smooth, curved shell-like surfaces — the same pattern as flint. The edges are extremely sharp. If a stone is sold as obsidian and has flat, right-angle breaks, it is more likely man-made glass.
- **Vitreous luster:** Obsidian has a glassy, reflective surface. This is expected and not a distinguishing feature on its own, since all glass shares it.
- **Natural flow patterns:** Most obsidian shows subtle layering or banding formed during the lava flow. These appear as faint parallel lines or swirling internal patterns, visible when held up to light. Man-made glass is more uniform.
- **Specific gravity:** Obsidian's density ranges from about 2.35 to 2.60 g/cm³. This makes it noticeably heavier than resin imitations of the same size.
- **Hardness:** At 5–5.5 Mohs, obsidian can be scratched by a knife blade or a piece of quartz. It will scratch glass with a Mohs value below 5.5.

## Step-by-Step Tests

### 1. Look for natural flow patterns

Hold the piece up to a light source and look into the body of the stone. Real obsidian almost always shows subtle layering or flow banding. Uniform, completely structureless glass is more likely to be man-made.

### 2. Examine any fracture edges

If the piece has any broken edges or chips, look at the fracture surface. Real obsidian shows the characteristic smooth, concave, shell-like surface (conchoidal fracture). Flat or jagged breaks suggest man-made glass.

### 3. Check for bubbles

Hold the stone up to strong backlighting and look for small round bubbles inside. Industrial slag glass — a common obsidian substitute found in gardens and along old rail lines — typically has more and larger bubbles than natural obsidian. Some bubbles are normal in obsidian, but an abundance of evenly distributed spherical bubbles is a warning sign.

### 4. Hardness test

Scratch the surface with a steel knife blade (hardness approximately 5.5). Real obsidian at 5–5.5 Mohs is at the borderline: some pieces will scratch, some will not. Then try scratching the stone against a piece of glass (typically Mohs 5.5). If the stone scratches the glass, it is at least as hard as glass. Resin imitations are much softer (Mohs 2–3) and scratch very easily. Conduct the test on an inconspicuous spot and clean the surface first — what looks like a scratch may be powdered residue from the tool.

### 5. Check the weight

Lift the piece and compare it to a similar-sized piece of resin or plastic. Obsidian, at 2.35–2.60 g/cm³, feels noticeably heavy relative to resin. Resin imitations feel light. Man-made glass is closer in density but tends to be more uniform.

### 6. Surface temperature

Natural stone, including obsidian, tends to feel cool to the touch and warms slowly. Resin and plastic warm quickly. This test is helpful only for distinguishing stone from resin; it will not separate obsidian from other glass.

## Common Fakes and Imitations

| Material | How to spot it | Key difference |
|---|---|---|
| Industrial slag glass | More bubbles, often greenish or brownish tint, angular edges | Furnace origin, not volcanic; irregular colours |
| Dyed man-made glass | Very uniform colour, no flow patterns, may be too perfect | No natural variation |
| Resin / plastic | Much lighter weight, scratches easily (fingernail can mark it), warms quickly | Density and hardness far below obsidian |
| Jet (different stone) | Much lighter weight, carves easily, organic origin | Lower density (~1.3 g/cm³) |
| Black tourmaline | Columnar crystal structure, striations along the length | Crystal structure visible |

**A note on "black obsidian" vs. "obsidianite":** Some sellers use "obsidianite" or "volcanic glass" as alternate names for genuine obsidian. These are fine. Be more cautious with vague labels like "healing glass" or "energy stone" without a clear material description.
