# How to Identify Real Agate and Spot Fakes

Real agate is a banded chalcedony (SiO2) with a Mohs hardness of 6.5 to 7, a waxy to dull luster, a specific gravity of 2.60 to 2.64, and naturally occurring banding that varies in translucency. Common substitutes include dyed quartz or chalcedony, glass, plastic, and resin composites. Most fakes can be identified with simple physical tests — no lab equipment required.

## What Genuine Agate Looks Like

Agate's characteristic feature is its layered banding — concentric rings or parallel bands of alternating translucency and opacity, formed by successive silica deposits in a rock cavity. Natural banding has these qualities:

- **Irregular spacing.** Natural bands vary in width, and the spacing is rarely perfectly uniform. Perfectly even, machine-precise bands suggest a manufactured replica.
- **Gradual color transitions at the edges.** Where one band meets another, the color change is typically gradual at microscopic scale, even if it looks sharp to the naked eye.
- **Mixed translucency.** Some bands are slightly translucent (hold the stone to a light source and you may see light pass through the thinner or lighter layers), while others are opaque.
- **Waxy to dull luster** on an unpolished surface; polished surfaces show a bright waxy shine.

Natural agate colors include white, gray, brown, red, pale blue, pale green, and black. Very vivid electric blue, neon green, or hot pink agate is almost certainly dyed (see below).

## Physical Tests You Can Do Without Equipment

### Hardness test

Agate will scratch glass (hardness 5.5) but will not scratch a steel file (hardness 6.5 to 7). Use a piece of glass — such as the underside of a glass tile — and try to scratch it with the agate. Agate will mark the glass easily. Then try the reverse: the glass should leave no mark on agate, or only a faint one. Plastic and resin fakes are much softer and will not scratch glass, or will scratch it only weakly and may leave a plastic smear rather than a clean cut line.

Note: scratching destroys the test surface; do this on an inconspicuous area or a separate reference piece, not on a display piece.

### Temperature

Hold the stone briefly against your cheek or inner wrist. Genuine agate feels cold and takes noticeably longer to warm to body temperature than glass, which also feels cold. Plastic and resin feel warmer almost immediately. This is a rough indicator only — not a definitive test.

### Weight

Agate has a specific gravity of 2.60 to 2.64. Glass is similar (around 2.4 to 2.6), so weight alone does not distinguish them reliably. Plastic and resin are lighter: a piece that feels surprisingly light for its size is likely not mineral.

### Visual examination under magnification

Examine the stone with a loupe (10x) or a jeweler's magnifying glass:

- **Bubbles** inside the material indicate glass. Glass fakes almost always contain small spherical or elongated gas bubbles trapped during manufacture. Agate will not have spherical bubbles, though it may have irregular inclusions or tiny fractures.
- **Flow lines or swirls** in glass imitate banding but tend to look smoother and more uniform than natural chalcedony layers. Natural agate bands have subtle texture variation.
- **Mold seams or flat-plane edges** suggest cast plastic or resin.

## How to Spot Dyed Agate

Dyeing is legal and widespread — much commercial agate is dyed to produce colors rare in nature. Dyeing is not a fake: the stone is still real agate. But buyers should know what they are getting.

Signs of dyed agate:

- **Unnaturally vivid, saturated color.** Natural agate is typically pale to medium in saturation. Intense electric blue, cherry red, bright orange, or neon green points to dye.
- **Concentrated color at cracks and pores.** Because agate is porous and dye enters the stone along microfractures, you may see the color pooled more intensely in surface crevices or at the edges of the stone.
- **Color transfer.** Wipe a damp cloth across the surface. If the cloth picks up color, the dye is not fully fixed. Natural agate will not transfer color to a cloth.
- **Uniform color depth.** Natural color variations occur at the banding level; dye penetrates evenly across the microstructure and can produce a uniform depth of color that looks flat rather than layered.

Dyed agate is not physically harmful and its metaphysical use is a matter of personal practice. If you want natural color, ask suppliers to specify whether the piece is dyed. ## Common Agate Imitations

| Material | How to tell it apart |
|---|---|
| Glass | Bubbles under magnification; similar weight; may be warmer or cooler depending on composition |
| Plastic / resin | Lighter weight; warmer to touch; mold seams possible; cannot scratch glass |
| Dyed chalcedony | This is still real chalcedony/agate — look for dye signs above |
| Synthetic silica glass | Rare in low-cost retail; behaves like glass |
| Painted stone (other minerals) | Surface color only — a scratch reveals a different color beneath |

## When to Use a Lab

For high-value purchases — large slabs, collector specimens, or investment-grade material — professional gemological testing gives certainty. A gemologist can measure refractive index (1.530 to 1.540 for chalcedony), specific gravity, and spectral absorption to confirm identity. This is worth commissioning when the price warrants it. For everyday tumbled stones in the $2 to $20 range, the visual and physical checks above are proportionate.
