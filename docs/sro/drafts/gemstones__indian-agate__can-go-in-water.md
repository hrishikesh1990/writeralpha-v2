# Can Indian Agate Go in Water?

Yes, Indian Agate can go in water without damage. It is silicon dioxide (SiO2), the same mineral family as quartz, with a Mohs hardness of 6.5–7, no cleavage, and no chemical reactivity with water. Brief immersion or rinsing under a tap is safe for natural, undyed pieces. Extended soaks and hot water are unnecessary and may dull a polished finish over time.

## Why Indian Agate Is Water-Safe

Indian Agate is a variety of chalcedony — microcrystalline quartz — that forms in the gas cavities of volcanic basalt. Its chemical formula is SiO2 (silicon dioxide), the same composition as sand and most common quartz. SiO2 does not dissolve in water, does not react with water at normal temperatures, and does not leach harmful compounds onto skin when wet.

With a Mohs hardness of 6.5–7 and no cleavage plane, Indian Agate is not prone to splitting or cracking when wet. This distinguishes it from minerals that are water-sensitive for structural or chemical reasons — selenite (soft, dissolves slowly), malachite (copper-bearing, potentially reactive in acidic water), or pyrite (iron sulfide, oxidizes in water).

For a broader comparison of which crystals are and are not water-safe, see [what crystals can go in water](/learn/what-crystals-can-go-in-water).

## Practical Guidelines for Water Cleaning

**Rinsing under a tap:** Hold Indian Agate under cool or lukewarm running water for 20 to 60 seconds. This is sufficient for routine cleaning. Pat dry with a soft cloth immediately and allow it to air-dry fully before storage.

**Soaking:** Short soaks of a few minutes in clean water are fine. Prolonged soaking — hours to days — is unnecessary and may allow water to seep behind any adhesive in mounted settings or gradually dull a polished surface.

**Hot water:** Avoid very hot water or sudden temperature changes. Rapid thermal cycling can cause micro-fractures in polished stones over repeated exposure, though this requires significant and repeated temperature swings.

**Ultrasonic cleaners:** Generally safe for solid, unfractured pieces. Avoid if your specimen has visible fractures, inclusions, or was heavily treated.

## The Dye Caution

Some Indian Agate sold for craft and jewelry markets is dyed to produce bright or uniform colors. Intense, vivid, or unnaturally even hues are typical indicators of dyed material; natural Indian Agate shows earthy, muted tones of green, brown, cream, red, and yellow. Dyed pieces can bleed color into water, particularly in warm or prolonged soaks. If you are unsure whether your piece is dyed, use short cool-water rinses only and observe the water for color.

## Salt Water and Salt-Based Cleansing

Rinsing in plain water is always preferable to salt water for polished stones. Salt is mildly abrasive and, in long soaks, can work into surface micro-pores. Brief contact with salt water is not harmful to Indian Agate's chemistry, but it adds no benefit and may dull the polish. For guidance on which stones can and cannot tolerate salt, see [salt-safe crystals](/learn/salt-safe-crystals).

## Sun Drying and Charging After Washing

Natural Indian Agate in earthy tones can be set in morning sunlight to dry and charge after washing; brief exposure (15 to 30 minutes) poses no documented fading risk. Dyed pieces should be dried indoors or in indirect light to protect the dye. For guidance on sun exposure more broadly, see [sun-safe crystals](/learn/sun-safe-crystals).
