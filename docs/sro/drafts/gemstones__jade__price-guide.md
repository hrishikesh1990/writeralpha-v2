# Jade Price Guide: What Jade Costs and Why

Jade spans one of the widest price ranges of any gemstone: tumbled nephrite sells for a few dollars per piece, while top-grade imperial jadeite commands $5,000 to over $100,000 per carat, with exceptional auction lots exceeding $3 million per carat. The gap exists because "jade" names two entirely different minerals with very different rarity profiles, and because treatment dramatically affects value.

## Two Minerals, Two Price Tiers

The commercial name "jade" covers two distinct silicate minerals sold alongside one another in nearly every gem market.

**Jadeite** (NaAlSi2O6) is a sodium aluminum pyroxene with a Mohs hardness of 6.5 to 7 and a specific gravity of 3.2 to 3.4. It is rarer than nephrite and commands considerably higher prices, especially in vivid green. Myanmar (Burma) is the primary source of gem-quality jadeite.

**Nephrite** (Ca2(Mg,Fe)5Si8O22(OH)2) is a calcium magnesium amphibole with a Mohs hardness of 6 to 6.5 and a specific gravity of 2.9 to 3.1. Nephrite is tougher than jadeite despite its slightly lower hardness — its fibrous interlocking crystal structure resists fracture well. Major sources include British Columbia, New Zealand, Russia, and China (Hetian, Xinjiang).

When shopping, confirm which mineral you are buying. Many affordable pieces labeled "jade" are nephrite; most high-value pieces are jadeite. For help confirming which material you have, see [how to identify jade](/gemstones/jade/how-to-identify).

## Treatment Grades and How They Affect Price

The gem trade uses a three-tier system for jadeite treatment, though the terminology also appears informally for nephrite.

| Grade | Treatment | Price effect |
|---|---|---|
| Type A | No bleaching, no polymer filling; may have surface wax only | Full market value; investment grade |
| Type B | Acid-bleached to remove staining, then polymer-impregnated to restore apparent quality | 70–95% lower than equivalent Type A |
| Type C | Dyed (with or without Type B treatment) | Lowest value; color is not stable long-term |

Type B and Type C stones are not investment pieces and may degrade over years as polymer yellows or dye fades. A reputable seller will state the treatment status, and a gemological certificate from GIA, GGTL, or an accredited Hong Kong laboratory will confirm it.

## Price Ranges by Type and Quality (September 2026)

Prices below are retail indications gathered from trade sources in September 2026. Values vary by seller, origin certification, and individual stone quality; these ranges are a starting point, not an appraisal standard.

**Jadeite (Type A, per carat)**
- Entry-level commercial: $50–$300
- Fine quality, good color and translucency: $200–$5,000
- Imperial green (vivid emerald green, high translucency): $5,000–$100,000+
- Exceptional auction-grade imperial jade: has exceeded $3,000,000 per carat at major houses

**Nephrite (per piece or per carat)**
- Tumbled stones and small carvings: $2–$30 each
- Standard jewelry-grade: $20–$200 per finished piece
- High-end Hetian white ("mutton-fat") nephrite: $100–$500 per carat, with certified-origin material at the upper end

**Finished pieces (bangles, pendants)**
- Commercial nephrite bangle: $30–$200
- Mid-grade jadeite bangle: $500–$10,000
- Fine jadeite bangle (Type A, vivid color): $10,000 and above

## What Drives Jade's Price

**Color** is the dominant factor for jadeite. Imperial green — a vivid, even, emerald-like color caused by trace chromium — commands the highest premiums. Lavender, red, and black jadeite also carry value premiums over pale or mottled material. For nephrite, white and mid-green are traditionally most prized; Hetian white with an even tone commands the highest prices in the Chinese market.

**Translucency** matters enormously for jadeite. A piece described as "glassy" or "icy" (high translucency) is worth multiples of an opaque stone with identical color.

**Texture** refers to the size and interlocking pattern of the mineral's crystals. Fine-grained, even texture without visible grain boundaries is the standard for high-quality jadeite.

**Evenness** of color distribution: patchy or zoned color reduces value significantly.

**Size and craftsmanship** in finished pieces: a well-carved bangle with no visible cracks carries a premium over an equivalent rough piece.

**Origin** adds a documentary premium: certified Burmese jadeite and certified Hetian nephrite both command higher prices than undocumented material of similar apparent quality.

## How to Avoid Overpaying

Request a gemological certificate for any significant purchase. For jadeite over roughly $500, a certificate from GIA, GGTL, or the Gemmological Association of All Japan (GAAJ) is standard practice; it will confirm species (jadeite vs nephrite), color origin, and treatment status.

Ask the seller to state treatment status in writing. Reputable dealers do this routinely; reluctance to disclose is a warning sign.

Compare prices across sellers before committing. Because jade is not exchange-traded like diamonds, prices for comparable material can vary widely between sellers. The [jade identification page](/gemstones/jade/how-to-identify) covers the tests that help you confirm what you are buying before paying.

Be skeptical of very low prices on supposedly high-quality pieces. A "Type A imperial jade bangle" at $50 is almost certainly not what it claims.
