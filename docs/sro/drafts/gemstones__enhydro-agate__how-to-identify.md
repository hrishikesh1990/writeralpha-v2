# How to Identify Real Enhydro Agate

The most reliable test for genuine Enhydro Agate is movement: gently tip or shake the specimen and feel or listen for liquid sloshing inside the cavity. A true enhydro agate has a hollow interior partially filled with trapped water (or, rarely, petroleum), and this fluid moves freely within the cavity when the stone is rotated. No amount of visual similarity can substitute for this physical confirmation.

## The Core Tests

### 1. The shake and tilt test

Hold the specimen gently and tip it slowly from one side to another. In a genuine enhydro:

- You may hear a soft sloshing or gurgling as the fluid moves
- You may feel a subtle shift of weight toward the lower end as the liquid settles
- In translucent specimens, you may see a darker mass or a bubble moving through the interior

If the stone is fully opaque and walls are thick, you may only detect movement by careful listening in a quiet room. Large specimens with ample fluid produce an audible slosh; small pieces with little fluid may only produce a faint sound detectable by pressing the stone gently against the ear.

### 2. The light test

Hold the specimen up to a bright light source (a LED flashlight works well). In a translucent enhydro agate, the fluid-filled cavity may appear as a darker or more shadowed region that shifts when the stone is tilted. The surrounding chalcedony transmits some light, making the internal void visible. Fully opaque specimens will not reveal the interior this way, but most enhydro agates on the collector market are at least partially translucent.

### 3. Hardness check

Enhydro Agate has a Mohs hardness of 6.5 to 7. It will:

- Not be scratched by a steel file or a knife blade (which rate around 5.5 to 6)
- Scratch glass easily (glass rates about 5.5)
- Not scratch a piece of quartz crystal (Mohs 7)

Glass fakes will scratch much more easily than genuine chalcedony. Plastic fakes will scratch with a fingernail (Mohs 2.5). Do not perform the scratch test on the specimen itself, as it will leave a mark; instead, test a fragment if one is available or request documentation from a reputable dealer.

### 4. Temperature sensation

Genuine mineral stones feel noticeably cool to the touch initially, while glass warms more quickly with body heat and plastic warms almost immediately. This is a supporting indicator, not definitive on its own, but it helps distinguish obvious plastic fakes.

## What Fakes Look Like

Several types of material are sold — sometimes deliberately, sometimes due to mislabeling — as enhydro agate.

**Fluid inclusions mislabeled as enhydros.** The most common confusion in the trade. Fluid inclusions are microscopic pockets of liquid sealed within quartz crystals during crystal growth. They show a tiny bubble moving in a small enclosed chamber, not a larger cavity of free-moving water. True enhydro agates (as defined by the American Geological Institute) are chalcedony nodules containing water in a central hollow cavity. Fluid inclusions in quartz crystals are a different, more common phenomenon. Both are genuine natural phenomena; the labeling matters for accurate collection and pricing.

**Sealed glass or resin spheres with injected water.** These fakes have water sloshing inside, but the exterior is glass or resin (easily distinguished by the scratch test and by warmer temperature feel). The water inside a glass fake is also usually pure and clear; the water in a genuine enhydro may carry fine mineral particles that make it slightly turbid.

**Regular agate labeled as enhydro.** Solid agate with no internal cavity produces no movement on the shake test. If a piece sold as enhydro does not move, it is either solid (mislabeled) or has already lost its water through cracking.

**Dyed or treated agate.** Agate is commonly dyed to enhance or alter color. Dyed material is not inherently fake — dyed agate is still agate — but dyed pieces should not be sold as naturally colored. Under magnification, dyed agate often shows color concentrated in surface pores and along band edges rather than evenly throughout.

## Practical Buying Tips

A genuine enhydro agate should always demonstrate movement when the seller tilts it in front of you. Reputable dealers will do this openly and will know the specimen's country of origin (Brazil, Uruguay, and Madagascar are primary sources). Unusually bright artificial colors, a price far below the market range for enhydro material, and a seller who declines to demonstrate movement are all signals worth noting.
