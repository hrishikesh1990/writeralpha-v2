# How to Identify Real Ruby Zoisite

Genuine Ruby Zoisite contains three distinct minerals — green zoisite, red ruby (corundum), and black hornblende — all visible in a real specimen. The absence of any one component, particularly the black hornblende, is the first warning sign. Hardness testing, UV fluorescence, and color distribution confirm the rest.

## The Three-Mineral Test

Ruby Zoisite (anyolite) is a metamorphic rock, not a single mineral. Its characteristic appearance comes from the combination of:

- **Green zoisite matrix:** a calcium aluminum silicate with a dull to slightly vitreous, earthy green color (caused by vanadium)
- **Red to pink ruby inclusions:** chromium-bearing corundum appearing as distinct patches or crystals embedded in the matrix
- **Black hornblende (pargasite):** dark amphibole mineral visible as patches or veins throughout the green matrix

All three components should be visible. Specimens sold as Ruby Zoisite that show only green and red, with no black mineral present, may be ruby in fuchsite (a different rock with a bright mica matrix) or dyed material. The black hornblende is the authenticating third component that most imitations lack or simulate poorly.

## Hardness Testing

Ruby Zoisite's composite structure produces a material with variable hardness across its surface. The ruby areas are Mohs 9 — among the hardest common minerals — and can scratch glass, steel, and most other minerals with ease. The zoisite matrix is Mohs 6–7, also capable of scratching glass but with noticeably more effort.

Test the red areas specifically. If the red portions cannot scratch a steel blade or glass surface, the material is probably not genuine ruby. On a polished specimen, ruby zones often sit slightly raised or feel crisper underfoot because corundum takes a tighter polish than the surrounding softer zoisite.

If both the red and green areas feel equally soft and cannot scratch glass, the stone may be dyed quartzite, dyed fuchsite, or glass.

## UV Fluorescence

Ruby fluoresces red under longwave UV light (365 nm). Hold a UV flashlight over the specimen in a darkened space: genuine ruby inclusions will glow red. The green zoisite matrix does not fluoresce in the same way. UV testing is one of the fastest non-destructive checks available to a buyer or collector, and a small UV flashlight costs around $20–$30.

## Color and Pattern Distribution

In genuine Ruby Zoisite, the ruby occurs as irregular patches or crystal outlines embedded in the zoisite. The pattern is three-dimensional and shifts as the piece is rotated. Under a 10x loupe, the zoisite looks granular and rocky, not glassy or waxy.

Warning signs of dyeing or imitation:

- Color pooling in cracks and fractures, especially around the red areas
- Unnaturally uniform green without the slightly granular texture of real zoisite
- Red "spots" that bleed into the surrounding material or that look painted
- A uniformly glossy surface, like clearcoat, rather than the variable luster of different minerals in one piece

Resin and glass cast imitations feel lighter than genuine stone (genuine density approximately 3.35 g/cm³) and display too uniform a pattern to be a natural rock.

## Common Look-Alikes

| Stone | How to tell it apart |
|---|---|
| Ruby in fuchsite | Fuchsite is green mica at Mohs 2–3: the green areas scratch with a fingernail; the green is brighter and sparkly rather than earthy |
| Dragon blood jasper | Brick-red color rather than true ruby red; uniformly glassy texture throughout |
| Unakite | Salmon-pink rather than ruby red; no high-hardness areas; smooth and even |
| Bloodstone | Dark green chalcedony with red specks; uniformly glassy throughout; no variable hardness |
| Dyed green jasper | Even hardness across both colors; color bleeds in fractures |

## Inflated Value Claims

Ruby Zoisite is an affordable ornamental material. The opaque ruby in anyolite is not comparable to transparent, gem-quality ruby used in fine jewelry and carries a fraction of the price. Fraudulent sellers sometimes pair a legitimate gemological lab report (which confirms identity only, not value) with a fabricated appraisal assigning wildly inflated figures. Any claim that a standard anyolite specimen is worth thousands or tens of thousands of dollars per piece should be treated with skepticism and independently verified.
