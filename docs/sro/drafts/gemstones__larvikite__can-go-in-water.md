# Can Larvikite Go in Water? What You Need to Know

Larvikite can tolerate brief water contact — a short rinse will not dissolve or structurally damage the stone. Prolonged soaking, salt water, and repeated wet-dry cycles are not recommended because they can gradually dull the polished surface. The stone should always be dried promptly after any water contact.

## Why Larvikite Is Broadly Water-Tolerant

Larvikite is a coarse-grained igneous rock composed predominantly of anorthoclase feldspar — a framework silicate mineral with a Mohs hardness of 6 to 6.5. Anorthoclase feldspar is not soluble in water at room temperature and atmospheric pressure. A brief rinse, a splash, or even a short soak will not dissolve any part of the stone or cause structural breakdown.

This distinguishes larvikite from water-sensitive minerals like [selenite](/gemstones/selenite), which dissolves in water, or pyrite, which oxidizes when wet. For a full overview of which crystals are and are not safe in water, see [what crystals can go in water](/learn/what-crystals-can-go-in-water).

## What "Brief Contact" Means in Practice

A brief rinse under cool running water — 10 to 30 seconds — is physically safe for polished larvikite. This is a standard approach for physically cleaning a stone of surface dust or oils before use. After rinsing, dry the stone immediately with a soft cloth and allow it to air dry completely before storing or wrapping it.

The following table summarizes water contact types and their risk to larvikite:

| Contact type | Risk to stone | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| Brief rinse (10–30 seconds, cool water) | Very low | Safe; dry promptly |
| Warm water rinse | Low | Acceptable; avoid hot water |
| Prolonged soak (30+ minutes) | Moderate | Not recommended; may dull polish |
| Salt water | Moderate | Avoid; salt is abrasive and leaves deposits |
| Hot water or steam | Moderate | Avoid; thermal shock can widen grain-boundary micro-fractures |
| Ultrasonic cleaner | Moderate | Not recommended for coarse-grained rocks |
| Water infusion (crystal-infused water for drinking) | Not applicable | Do not use larvikite for gem water; no safety basis for this use |

## Why Prolonged Soaking Is Not Recommended

Larvikite's coarse grain structure means it has more grain boundaries than a fine-grained or single-crystal gem. Over time, water can work into these boundaries, and mineral salts dissolved in water can precipitate there as the stone dries. This process does not destroy the stone but can gradually reduce the sharpness of a polished surface and, in the long term, may lead to small surface pits or cloudiness in highly polished specimens.

Salt water accelerates this process. Salt (sodium chloride) is mildly abrasive, and a salt solution leaves a crystalline residue in grain boundaries as it evaporates. For this reason, salt-water baths are not recommended for larvikite. See [salt-safe crystals](/learn/salt-safe-crystals) for a broader discussion of which stones tolerate salt and which do not.

## Physical Cleaning Without Water

The safest physical cleaning method for larvikite is wiping with a soft, dry or barely damp cloth. A small amount of plain water on a cloth, followed by immediate drying, removes surface oils and dust with minimal moisture contact.

For energetic cleansing — which is a separate practice from physical cleaning — smoke, sound, moonlight, and selenite are all safe options that involve no water. The full list of cleansing methods is at [how to cleanse larvikite](/gemstones/larvikite/how-to-cleanse).

## Sunlight After Washing

If you rinse larvikite and want to dry it in sunlight, brief exposure (15 to 30 minutes) is safe. Larvikite is an opaque dark rock with no photosensitive pigment that would fade; it is not analogous to colored gemstones that bleach in strong UV. For context on which stones are and are not sun-safe after washing, see [sun-safe crystals](/learn/sun-safe-crystals).

## What to Do If Larvikite Gets Wet

If larvikite gets accidentally submerged — dropped in a sink, caught in rain — dry it as promptly as possible with a soft cloth and leave it in a warm, dry place for several hours. A single accidental soaking is very unlikely to cause visible damage to the stone. Concern arises only with repeated or prolonged wet exposure.
