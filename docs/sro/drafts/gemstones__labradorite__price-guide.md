# Labradorite Price Guide: Cost, Value, and What to Pay

Labradorite typically costs $2 to $150 or more per carat at retail, with most commercial material falling between $2 and $50 per carat (prices checked September 2026). Flash quality — the strength, coverage, and color range of the labradorescence — drives value more than carat weight. A 10-carat stone with strong, broad blue-green flash is worth considerably more than a 30-carat stone with a faint, localized shimmer.

## What Drives Labradorite's Price

### Flash quality and coverage

Labradorescence — the iridescent play of color — is the primary value driver. Two dimensions matter:

- **Coverage:** How much of the visible face shows color flash. Stones where flash covers most of the face at a useful viewing angle are significantly more valuable than stones where flash is limited to one patch.
- **Intensity:** How vivid and saturated the flash appears. Weak, washed-out shimmer is common; strong, electric color is less so.
- **Color range:** Blue and green flash is most common. Gold and yellow is moderately rarer. Orange, red, and violet components are rare and add premium value. Full-spectrum multicolor stones (all colors visible as the stone is rotated) are the rarest and most expensive.

A single good photograph taken at the optimum angle can make any labradorite look exceptional. Reliable assessment requires seeing the stone in motion or rotated through multiple angles.

### Grade and origin

| Quality tier | Typical characteristics | Approximate retail price (September 2026) |
|---|---|---|
| Entry commercial | Weak or narrow flash, small color area, rough polish | $2–$10 per carat |
| Good commercial | Visible blue or green flash with reasonable polish | $10–$20 per carat |
| Fine | Bright flash covering most of the face, good viewing range | $20–$50 per carat |
| Premium | Strong multicolor display, rare orange/red/violet, clean surface | $50–$100 per carat |
| Exceptional / Spectrolite | Full-spectrum, superior contrast, collector quality | $100–$150+ per carat |

**Spectrolite** is a trade name for high-quality labradorite from Finland that displays the full spectral range especially vividly. It commands a significant premium over commercial grades from Madagascar, Canada, and other sources.

### Cut and form

Most labradorite is cut as cabochons. Orientation of the cut relative to the lamellae determines how much flash is visible, so skill in cutting directly affects value. Well-oriented, cleanly polished cabochons are worth more than poorly oriented stones with dull or restricted flash. Faceted labradorite exists but is uncommon, as the cabochon form best displays labradorescence.

### Size

Labradorite is available in large sizes and does not carry the steep per-carat premium that scarce gems like fine ruby or emerald do. A 40-carat commercial cabochon is not forty times as valuable as a 1-carat stone of similar quality. Per-carat price stays relatively stable with weight unless superior flash continues consistently across a very large face.

## Typical Retail Examples (September 2026)

| Example form | Approximate price range |
|---|---|
| 10 ct commercial cabochon | $20–$100 |
| 20 ct good blue-flash stone | $200–$400 |
| 30 ct fine full-face cabochon | $600–$1,500 |
| 40 ct premium multicolor stone | $2,000–$4,000 |
| Exceptional Spectrolite or custom-cut gem | Individually evaluated |

These are retail possibility ranges; actual transaction prices vary by seller, market, and individual stone. Many commercial cabochons trade below the table when flash coverage is weak despite large size.

## Durability and Value Retention

Labradorite has a Mohs hardness of 6 to 6.5 and perfect cleavage in one direction. It is moderately durable for pendants, earrings, and brooches but more vulnerable than harder gems in ring settings. Surface scratches can dull labradorescence over time if the stone is not protected. Protective bezel settings preserve surface integrity better than open prong settings for everyday wear. For context on hardness and how it affects durability in use, see [Mohs hardness scale](/learn/mohs-hardness-scale).

## How to Avoid Overpaying

- **Ask for video, not just photos.** A single static photograph taken at the optimum angle makes any labradorite look better than it is in hand. A video showing slow rotation reveals true flash coverage and intensity.
- **Compare across angles, not just one hero shot.** Quality material shows flash across a range of useful viewing angles; weak material looks good from one precise position only.
- **Be skeptical of very high flash at very low prices.** Strong flash at unusually low prices is a warning sign for glass or resin imitation. See [how to identify real labradorite](/gemstones/labradorite/how-to-identify) for practical tests.
- **Check seller disclosure.** Reputable sellers state origin (Madagascar, Canada, Finland, etc.), material type (natural labradorite feldspar), and any treatments. Bare-minimum disclosure is a red flag.
- **Factor in form.** A bracelet or set piece includes cutting, metal, and labor costs. Comparing a loose cabochon per-carat price to finished jewelry is not meaningful.
