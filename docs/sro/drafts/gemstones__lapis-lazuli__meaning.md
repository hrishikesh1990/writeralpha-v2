# Lapis Lazuli Meaning, History, and Traditional Uses

Lapis lazuli is a deep-blue metamorphic rock composed primarily of lazurite, calcite, and pyrite. The name comes from Latin "lapis" (stone) and Persian "lazhward" (blue), the same root that gave English the word "azure." Traded across civilizations for more than 6,500 years, it is traditionally associated with wisdom, truth, and communication.

## What Lapis Lazuli Is

Lapis lazuli is a rock, not a single mineral — a distinction covered in depth in the site guide to [gemstone vs crystal vs mineral](/learn/gemstone-vs-crystal-vs-mineral). Its blue color comes from lazurite, a sulfur-bearing mineral in the hauyne-sodalite group. White veins come from calcite, and the characteristic gold flecks come from pyrite inclusions. The rock may also contain diopside, mica, amphibole, and other minor minerals. The full physical profile is on the [lapis lazuli hub page](/gemstones/lapis-lazuli).

Mohs hardness runs 5 to 6, variable because the rock's mineral composition shifts from specimen to specimen. Specific gravity ranges from 2.50 to 3.00. The stone is always opaque.

The primary source for over 6,000 years is the Sar-e-Sang deposit in Badakhshan Province, Afghanistan — widely recognized as the world's oldest continuously worked gemstone mine. Significant deposits also occur in Chile (often heavily mottled with calcite) and near Lake Baikal in Siberia.

## Historical Uses

Lapis lazuli has been used continuously since at least the 7th millennium BC. Ancient Egyptian royalty used it in jewelry, amulets, and funerary objects, most famously in the gold-and-lapis mask of Tutankhamun. Historical records indicate Cleopatra used powdered lapis as eye cosmetic. At Karnak, carvings of Thutmose III show lapis being delivered as tribute.

In Mesopotamia, lapis lazuli was fashioned into game boards, bowls, dagger handles, and amulets. It traveled the ancient Silk Road to Greece, Rome, China, and India, where it held ceremonial and protective roles in each culture.

Renaissance painters ground lapis to produce ultramarine, the most prized blue pigment of its era. Because the sulfur-radical chromophore in lazurite is chemically stable, ultramarine made from lapis does not fade — which is why blue in surviving Renaissance paintings remains vivid centuries later.

## Meaning of the Name

"Lapis" is Latin for stone. "Lazuli" derives from the Persian word for the Lāzhward region, historically associated with sources of the blue stone. The same root produced Old French "azur," English "azure," Spanish "azul," and Portuguese "azul." Lapis lazuli is, etymologically, "the stone of blue."

In early Christian tradition the stone was associated with the Virgin Mary and used to produce the blue pigment for her robes in medieval and Renaissance painting. The combination of deep blue and golden pyrite carried symbolic weight across the ancient world: the blue of the heavens and the glitter of the sun in one stone.
