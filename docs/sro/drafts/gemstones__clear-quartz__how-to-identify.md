# How to Identify Real Clear Quartz vs. Fakes and Glass

Real clear quartz (SiO2, Mohs 7) can be distinguished from glass, plastic, and dyed imitations using six practical tests: hardness, temperature feel, bubble inspection, specific gravity, optical behavior, and price cross-check. No single test is conclusive on its own; use at least three together.

## What You Need Before Testing

Before testing any piece, read its [full properties on the clear quartz hub page](/gemstones/clear-quartz). Understanding what genuine material looks like — including natural internal features — makes the tests below easier to interpret. ## Test 1: Hardness

Clear quartz has a Mohs hardness of 7, which means it easily scratches a steel knife blade (Mohs ~5.5) and window glass (Mohs ~5.5). A genuine piece should scratch glass without effort when dragged firmly across it. Common glass imitations have a hardness of around 5 to 6 and will not scratch steel or will only barely do so.

How to do it: Hold the suspected quartz piece at a firm angle against a piece of scrap glass or a steel file. If the quartz scores a clear mark on the glass and itself shows no damage, that is consistent with genuine quartz. Wipe the surface and look at the mark under good light — powder from the glass confirms a scratch, not just a smudge.

**Caution:** Synthetic quartz (lab-grown SiO2) also passes this test, because it has the same chemistry and hardness as natural quartz. The hardness test eliminates glass and plastic, not lab-grown material. For the distinction between lab-grown and natural, see [lab-created vs. natural gemstones](/compare/lab-created-vs-natural-gemstones).

## Test 2: Temperature Feel

Natural quartz feels distinctly cool when first picked up and warms slowly in the hand. Glass also starts cool but warms more quickly because glass has a lower thermal conductivity than crystalline quartz. Plastic warms within seconds. This test is subjective but useful as a quick screen; it does not replace the hardness test.

## Test 3: Bubble and Inclusion Inspection

Hold the piece up to a strong light source and examine the interior:

- **Natural quartz** typically contains natural inclusions: tiny fractures called feathers or veils, rutile needles, thin fluid inclusions, or small mineral particles. The interior may look like frozen water or contain wisps and planes of trapped material. Perfectly clear, flawless natural quartz exists but is rarer and commands a higher price.
- **Glass** often contains small round bubbles trapped during manufacture. Round bubbles are a strong indicator of glass; natural quartz does not produce round gas bubbles. Some high-quality glass is bubble-free, so absence of bubbles does not confirm quartz.
- **Dyed or coated material** may show uneven color concentrated at cracks or the surface.

## Test 4: Weight

Quartz has a specific gravity of 2.65–2.66. Common glass runs around 2.5, so quartz feels slightly denser than glass of the same size. Plastic is significantly lighter. If you can weigh two similar-sized pieces — one known quartz, one suspected fake — the comparison is useful, though small differences are hard to detect by hand.

## Test 5: Optical Behavior (Double Refraction)

Quartz is a uniaxial birefringent material in its trigonal crystal form, meaning it splits light into two beams when light passes through it along most directions. In transparent point crystals, you can sometimes see this as a doubling of a line placed beneath the stone (look through the body of the stone at a thin pen line on paper). Glass is optically isotropic and does not produce this doubling. This test works best in well-formed, transparent single crystals viewed perpendicular to the c-axis; tumbled or opaque pieces are not suitable for it.

## Test 6: Price Check

Clear quartz is one of the most abundant minerals on Earth, sourced in large quantities from Brazil, the US, India, and Madagascar. Genuine quartz should not be expensive: common tumbled stones sell for a few dollars each, and raw points and small clusters are similarly affordable. If a vendor claims a specimen is an extremely rare form of clear quartz and charges accordingly, scrutinize the identification carefully. Price alone does not confirm or deny authenticity, but implausibly high prices can signal misrepresentation. See the [clear quartz price guide](/gemstones/clear-quartz/price-guide) for current market context.

## What Genuine Clear Quartz Looks Like

Genuine clear quartz, sometimes called rock crystal, is colorless to white. Well-formed crystals are hexagonal in cross-section with six prism faces and a six-sided pyramidal termination. Surface features may include horizontal striations on the prism faces. The interior ranges from perfectly transparent to milky-white (the milky appearance comes from dense clouds of fluid inclusions). ## Common Imitations to Know

| Imitation | Hardness | Bubbles | Weight vs. quartz | How to spot |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Glass (soda-lime) | ~5.5 | Often present | Slightly lighter | Scratch test fails; bubbles likely |
| Lead crystal glass | ~5–6 | Rare | Similar or heavier | Scratch test fails; no birefringence |
| Plastic / acrylic | ~2–3 | None | Much lighter | Warms quickly; scratches very easily |
| Lab-grown SiO2 | 7 | None | Same | Passes all physical tests; documentation required |
| Selenite (misidentified) | 2 | N/A | Much lighter | Scratched by fingernail; distinct cleavage |

Lab-grown quartz is genuine SiO2 with identical physical properties to natural quartz. No simple home test reliably separates lab-grown from natural; laboratory analysis or documentation from the seller is required if the distinction matters to you.
