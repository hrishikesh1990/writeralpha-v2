# How to Identify Real Brazilian Agate

Real Brazilian Agate is a variety of chalcedony (SiO2) with concentric banding, a Mohs hardness of 6.5–7, waxy to vitreous luster, and a specific gravity near 2.6. The most common identification challenges are distinguishing natural from dyed material, spotting glass imitations, and understanding that most vivid colors are treated — which is normal and not fraud. The tests below can be done at home without laboratory equipment.

## Understanding What "Real" Means for Brazilian Agate

Calling a piece "real" Brazilian Agate involves two separate questions. First: is it actually agate (chalcedony), or is it glass or plastic? Second: is the color natural or dyed? Both "real" categories — natural-color and dyed — are genuine agate and sold openly as such. A vivid blue agate slice is not fake because it is dyed; dyeing is a standard, disclosed commercial process in the Brazilian agate trade.

The deceptive scenarios to watch for are: glass sold as agate, synthetic resin sold as agate, and sellers charging natural-stone prices for glass or plastic. For a general guide to buying authentic crystals online, see [are online crystals real](/learn/are-online-crystals-real).

## Visual Checks

**Banding structure.** Real Brazilian Agate shows concentric banding — parallel curved layers wrapping toward the center of the original nodule. Bands vary in width, shade, and translucency. This structure is formed by successive mineral deposition and is not reproducible in glass at this scale without obvious regularity. Glass imitations are typically either uniformly clear or have swirling streaks rather than true bands.

**Translucency against light.** Hold a thin slice or polished piece against a bright light source. Real agate shows layered translucency where bands of different densities alternate — some bands transmit light, others are more opaque. Glass is uniformly transparent or opaque. Plastic is typically uniformly opaque and shows visible seam lines or surface texture under close inspection.

**Natural color range.** Untreated Brazilian Agate from Rio Grande do Sul is predominantly grey, white, pale beige, and light brown. Vivid, saturated colors (electric blue, deep red, bright green, purple, pink) are the product of dyeing. Knowing this helps set expectations: if you are buying "natural color" Brazilian Agate and it is bright blue, it is almost certainly dyed.

## Physical Tests

**Hardness test.** Agate (Mohs 6.5–7) will not be scratched by a steel knife blade (Mohs ~5.5) and will not scratch glass easily unless pressed firmly (glass is Mohs ~5.5). It should scratch a copper coin (Mohs ~3) cleanly. Glass (Mohs ~5.5) will be scratched by a steel file more easily than agate. Test on an inconspicuous edge, not the polished face.

**Temperature feel.** Like all minerals with reasonable density, real agate feels notably cooler to the touch than glass of the same size when first handled. This effect diminishes after the stone warms in your hand. Plastic warms quickly and does not have the initial coolness of stone.

**Weight.** Agate has a specific gravity of approximately 2.6. A piece of real agate feels noticeably denser than a same-sized piece of plastic, and slightly denser than glass (SG ~2.5). This is a rough test — unusual agate shapes make direct comparison difficult — but a very light piece raises suspicion.

## Checking for Dye

**Swab test.** Dampen a white cotton swab and rub a small area on the back or edge of a vividly colored piece. Color transfer onto the swab indicates the dye is not fully stable under moisture. This does not mean the stone is not real agate — it means the dye finish is porous. Sellers of deliberately natural-color agate can confirm it is untreated; this test helps verify that claim.

**UV light.** Some dyed agates fluoresce distinctly under ultraviolet light, while untreated chalcedony typically shows weak or no fluorescence. A UV response is not definitive on its own but can be a useful extra indicator when combined with the color check.

## What Dyed Agate Looks Like vs. Natural

| Feature | Natural color | Dyed |
|---|---|---|
| Colors | Grey, white, pale beige, light brown, pale blue-grey | Vivid blue, red, green, purple, bright orange, pink |
| Band contrast | Subtle variation | Often sharp contrast between bands of different dye uptake |
| Under strong magnification | Fine grain, consistent texture | Dye concentrated in micro-pores; some color pooling visible |

## Glass Imitations: Key Differences

| Feature | Real agate | Glass |
|---|---|---|
| Banding | Concentric, irregular | Absent or swirling streaks |
| Bubbles | None (in natural agate) | Sometimes present as tiny spherical inclusions |
| Hardness | Scratches steel harder than steel scratches it | Scratched by steel file |
| Feel | Cooler initially, dense | Similar or lighter than agate |

## Price as a Signal

Price is a useful reference but not definitive on its own. Small tumbled Brazilian Agate pieces retail for roughly $2–$8 each; agate slices of modest size run $5–$20; display pairs and bookends typically start at $20 and run to over $100 for large, striking pieces. A price far below these ranges for something marketed as a substantial specimen is a flag worth investigating.
