# Lepidolite Price Guide

Lepidolite is an accessible stone in the crystal market. Tumbled pieces retail for approximately $8–$18 each, palm stones for $18–$35, and raw specimens or carvings for $25–$120 depending on size and workmanship (retail prices checked September 2026; prices vary by grade and seller). Material below $5 per piece is a warning sign for dyed mica substitutes.

## Current Retail Price Ranges

| Form | Typical Retail Range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Tumbled stone (small–medium) | $8–$18 per piece | Most common form in the market |
| Palm stone | $18–$35 | Larger, hand-polished pieces |
| Raw specimen | $25–$80 | Size-dependent; larger pieces command more |
| Carved figure or sphere | $40–$120+ | Skilled carving adds significant cost |
| Beaded bracelet | $15–$45 | Grade and bead size drive price |

These ranges reflect natural, untreated lepidolite from established retailers. Prices vary meaningfully by origin, grade, color saturation, and seller. Wholesale prices for rough material are substantially lower.

Lepidolite is rarely faceted or cut into gemstones in the traditional sense: its Mohs hardness of 2.5–3 and perfect basal cleavage make it prone to chipping during cutting, and finished faceted gems would not survive everyday jewelry wear. A per-carat gem market for lepidolite essentially does not exist. See [how the Mohs hardness scale works](/learn/mohs-hardness-scale) for context on why softness affects processing and value.

## What Drives Lepidolite's Price

**Softness raises finishing costs.** Lepidolite's hardness of 2.5–3 means that polishing requires careful hand-work to avoid shattering thin pieces. The yield from rough stock is lower than for harder stones, so hand-finished pieces carry a cost premium. Peruvian hand-polished material is particularly valued for consistent color and technique.

**Color saturation.** Deep, even purple commands the highest prices within the lepidolite category. Pale lavender or pale pink material is less sought-after. The pink-to-purple color comes from lithium, with iron, manganese, and rubidium adding secondary hues. More saturated color indicates higher trace element concentrations.

**Origin.** Peruvian material from the Arequipa region, hand-polished by skilled artisans, tends to sit at the upper end of price ranges. Brazilian material from Minas Gerais is common for larger raw specimens. Madagascar and Zimbabwe are additional sources; pricing for these varies by exporter.

**Treatments.** Genuine lepidolite is almost always untreated — its color is natural. Some lower-cost material has been resin-stabilized to consolidate crumbly pieces or dyed to deepen faded color. Treated or dyed material should be disclosed and priced lower than natural material. If a seller cannot confirm treatment status, treat the piece as potentially treated.

**Size and form.** As with most minerals, price scales with size and complexity of form. A simple small tumbled piece costs less than a large carved figure that required many hours of careful work given the stone's fragility.

## How to Avoid Overpaying (or Underpaying for Substitutes)

The main risk in the lepidolite market is paying lepidolite prices for dyed purple micas or harder substitutes such as charoite or sugilite. See [how to identify real lepidolite](/gemstones/lepidolite/how-to-identify) for the specific tests. A practical rule: if a "lepidolite" tumbled stone is priced below $5, ask about material composition. Genuine lepidolite at that price point is difficult to source profitably after accounting for the cost of careful finishing.

Conversely, lepidolite should not cost the same as high-demand stones like [amethyst](/gemstones/amethyst) in fine grades, which command premium prices. Lepidolite is available and not rare; very high prices for standard tumbled material are not typical.
