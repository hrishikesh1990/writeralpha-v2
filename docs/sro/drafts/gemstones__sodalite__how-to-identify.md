# How to Identify Real Sodalite

Real sodalite is a royal-blue sodium silicate mineral (Mohs 5.5–6) that typically shows broad white calcite veining and no metallic pyrite. Its streak on unglazed porcelain is white, and its specific gravity (2.27–2.33) is noticeably lower than lapis lazuli's (2.7–2.9). Common substitutes include dyed howlite, dyed magnesite, blue glass, resin, and — most frequently — lapis lazuli sold under the wrong name or sodalite sold as lapis.

## The Main Visual Markers of Genuine Sodalite

**Color and tone.** Sodalite ranges from royal blue to violet-blue to grey-blue. The blue is often somewhat muted or variable rather than a perfectly saturated solid color. Edges of translucent material may show slightly lighter blue or grey where the stone is thinner.

**White calcite veining.** Genuine sodalite almost always shows white or pale grey veining or clouding from calcite inclusions. These veins tend to be broad, irregular, and cloud-like rather than narrow hairlines. A stone that is uniformly saturated blue with no variation at all may be dyed.

**No metallic pyrite flecks.** Unlike lapis lazuli, pure sodalite does not contain brass-yellow metallic pyrite. If you see small golden metallic grains scattered through a blue stone, it is almost certainly lapis lazuli (or a fake of lapis) rather than sodalite. The absence of pyrite does not prove sodalite on its own — pyrite-poor lapis exists — but abundant pyrite rules sodalite out.

**Small black inclusions.** Translucent sodalite commonly contains small black mineral inclusions visible under magnification. These are a positive indicator for genuine material.

## Physical Tests You Can Do at Home

**Streak test.** Drag the stone across the unglazed back of a ceramic tile (or unglazed porcelain). Genuine sodalite leaves a white streak. Dyed howlite or magnesite may leave a pale blue streak where dye wipes off. Lapis lazuli can leave a blue streak from lazurite. A white streak is consistent with sodalite but not conclusive alone.

**Hardness check.** Sodalite's Mohs hardness is 5.5 to 6. A steel nail or knife blade (approximately Mohs 5.5) should just barely scratch sodalite — you may not be able to produce a scratch at all, or only a faint one. Dyed howlite (Mohs approximately 3.5) is noticeably softer and will scratch more easily. Do not perform scratch tests on finished facets or polished surfaces you care about; test an inconspicuous edge.

**Weight (specific gravity).** Sodalite has a specific gravity of approximately 2.27 to 2.33 — it is a relatively light stone. Lapis lazuli has a specific gravity of approximately 2.7 to 2.9 and will feel noticeably heavier for the same size. If you have a known piece of each, comparative hand-weighing is a useful quick check; precise measurement requires a jeweler's scale and water displacement.

**Acetone swab test for dye.** Moisten a cotton swab with acetone (nail polish remover) and rub an inconspicuous area of the stone. If blue color transfers to the swab, the stone has been dyed. This test does not distinguish which mineral is beneath the dye. Natural sodalite that is deeply colored should leave no blue on the swab; surface coatings or wax may smear.

**Cold test.** Real stone feels cold and stays cool longer than glass or resin at room temperature. This is a rough guide rather than a definitive test — it distinguishes stone from resin and plastic but not from other minerals.

## Common Fakes and How to Distinguish Them

| Substitute | How to spot it |
|---|---|
| Dyed howlite | Dark spiderweb veins (not broad pale calcite); softer (Mohs ~3.5); blue dye may transfer on acetone swab; may look too even in color |
| Dyed magnesite | Chalky or granular surface texture; softer than sodalite; blue may concentrate visibly in pores |
| Blue glass | No internal mineral texture; perfectly homogeneous color; may show small bubbles under magnification; slightly heavier feel; warm faster in hand |
| Resin or composite | May show mold seams; internal texture can look poured; often lighter than stone; warms faster in hand |
| Lapis lazuli sold as sodalite | Contains pyrite flecks; heavier; blue may be deeper or more violetish; streak may be blue |

## The Sodalite–Lapis Lazuli Confusion

Sodalite and lapis lazuli are frequently confused because lazurite (the blue mineral in lapis) belongs to the same mineral group as sodalite, and both produce a similar royal-blue appearance with white calcite veining. The confusion runs in both directions: sodalite sold as lapis commands a fraudulent premium, while lapis sold as sodalite is usually a mislabeling error rather than fraud (sodalite is less expensive). For pricing context that helps identify value mismatches, see [sodalite price guide](/gemstones/sodalite/price-guide).

The single most reliable visual separator is pyrite: sodalite does not normally contain it; lapis commonly does. Density is the reliable physical separator: sodalite is distinctly lighter.

## When to Request Professional Testing

Home tests narrow the possibilities but cannot confirm mineral identity with certainty. If you are making a significant purchase — a large carved piece, a collector specimen, or any stone at a price inconsistent with typical sodalite values — request a gemological laboratory report or Raman spectroscopy analysis. Raman spectroscopy identifies the mineral phases directly and can distinguish sodalite, lazurite, calcite, howlite, magnesite, and glass in a single non-destructive test.
