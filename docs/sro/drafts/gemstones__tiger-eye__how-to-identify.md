# How to Identify Real Tiger Eye — 5 Practical Tests

Real Tiger Eye is a quartz-family mineral (Mohs 7) with a silky, mobile band of light caused by parallel fibrous inclusions inside the stone. The five tests below — chatoyancy, hardness, temperature, fiber structure, and dye detection — identify genuine material and distinguish it from glass, dyed howlite, plastic, and treated or assembled pieces.

## What Makes Tiger Eye Distinctive

Tiger Eye forms when quartz replaces fibrous crocidolite (a type of amphibole), locking iron oxide minerals — limonite and goethite — into the parallel fibrous structure. This produces the stone's characteristic golden-brown color and its chatoyancy: a soft, silky band of reflected light that moves across the polished surface as the stone tilts. The chatoyancy is a structural property of the whole stone, not a surface coating or optical trick. That structural distinction is the key to separating genuine Tiger Eye from imitations.

## Test 1: The Chatoyancy Check

Hold the stone under a single-point light source — a flashlight or a focused desk lamp — and slowly rotate it.

**Genuine Tiger Eye** displays a soft, diffuse, silky band of light that moves fluidly and continuously across the surface as the stone tilts. The band is broad and slightly irregular, sometimes narrowing or widening with the natural variation in the fiber structure. It appears to originate from inside the stone, not from the surface.

**Glass and manufactured imitations** typically show a single, hard, mirror-sharp line that snaps from edge to edge. The effect is more theatrical and more uniform than natural chatoyancy; it sits on or near the surface rather than within the body of the stone. Glass cat's-eye beads are the most common imitation sold alongside Tiger Eye.

**Dyed material without a fibrous structure** (such as dyed agate) shows a general sheen or luster but no distinct mobile band.

This single test eliminates most imitations. A stone that fails it is not Tiger Eye.

## Test 2: The Hardness Test

Tiger Eye has a Mohs hardness of 7, which means it will scratch glass (Mohs 5.5) and will not be scratched by a steel knife (Mohs around 6.5).

Use an edge or corner of the suspected Tiger Eye to scratch a glass surface firmly. If it is genuine, it leaves a permanent groove that cannot be wiped away — only a powder smear that clears off. If the stone itself shows a scratch instead, or leaves only a smear, it is not genuine Tiger Eye.

**Glass imitations** (Mohs 5.5) cannot scratch glass. **Plastic and resin** (Mohs 2–3) cannot scratch glass and will be scratched by a steel knife. **Dyed howlite** (Mohs around 3.5) cannot scratch glass.

Perform this test on an inconspicuous edge of the stone and on a piece of glass you are willing to mark.

## Test 3: The Temperature Test

Hold the stone against the back of your hand or your cheek for a few seconds.

Genuine stone is a better thermal conductor than air: it draws heat away from skin and feels distinctly cool, warming only slowly with prolonged contact. Glass warms faster than stone but still more slowly than plastic or resin. Plastic and resin warm quickly — they feel close to room temperature almost immediately.

This test is most useful for distinguishing plastic and resin imitations from genuine material. It is less definitive for separating glass from natural stone.

## Test 4: Fiber Structure Under Magnification

Examine the stone under a 10x loupe or magnifying glass.

**Genuine Tiger Eye** shows fine, parallel, slightly undulating fibrous lines running consistently through the body. The lines are tightly packed and slightly wavy, and the color variation between golden and darker brown bands follows the fiber pattern. Even in polished specimens, this internal structure is visible with magnification.

**Glass** is completely featureless under magnification: no fibrous structure, no internal organization, often small rounded gas bubbles or swirling flow lines visible.

**Dyed material** may show a fibrous structure if it is dyed genuine chalcedony, but the color distribution will be unnatural — either too uniform or concentrated along cracks and grain boundaries.

**Plastic and resin** show mold marks, tool marks, or flow lines, with no natural fibrous structure.

## Test 5: Dye Detection

Examine any natural cracks, fractures, surface pits, or drill holes under a loupe in good light.

**Genuine Tiger Eye** has its golden-brown color uniformly distributed through the fibrous structure, including inside cracks, because the color comes from iron oxide incorporated during geological formation — not applied to the surface.

**Dyed Tiger Eye** (which is still genuine stone, but color-enhanced) shows color concentrated in cracks and fractures, where liquid dye has seeped in. The color in cracks appears more intense or a slightly different hue than the surrounding stone face. In transmitted light (holding the stone up to a lamp), dyed specimens often show visible color pooling along structural irregularities.

Check drill holes carefully on beads: dye-treated beads often show a ring of deeper color at the hole entrance.

## Common Imitations and What They Are

| Material | How to spot it |
|---|---|
| Glass cat's-eye | Single hard mirror-bright line; may have gas bubbles; softer than real quartz; warms faster |
| Dyed howlite | White stone artificially colored; cannot scratch glass; sometimes shows dye transfer on a damp white cloth |
| Plastic or resin | Warms quickly; lighter weight than stone; mold marks visible |
| Dyed genuine Tiger Eye | Natural fibrous structure present, but color pools in cracks; may be unnaturally vivid |

## Treatments in Genuine Tiger Eye

Not all treated Tiger Eye is fake. Two treatments are accepted and widely disclosed in the gem trade:

**Heat treatment** produces red Tiger Eye. Gentle heating oxidizes the iron compounds further, shifting the color from golden-brown to red. Red Tiger Eye with heat treatment is still genuine Tiger Eye — the fiber structure and chatoyancy are intact. Reputable sellers disclose this.

**Nitric acid treatment** is used on overly dark specimens to lighten them to a more marketable golden color. Again, the fundamental mineral identity is unchanged. Disclosure is the ethical standard.

Neither treatment is fraudulent, but both should be stated at point of sale. A good retailer answers questions about treatments in structural terms (heated, acid-lightened, natural) rather than appearance-based language. See [Tiger Eye price guide](/gemstones/tiger-eye/price-guide) for how treatments affect value, and [lab-created vs natural gemstones](/compare/lab-created-vs-natural-gemstones) for the broader question of synthetic vs natural.

Note: there is no commercially available lab-grown synthetic Tiger Eye. If a seller describes a stone as "synthetic," they almost certainly mean an imitation material such as glass or plastic — worth asking for clarification before purchase.

## Buying Online

When buying Tiger Eye online, request a video rather than still photographs — chatoyancy is only visible in motion. Ask the seller directly whether the stone is natural, dyed, or heat-treated. Check buyer-submitted photos in reviews over studio shots, which are often taken under flattering lighting. A listing that uses only appearance-based language ("looks beautiful," "high quality") without specifying the material composition is a flag worth investigating.
