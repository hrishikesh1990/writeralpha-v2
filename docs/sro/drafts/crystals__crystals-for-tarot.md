# Crystals for Tarot: Which Stones Practitioners Use and Why

The stones most commonly used alongside tarot are amethyst, labradorite, clear quartz, moonstone, obsidian, fluorite, and lapis lazuli. Each is chosen for a specific quality in crystal tradition — intuition, clarity, receptivity, or protection — that practitioners align with different aspects of a reading practice. The right stone depends on what you want from the session: preparation, presence during a reading, or grounding afterward.

## What Practitioners Look For in a Tarot Stone

When crystal practitioners choose stones for tarot, they evaluate along three lines:

1. **Tradition alignment:** The stone's symbolic associations should complement one or more aspects of tarot — the intuitive or receptive state before a reading, the clarity needed to interpret cards, or the energetic boundaries that prevent a reader from absorbing a querent's distress.
2. **Physical compatibility with the reading surface:** Stones placed on a reading cloth or mat must be stable and not roll into the spread. Flat bases, palm stones, and small tumbled pieces work best. Sharp or pointed specimens can snag cloth or scatter cards.
3. **Durability under frequent handling:** Tarot practitioners who read regularly handle these stones daily. Mohs 6 or above is the practical minimum for a stone that will be picked up, set down, and occasionally dropped session after session.

## Amethyst

[Amethyst](/gemstones/amethyst) is purple quartz (SiO2), colored by Fe3+ ions under natural irradiation, Mohs 7. It is durable for daily handling, physically stable on a reading surface, and available in formats that suit tarot work — small tumbled pieces, palm stones, and flat slabs that stay in place. Color can fade with prolonged direct sunlight; keep it off sun-facing surfaces when not in use.

In tradition, amethyst is the most recommended stone for developing and maintaining intuition. Practitioners place it at the top of a spread to represent openness to insight, hold it before reading to settle the mind, or keep it on the table throughout the session. For readers who find that their analytical mind crowds out intuitive responses to the cards, amethyst is the primary suggestion.

**Who it suits:** Readers whose main challenge is overthinking or second-guessing card meanings; those new to tarot who want to build confidence in their first impressions.
**Placement:** At the head of the spread; or held in non-dominant hand while shuffling.

## Labradorite

[Labradorite](/gemstones/labradorite) is a plagioclase feldspar, Mohs 6–6.5, with two cleavage directions. Its labradorescence — the shifting blue-green-gold optical effect — comes from light interference between thin alternating feldspar layers. Avoid dropping labradorite on hard floors; cleavage means it can split cleanly along an internal plane if struck at the right angle.

In tradition, labradorite is one of the most strongly linked stones to the threshold between ordinary and intuitive awareness — precisely the space a tarot reader works in. Practitioners associate it with sharpening symbolic perception, deepening the connection to imagery, and moving easily between reading and reflection. It is especially popular with readers who work with complex or esoteric decks where imagery demands sustained interpretive engagement.

**Who it suits:** Experienced readers developing an intuitive or archetypal reading style; those drawn to Thoth, Marseille, or other imagery-dense decks.
**Placement:** Beside the spread; held during the quiet before the reading begins.

## Clear Quartz

[Clear quartz](/gemstones/clear-quartz) is colorless SiO2, Mohs 7. It is the most versatile stone in any practice context, including tarot. In tradition it functions as a neutral amplifier — it intensifies whatever intention or state the reader brings to the session without adding its own directional association. A terminated (pointed) clear quartz is sometimes used to "activate" a spread by tracing the layout before reading.

Practitioners who do not have a specific tradition they follow often default to clear quartz as their tarot stone because it is compatible with any approach. It is also useful when multiple readers share a deck, since its neutral quality does not carry the strong personal associations that stones like labradorite or obsidian can accumulate.

**Who it suits:** Readers who work with many different querents or share decks; beginners who want a versatile starting stone.
**Placement:** At the center of the spread as a clarifying anchor; or beside the deck.

## Moonstone

[Moonstone](/gemstones/moonstone) is orthoclase or sanidine feldspar, Mohs 6–6.5, with two cleavage directions. Its adularescence — the floating, glowing light effect — comes from thin alternating layers of orthoclase and albite feldspar scattering light. Like labradorite, moonstone has cleavage that makes it prone to splitting if dropped or knocked from a table. Its curved, smooth surface means it tends to roll; use a small dish or cloth pocket to keep it stable during a reading.

In tradition, moonstone is associated with receptivity, cycles, intuition, and the ebb and flow of awareness. It is a frequent recommendation for readers who use tarot to track emotional or cyclical patterns rather than to answer direct questions — spread layouts around the phases of a lunar cycle, relationship timings, or inner process work. Practitioners also recommend it for readers who do self-readings, as its tradition includes a strong inward-looking quality.

**Who it suits:** Readers focused on emotional, relational, or cyclical themes; self-reading practice; those drawn to the moon imagery present in many tarot decks.
**Placement:** In a small dish beside the reading surface, or at the left of the spread.

## Obsidian

[Obsidian](/gemstones/obsidian) is volcanic glass, primarily SiO2 at roughly 70–75% by weight, with no crystal structure. Its Mohs hardness is approximately 5–5.5, making it softer than the quartzes and feldspars above, but its main physical property of concern is different: obsidian fractures conchoidally, producing extremely sharp edges on broken surfaces. **Handle polished obsidian carefully and keep it away from surfaces where it could fall onto hard floors and break into sharp fragments.** Tumbled or sphere-cut obsidian is safer for a reading table than raw pieces.

In tradition, obsidian is a truth-revealing and boundary-setting stone. Practitioners who use obsidian in tarot work associate it with uncovering things that are hidden or denied — the shadow aspects that shadow-work spreads are designed to surface. It is a specific recommendation for readers who specialize in difficult or unflinching readings rather than general guidance. Some practitioners place it beside the deck only for shadow-work sessions and put it away for lighter readings.

**Who it suits:** Readers who regularly work with shadow spreads, grief, trauma-adjacent themes, or uncomfortable truths; those who want a boundary stone during emotionally heavy sessions.
**Placement:** Beside the deck, not at the center of the spread; remove after shadow-work sessions.

## Fluorite

[Fluorite](/gemstones/fluorite) is calcium fluoride (CaF2), Mohs 4, with perfect octahedral cleavage in four directions. Its Mohs 4 hardness is the lowest in this group — fluorite scratches easily and chips along cleavage planes if knocked. Use polished or tumbled pieces and handle them gently. For a tarot reading surface, a small tumbled fluorite in a dish is safer than a raw crystal.

In tradition, fluorite is associated with mental organization, discrimination, and the ability to distinguish signal from noise — qualities that practitioners relate to reading clearly without being pulled in too many directions by a complex spread. Readers who work with large spreads (ten or more cards), or who find that they lose coherence in the middle of an interpretation, use fluorite as a focus stone. It is less common in the intuitive-read camp and more common among readers who value logical structure in their interpretations.

**Who it suits:** Analytically oriented readers; those who regularly use complex or multi-card spread structures.
**Placement:** At the side of the reading space; held briefly when the reading feels fragmented.

## Lapis Lazuli

[Lapis lazuli](/gemstones/lapis-lazuli) is a rock — primarily lazurite with calcite and pyrite — Mohs 5–6. The gold flecks in quality specimens are pyrite inclusions. Its surface is susceptible to acids, strong cleaners, and prolonged water contact; clean with a dry or barely damp cloth. At Mohs 5–6, it is softer than quartz and will scratch if stored with harder stones. Keep it in a separate pouch.

In tradition, lapis lazuli is one of the oldest stones associated with truth, wisdom, and communication with deeper knowing. Practitioners connect it to the act of articulating what the cards show — not just understanding a reading internally, but finding the language to express it clearly to a querent. It is a specific recommendation for readers who give readings for others, especially those who want to communicate difficult card combinations with honesty and care.

**Who it suits:** Readers who read for others and want support in expressing complex or hard-to-hear messages; those who journal readings and want language to flow.
**Placement:** Beside the spread; or on the table between the reader and the querent.

## Placing Stones During a Reading

| Position | Stone | Tradition purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Head of the spread | Amethyst | Open intuition |
| Beside the deck | Labradorite or moonstone | Deepen symbolic receptivity |
| Center of the spread | Clear quartz | Neutral amplification of intention |
| Side of the table | Fluorite | Maintain interpretive clarity |
| Querent side | Lapis lazuli | Honest communication |
| Shadow or boundary position | Obsidian | Truth and protection for difficult readings |

No single session needs all six positions. Most practitioners use two or three stones per reading: one for their internal state, one to shape the reading surface, and one for the specific intention of the session.

## Caring for Tarot Stones

Stones used in regular readings accumulate handling and should be cleansed periodically. The quartz varieties (amethyst, clear quartz) can be briefly rinsed with water and air-dried. Moonstone and labradorite can be wiped with a damp cloth, avoiding soaking (feldspar cleavage can absorb water at seams). Lapis lazuli needs dry or minimal-moisture cleaning. Obsidian can be rinsed carefully. For all of these, [how to cleanse crystals](/learn/how-to-cleanse-crystals) covers sound cleansing, moonlight, and other water-free options.
