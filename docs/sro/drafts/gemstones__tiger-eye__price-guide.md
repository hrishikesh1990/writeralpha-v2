# Tiger Eye Price Guide — What It Costs and Why

Tiger Eye is an affordable gemstone. Tumbled stones typically cost $1 to $5 per piece, loose stones $0.50 to $15 per carat depending on grade and chatoyancy, and beaded bracelets $10 to $40. Finished jewelry in silver or gold-plated settings runs $20 to $100 or more. Prices checked September 2026; they vary by grade, seller, and format.

## Typical Price Ranges

| Format | Typical retail range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Small tumbled stone | $1–$5 per piece | Most common retail format |
| Loose stone, common grade | $0.50–$6 per carat | Low-polish, smaller sizes |
| Loose stone, mid-grade | $2–$10 per carat | Better color banding and chatoyancy |
| Cabochon (lapidary cut) | $3–$15 per carat | Well-oriented dome to maximize chatoyancy |
| Beaded bracelet | $10–$40 per piece | Wide spread due to bead size and quality |
| Pendant or ring, silver/gold-plated | $20–$100+ per piece | Setting metal is a major cost driver |
| High-quality statement piece | $100–$500+ | Large stone, strong chatoyancy, quality setting |

These ranges reflect broad retail observation from multiple sources, not a gem-lab grading standard. Tiger Eye has no standardized grading system equivalent to diamond grading, so treat any single listed price as a data point rather than a fixed rule.

## What Drives the Price

**Chatoyancy quality** is the primary value driver. Tiger Eye with a well-defined, centered, smooth-moving band of light commands a higher price than material with a weak, off-center, or interrupted eye. The best specimens show a single strong band that sweeps cleanly across the full face of the stone as it tilts.

**Color uniformity and banding** matter for visual appeal. Even, clearly defined golden-brown bands with good contrast are preferred over muddy or patchy coloring. Heat-treated red Tiger Eye is widely available; genuine, well-colored natural red material is less common.

**Cut and orientation** significantly affect value in cabochons and beads. The cutter must align the dome of a cabochon precisely parallel to the fibrous structure to produce a centered eye — a well-executed cut is worth more than a poorly oriented one. Tumbled stones are less cut-dependent and therefore cheaper per unit.

**Size** raises the per-carat price for top-grade material. Larger cabochons with strong chatoyancy are harder to find without inclusions or fractures and price accordingly.

**Origin** has some effect. South Africa's Northern Cape province is the primary and most established source; Western Australia (Marra Mamba Tiger Eye, a multicolored variant) produces rarer material that can fetch a premium. India also supplies a significant portion of the global market at competitive prices.

**Treatment** affects transparency of description more than price outright. Heat-treated red Tiger Eye and acid-lightened golden Tiger Eye are genuine stone, widely sold, and generally priced within the normal range for their color. What matters is disclosure: reputable sellers state treatments clearly. See [how to identify Tiger Eye](/gemstones/tiger-eye/how-to-identify) for how to spot dyed material at point of sale.

## How to Avoid Overpaying

Tiger Eye is inherently affordable relative to most colored gemstones. Overpaying usually happens in one of three scenarios:

**Paying natural-stone prices for an imitation.** Glass cat's-eye beads are the most common imitation. The chatoyancy test — a soft, diffuse, moving band versus a single hard mirror-bright line — separates them reliably. Check [how to identify Tiger Eye](/gemstones/tiger-eye/how-to-identify) for the full set of tests before buying.

**Paying premium prices for undisclosed dyed material.** Unnaturally vivid or uniformly colored stones that lack the natural gold-to-brown gradient of genuine Tiger Eye should prompt a question about treatment. Reputable sellers disclose dyeing.

**Paying high prices at tourist or gift-shop retail.** Markup in tourist retail contexts is often three to five times the wholesale price. Comparing a few sources online before purchasing in a physical shop gives a realistic sense of fair value.

**Buying without seeing chatoyancy in motion.** Still photographs can make flat material look chatoyant under the right lighting. When buying online, ask for a short video of the stone rotating under a single light source. A seller who refuses this for a premium piece is worth approaching with caution.

## Durability and Value Over Time

Tiger Eye at Mohs 7 is durable enough for daily jewelry wear. It is harder than glass and resists most common scratching hazards from everyday contact. This durability means Tiger Eye jewelry holds up well without requiring the care needed for softer stones. See [Mohs hardness scale](/learn/mohs-hardness-scale) for context on how hardness affects practical durability.

Tiger Eye does not fade in sunlight — its color comes from stable iron oxides, not light-sensitive pigments — so display and sun-charging do not risk long-term color loss.
