# Cat's Eye Price Guide: What It Costs and Why

Cat's Eye chrysoberyl (cymophane) sells for roughly $50 to over $2,000 per carat depending on the sharpness of the cat's eye effect, color saturation, carat weight, and origin. Commercial-grade stones with a visible but weak eye start near $50 per carat; fine stones with a sharp, centered eye and honey-golden color reach $1,000 to $2,000 per carat or more.

## What Drives Cat's Eye Price

Four factors determine where a stone sits within the price range.

**Sharpness and clarity of the cat's eye line.** The chatoyant band should be narrow, bright, and well-centered. A sharp, clearly defined line that moves cleanly as the stone tilts is the single largest value driver. Stones with a broad, diffuse, or off-center band are less valuable even if their color is good.

**Color.** Honey-golden yellow is considered the most desirable color and commands the highest premiums. The "milk and honey" effect — where direct light produces a rich golden half while the opposite side glows pale — is particularly prized. Greenish-yellow and brownish-green stones are genuine and attractive but typically trade at a discount to golden material. Pale, washed-out, or unevenly colored stones are at the bottom of the range.

**Carat weight.** Fine-quality cat's eye in sizes above five carats is genuinely rare. Prices do not scale linearly: a three-carat stone of the same quality as a one-carat stone typically costs more than three times as much per carat, because large chatoyant material with a strong eye is difficult to find.

**Origin.** Sri Lankan cat's eye is recognized as the benchmark for the trade and commands a premium over material from India, Madagascar, or Brazil. Sri Lanka produces the greatest proportion of high-quality honey-colored stones with sharp eyes.

## Price Ranges by Grade (September 2026)

Prices below are approximate retail ranges per carat, aggregated from gem trade sources checked in September 2026. Actual prices vary by seller, certification, and individual stone quality.

| Grade | Color | Eye quality | Approx. per carat (USD) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Commercial | Pale yellow or brownish | Weak or diffuse eye | $50–$150 |
| Medium | Yellowish to brownish-green | Good eye, some inclusions | $150–$500 |
| Fine | Golden-yellow | Sharp, centered eye | $500–$1,200 |
| Collector (Sri Lankan) | Honey, milk-and-honey | Exceptional sharp eye | $1,200–$2,000+ |

Stones above 5 carats at fine quality are rare and can exceed these ranges at auction. Stones below 1 carat are available for considerably less per carat.

## Cat's Eye vs. Other "Cat's Eye" Stones

"Cat's eye" without a mineral qualifier always means chrysoberyl in reputable gem trade usage. Other materials that show chatoyancy — quartz, tourmaline, apatite, scapolite — must be sold with their mineral name attached. These alternatives are much less expensive: cat's eye quartz, for instance, typically sells for a few dollars to $20 per carat. If a stone is sold simply as "cat's eye" for $5 per carat, it is not chrysoberyl.

For guidance on telling genuine chrysoberyl from imitations and other chatoyant materials, see [how to identify Cat's Eye](/gemstones/cats-eye/how-to-identify).

## Treatments and Their Effect on Value

Chrysoberyl cat's eye is generally sold untreated. Heat treatment and surface coatings are occasionally reported but are not standard trade practice. Untreated stones from reputable sources carry a premium over treated material. When buying at mid- to upper-price points, ask for a gemological laboratory report (GIA, GüblinLab, SSEF, or AGL) confirming the stone is natural and untreated. Laboratory certification also verifies that the chatoyancy is natural and not the result of a fiber-optic or glass composite filling.

## Durability and Long-Term Value

Chrysoberyl registers 8.5 on the Mohs hardness scale — harder than most stones encountered in daily wear — and is not affected by common acids. Its durability is one reason it commands a premium over many other chatoyant gems. For a broader explanation of how hardness affects gem durability and suitability, see [Mohs hardness scale](/learn/mohs-hardness-scale).

## How to Avoid Overpaying

- Buy from sellers who identify the stone correctly as "chrysoberyl cat's eye" or "cymophane," not simply "cat's eye."
- Request a gemological certificate for stones priced above $300 total, or for any stone above 2 carats.
- Compare the sharpness and centering of the eye across multiple stones in the same price bracket; eye quality is easily assessed in person under a single-point light source.
- Treat origin claims (especially "Sri Lanka" or "Ceylon") as price premiums that need documentation; country of origin should appear on the lab report.
- For ethical sourcing considerations when buying from online sellers, see [ethical crystal sourcing](/learn/ethical-crystal-sourcing).
