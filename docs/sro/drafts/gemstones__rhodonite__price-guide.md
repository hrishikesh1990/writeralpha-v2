# Rhodonite Price Guide: What It Costs and Why

Rhodonite is an accessible, moderately priced stone. Standard tumbled pieces retail for roughly $2 to $8 each in the US market (September 2026), with cabochons, carvings, and exceptional-quality specimens ranging higher. The main price drivers are color intensity, quality and complexity of the black manganese veining, form, and origin.

## Retail Price Ranges (US, September 2026)

Prices observed across US retail crystal shops and online sellers:

| Form | Typical US retail range |
|---|---|
| Standard tumbled stone (0.5 – 1 inch) | $2 – $5 per piece |
| High-grade tumbled stone | $5 – $10 per piece |
| Cabochon for jewelry (small to medium) | $3 – $20 per piece |
| Raw or rough specimen | $5 – $30+ per piece |
| Palm stone or egg (polished) | $8 – $25 per piece |
| Carved figurine or sphere | $15 – $60+ depending on size |

Wholesale prices are lower. Bulk lots of tumbled rhodonite wholesale for approximately $40 to $90 per pound, which works out to roughly $1.50 to $2.50 per piece at typical piece counts (35 to 40 pieces per pound for standard tumbled material). Consumer retail prices carry the seller's markup above these wholesale costs.

Prices vary by grade, origin, seller, and availability. The ranges above are a general orientation based on sources checked in September 2026, not guarantees.

## What Drives Rhodonite's Price

**Color.** Deep, saturated rose-red or cherry-pink commands the highest prices. Pale, washed-out, or brownish-pink material is less desirable and priced lower. Color saturation is the single most significant factor in per-piece value for retail-grade material.

**Black veining pattern.** The black manganese oxide veining in rhodonite can range from sparse fine lines to dense, complex dendritic or landscape-like patterns. Fine, intricate, aesthetically balanced patterns on a vivid pink body are frequently sold at a premium, particularly in carved pieces where the pattern becomes part of the design.

**Origin.** Russian rhodonite from the Ural Mountains is historically considered the benchmark for quality: intense color, clean texture, relatively fine inclusions. Australian material from Broken Hill, New South Wales, is also well-regarded. Chinese material is most commonly found at the budget end. Origin labeling is inconsistent in retail; if provenance matters to you, ask the seller directly.

**Form and workmanship.** Carved animals, spheres, eggs, and decorative bowls command premiums over tumbled pieces for the same weight of stone, reflecting labor. Faceted rhodonite is rare because the stone's perfect cleavage and inclusions make clean faceting difficult; faceted stones command specialty prices when available.

**Treatment.** Some rhodonite is dyed to achieve more uniform or vivid color. Dyed material is worth less than natural and should be disclosed by the seller, though disclosure is not universal. For tests that can flag obvious dyeing, see [how to identify rhodonite](/gemstones/rhodonite/how-to-identify).

## Durability and Practical Value

Rhodonite's Mohs hardness of 5.5 to 6.5 places it in moderate durability territory — suitable for earrings, pendants, and occasional-wear bracelets, but prone to scratching if worn in rings or subjected to daily hard contact. See [Mohs hardness scale](/learn/mohs-hardness-scale) for what different hardness ratings mean for jewelry durability.

Rhodonite does not appreciate in value the way rarer gems do. Well-patterned, high-color material from classic localities (Russia, Broken Hill) has stable collector demand, but the overall market for rhodonite is broad and supply is adequate. Buying rhodonite as an investment is not supported by retail market dynamics.

## Avoiding Overpaying

- Standard tumbled stones priced above $12 to $15 for common grades without exceptional pattern or provenance are likely overpriced.
- Vivid, perfectly uniform pink stones with no veining sold as rhodonite at very low prices deserve scrutiny — this presentation is more consistent with dyed howlite or quartz.
- Prices significantly below $2 per piece for supposedly high-quality material may reflect low-grade, heavily included, or dyed stock.
- Buy from sellers who describe the origin, form, and approximate size clearly, and who photograph the actual stones rather than generic stock imagery.
