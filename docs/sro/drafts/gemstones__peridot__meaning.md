# Peridot Meaning, History, and Traditional Uses

Peridot is the gem-quality form of olivine, a magnesium iron silicate mineral with the formula (Mg,Fe)₂SiO₄. Its yellow-green color comes directly from iron in its crystal structure — not from trace impurities — making it one of few gemstones whose color is intrinsic to its own chemistry. Traditions across several cultures have associated it with renewal, compassion, and emotional clarity.

## What Peridot Is

Peridot is a transparent, vitreous mineral with a Mohs hardness of 6.5 to 7 and an orthorhombic crystal system. It forms in the earth's upper mantle and reaches the surface through volcanic activity; it has also been found in pallasite meteorites, fragments of the early solar system. The color ranges from yellow-green through olive to brownish-green, depending on iron content — higher iron produces a more yellow or brownish tone, while the most prized stones show a pure, saturated lime green.

Because its color comes from its own iron chemistry rather than from added impurities, peridot is described as idiochromatic. It is never treated with heat, irradiation, or fracture filling; what you see is the natural stone. ## Where the Name Comes From

The name's origins are disputed. The Oxford English Dictionary traces it to an Anglo-Norman word derived from the classical Latin *pæderot*, a kind of opal, rather than the commonly cited Arabic *faridat* (meaning "gem"). The earliest recorded English use appears in a 1705 register of St. Albans Abbey, referencing a bequest made at the death of Bishop John in 1245. Earlier medieval Latin documents often list peridot among church treasures alongside gold and reliquaries.

Alternative historical names include chrysolite (a Greek term meaning "golden stone") and, informally, "evening emerald," because peridot's iron-derived color stays vivid under incandescent light in a way that some other green stones do not.

## Historical Significance

Ancient Egyptians mined peridot on Zabargad Island (also called St. John's Island) in the Red Sea, one of the earliest recorded gem sources. They called it the "gem of the sun" and prized it for its strong color under artificial light; ancient mine workings on the island date back thousands of years. Large peridots from this source appear in the collections of several major natural-history museums.

The gem was known throughout medieval Europe — it appears in the regalia of European rulers and in ecclesiastical art — and in ancient Rome, where it was reportedly a favorite of Emperor Nero. Historians have suggested that some gems described as "emeralds" in ancient and medieval records may have been peridot, including, according to some accounts, stones in Cleopatra's collection.

Peridot is the August birthstone and the traditional gift for a 15th wedding anniversary, designations assigned by the American National Retail Jewelers Association in 1912 and subsequently adopted across the trade.

## Metaphysical and Symbolic Associations

In traditional and crystal-healing contexts, peridot is described as the "Stone of Compassion." Practitioners use it with the intention of supporting emotional balance, releasing past hurt, and cultivating compassion toward oneself and others. It is traditionally associated with the heart and solar-plexus energy centers.

In Vedic and Western astrological traditions, peridot is linked to the Sun and is used by people who want to work with solar qualities — vitality, clarity, and self-expression. It is considered a zodiac stone for Leo and Virgo; see [Leo stones](/gemstones/zodiac/leo) for context on how zodiac associations are assigned.

Crystal practitioners carry or wear peridot as a stone they associate with growth and renewal, and it appears in guides on emotional well-being and grief work — see [crystals for healing](/crystals/crystals-for-healing) and [crystals for grief](/crystals/crystals-for-grief) for how it fits into broader crystal practice. These associations are tradition; they are not medical claims.

## How Peridot Is Traditionally Used

People drawn to peridot's traditional associations typically wear it as jewelry close to the body, carry a tumbled stone, or place raw specimens in spaces where they spend focused or reflective time.
