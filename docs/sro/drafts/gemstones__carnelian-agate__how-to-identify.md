# How to Identify Real Carnelian Agate

Real carnelian agate is a translucent orange-to-red chalcedony (SiO2) with a Mohs hardness of 6.5–7, a cloudy or diffuse color distribution, and no visible crystal faces or cleavage planes. The most common substitutes are dyed grey agate, colored glass, and resin. Four checks — light transmission, drill-hole inspection, hardness, and a dye wipe — catch most fakes without laboratory equipment.

## Understanding What "Real" Means for This Stone

Carnelian agate occupies a broad commercial category. The term covers several legitimate materials:

- **Natural carnelian agate:** Chalcedony whose orange-red color comes from naturally deposited iron oxides during formation. Relatively uncommon in retail.
- **Heat-treated carnelian agate:** Pale or yellow agate or chalcedony heated to oxidize its iron content and develop an orange or red color. This has been standard trade practice for centuries, is widely acknowledged in gem references (Schumann, 2001), and produces genuine chalcedony with the same hardness, chemistry, and durability as natural material. Heat-treated carnelian is not a fake.
- **Dyed agate:** Grey agate soaked in iron salts or synthetic dyes to produce orange or red color. This is also genuine stone but has been treated in a way that affects surface color only, not the underlying mineral. Disclosure is expected but not always given.

What is definitively fake: colored glass, resin, plastic, and dyed non-quartz minerals sold as carnelian agate.

## The Four Key Checks

### 1. Light transmission test

Hold the piece up to a phone torch or bright light source. Press the light flat against one face.

- **Natural or heat-treated carnelian agate:** Glows with a warm, slightly uneven, cloudy orange from within. The color varies across the piece, and internal texture is visible.
- **Dyed agate:** Also glows, but you may see sharp, defined banding — straight stripes with crisp edges where the dye concentrated in the more porous bands.
- **Glass:** Glows evenly with no internal texture, and may reveal small round air bubbles or a faint seam line running around the piece.
- **Resin or plastic:** Minimal glow; the piece looks flat or opaque compared with real stone.

Genuine carnelian rarely shows ruler-straight, high-contrast banding. Soft, gradual color zoning is the natural signature.

### 2. Drill-hole inspection (for beads)

For strung beads, this is the most reliable single test. Hold the bead so light falls into the hole.

- **Natural or heat-treated carnelian:** The inside of the hole is the same orange as the outer surface, because the color runs through the stone.
- **Dyed agate:** The hole typically shows a pale white or grey core with a darker orange ring around the rim, where the surface dye pooled.

A two-toned drill hole — pale center, dark rim — is strong evidence of dye rather than through-stone color.

### 3. Hardness test

Carnelian agate sits at Mohs 6.5–7 — harder than ordinary steel (approximately 5.5) and harder than glass (around 5.5). Draw a steel key firmly across a hidden surface of the piece.

- **Real carnelian agate:** No scratch, or the key leaves only a grey metal streak that wipes away cleanly.
- **Glass:** The steel may leave a faint scratch line.
- **Resin or plastic:** Gouges immediately.

Test on an inconspicuous area — a face that sits against the skin or the underside of a piece. Do not test the display face.

### 4. Dye wipe

Dampen a white cloth or cotton bud with rubbing alcohol (or nail polish remover). Rub firmly on one spot for 10 to 15 seconds.

- **Natural or heat-treated carnelian:** The cloth remains white. The iron oxide color is integrated within the crystal lattice and does not wipe off.
- **Dyed material:** The cloth picks up a faint orange trace.

This test is not foolproof for deeply impregnated dyes, but it catches the most common surface-dyed agates readily.

## Distinguishing Natural from Heat-Treated

Home testing reaches its limits here. Natural and heat-treated carnelian are the same mineral (chalcedony, SiO2) with the same hardness, the same water stability, and often the same visual appearance. Heat treatment leaves no reliable home-detectable signature. Laboratory infrared spectroscopy can sometimes differentiate them, but even this is not always conclusive.

For buying purposes, the practical question is usually not natural versus heat-treated — both are genuine chalcedony — but genuine stone versus dyed agate or glass. For price implications, see [Carnelian Agate price guide](/gemstones/carnelian-agate/price-guide).

## Where Fakes Are Most Common

Mass-produced bead strands and inexpensive bracelet packs are the highest-risk category. Fine cabochons from established lapidary suppliers, rough carnelian from mineral dealers, and pieces with lab certificates carry the lowest risk.
