# How to Identify a Real Silver or Gold Tortoise Ring

A genuine 925 silver tortoise ring can be identified by a hallmark inside the band, a non-magnetic response, and appropriate weight for its size. A genuine gold ring carries a 22K, 18K, or BIS 916 stamp. None of the home checks below require chemicals, though a jeweller's acid test gives a definitive result.

## Check the Hallmark First

The most reliable starting point for any silver or gold ring is the hallmark — a stamped or laser-engraved mark inside the band.

**For sterling silver:**
- "925" or "92.5" indicates 92.5% silver content, the international sterling standard.
- In India, BIS (Bureau of Indian Standards) certified silver carries a hallmark that includes the BIS logo, the fineness mark (925), a centre code, and the year of assaying. Reputable Indian retailers such as Touch925 and Amaltaas describe their rings as "BIS Hallmark Certified 925 Silver."
- "EPNS" (electroplated nickel silver), "silver-plated," or no mark at all indicates the piece is not solid silver.

**For gold:**
- "916" or "22K" indicates 91.6% (22-karat) gold, the standard for Indian traditional jewellery.
- "750" or "18K" indicates 18-karat (75% gold).
- "585" or "14K" indicates 14-karat (58.5% gold).
- BIS hallmarked gold in India carries a six-digit HUID (Hallmark Unique ID) number since 2021. Retailers such as Khazana and Hirapanna Jewellers display this alongside their gold tortoise ring listings.
- No karat stamp, or a stamp reading "gold-plated" or "GP," indicates the piece is not solid gold.

Look for the hallmark using a loupe or magnifying glass in good light. The interior of the band — the part against the skin — is the conventional location for hallmarks on rings.

## The Magnet Test for Silver

Real silver is not magnetic. Sterling silver (925) will not be attracted to a strong rare-earth magnet (neodymium, available cheaply online or at hardware stores). If a ring leaps to the magnet, the base metal is iron or steel — the piece is plated, not solid silver.

**Limitations:** Brass, copper, zinc alloy, and aluminum are also non-magnetic, so passing the magnet test does not confirm the ring is silver — it only rules out an iron or steel core. A ring that passes the magnet test may still be brass or copper with a silver flash coating. Use the magnet test alongside the other checks below.

## Weight and Density Check

Silver has a density of approximately 10.5 g/cm³. Gold is denser still (around 19.3 g/cm³ for pure gold). Brass is approximately 8.5 g/cm³. Zinc alloys and pot metal are typically 6–7 g/cm³.

In practical terms: a solid 925 silver tortoise ring in a typical medium size (2–4 grams) will feel noticeably heavier than a visually similar brass or zinc-alloy ring of the same size. A ring that feels unexpectedly light for its visual bulk is a signal to investigate further. When comparing rings side by side, the genuine silver piece feels more substantial in the hand.

You cannot measure density without scales and water displacement at home, but the weight-in-hand comparison is a useful first screen, especially when purchasing in a market where multiple pieces are present.

## Skin Reaction and Tarnish Behavior

**Skin greening:** A ring that leaves a green or black mark on the skin after normal wear is not necessarily fake. The copper in 925 alloy (7.5%) can occasionally react with acidic sweat to deposit a faint green trace on the skin in humid conditions. However, heavy persistent green staining within a few hours of wear is more characteristic of a high-copper base metal such as brass with a thin silver coating, where the underlying metal is contacting the skin through worn plating.

**Tarnish pattern:** Genuine 925 silver tarnishes evenly — the entire silver surface develops a gray-to-black tone over weeks to months depending on the environment. A plated ring often shows tarnish starting at edges, high-wear points, and the inside of the band — areas where the thin plating wears through first, exposing the base metal underneath.

## Acid Test (Professional Method)

A nitric acid test gives a definitive result. A jeweller places a tiny drop of nitric acid on an inconspicuous area of the ring. On genuine silver: a cream or grey color. On brass or copper: the reaction is green. On silver-plated brass: a green reaction under the surface layer. This test cannot be safely done at home — nitric acid is corrosive and requires proper handling. Most reputable jewellers will perform this test at low or no cost if you request it.

## Identifying a Genuine Jade-Set Tortoise

Tortoise rings set with a carved [jade](/gemstones/jade) tortoise (rather than a sculpted metal tortoise) have an additional identification layer. Genuine jade (jadeite or nephrite) is hard — Mohs 6 to 7 — and will not be scratched by a copper coin (Mohs ~3) or a fingernail (Mohs ~2.5). Imitation jade made from glass, plastic, or dyed quartzite is often softer or visually too uniform. Natural jade has a waxy, slightly uneven luster and typically shows internal variation in color when held up to light. A perfectly uniform, glossy green stone at a very low price is likely dyed quartzite or glass.

## Common Fake and Plated Configurations

| What it looks like | What it likely is | Key tell |
|---|---|---|
| Bright silver, very light, no hallmark | Zinc alloy or aluminum, unplated | Light weight, no stamp |
| Silver surface, green under a scratch | Copper or brass, silver-plated | Visible base metal at worn edge |
| "Gold-look" silver price range | Rhodium-plated or gold-flashed silver | Rhodium wears off; 925 stamp underneath |
| Heavy, dull finish, no stamp | Unknown alloy | Magnet test + jeweller acid test |
| Very cheap with "925" | Check BIS number; hallmark may be fake | Only buy from registered retailers |
