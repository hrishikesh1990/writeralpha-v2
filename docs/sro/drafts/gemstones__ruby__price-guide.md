# Ruby Price Guide — What Drives the Cost and What to Pay

Ruby prices span one of the widest ranges of any gemstone: from under $100 per carat for commercial-grade heated material to over $80,000 per carat for unheated Burma pigeon-blood stones at auction. Color, origin, treatment status, and carat weight are the four main price drivers. Understanding them helps you identify whether a price is reasonable or a red flag.

## Ruby Price Ranges (September 2026)

The following ranges reflect the gem trade as of mid-to-late 2026. Retail markups typically add 20–40% to these figures. Prices shift with market demand and should be treated as guidance rather than fixed rates.

| Grade | Treatment | Origin | Price per carat (trade) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Commercial | Heated | Mozambique or other | $100–$3,000 |
| Fine | Heated | Mozambique | $3,000–$8,000 |
| Fine | Unheated | Mozambique | $8,000–$25,000 |
| Fine | Unheated | Burma (Mogok Valley) | $15,000–$80,000 |
| Exceptional pigeon blood | Unheated | Burma | $80,000–$500,000+ |

At the other end of the market, small ruby cabochons, tumbled stones, and low-clarity rough material are sold in crystal shops for $5–$50 per piece. These are real ruby (or low-clarity corundum) but are not gem-trade material; treatment disclosure is often absent. For astrological or energetic purposes, some traditions require natural unheated stones; for display or general use, the quality distinction matters less.

Astrological-grade ruby (minimum 3 carats, natural, certified) is available from specialist Vedic gem dealers, with natural heated stones in the 1–2 carat range found for $1,700–$2,600 per carat based on September 2026 retail listings.

## The Four Price Drivers

### 1. Color

Color is the primary value factor. The standard of excellence is "pigeon-blood red" — a pure, vivid red with a slight blue undertone, free of orange or brown. GRS (Gem Research Swisslab) and other top laboratories award this designation to stones meeting specific hue, saturation, and tone criteria. A GRS pigeon-blood designation adds 30–50% to market value at the fine end. Pink rubies and orangey-red rubies are priced significantly lower than pure red material.

### 2. Origin

Burma (Myanmar), specifically the Mogok Valley in the Mandalay Region, is considered the premier ruby origin. Mogok rubies command the highest per-carat prices for equivalent quality, partly on the basis of centuries-long auction history and partly because the region's geology produces the chromium-iron balance responsible for the classic pigeon-blood color and strong red fluorescence.

Mozambique, from the Montepuez deposit, has become the largest current source of fine rubies. Top unheated Mozambique stones can match Burma in color quality, and GRS also awards the pigeon-blood designation to the finest Mozambique material. Mozambique stones typically price at 60–80% of equivalent Burma material.

Thailand and Sri Lanka also produce rubies; Thai material tends toward a darker, less fluorescent red and prices accordingly.

Origin determination requires laboratory testing; it cannot be reliably determined by eye.

### 3. Treatment

Approximately 95% of rubies on the market have been heat-treated. Heat treatment is permanent and stable, widely accepted in the trade, and does not by itself indicate lower quality — fine heated rubies can still be extremely valuable. The distinction that matters most is:

**Heated vs unheated.** A laboratory conclusion of "no indications of heating" is among the most commercially significant results a ruby can receive. Unheated rubies command a 2–5x premium over equivalent heated material at the fine end.

**Fracture-filled (lead-glass filled).** Ruby with significant fractures is sometimes filled with lead glass to improve apparent clarity. Fracture-filled material is priced sharply lower — typically a fraction of untreated material — and requires care in cleaning (no acids, no ultrasonic). GIA and other labs test for and disclose fracture-filling; see [how to identify ruby](/gemstones/ruby/how-to-identify) for detection methods.

### 4. Carat weight

Fine rubies above 1 carat escalate sharply in per-carat price because gem-quality material in larger sizes is rare. A 3-carat fine unheated Burma ruby is not simply three times the price of a 1-carat equivalent — it may be five to ten times higher per carat due to scarcity.

## Certification and Why It Matters

For any ruby priced above $500 per carat, a certificate from GIA, GRS, Gübelin, or SSEF is the baseline for establishing value. Certificates record: species and variety (confirming it is corundum / ruby), treatment status (heated, unheated, fracture-filled), geographic origin, and for top stones, a color designation. Without a certificate, a seller's claims about treatment and origin cannot be independently verified.

Both GRS and Gübelin provide online certificate verification systems allowing buyers to confirm a certificate number matches the stone description.

## How to Avoid Overpaying

1. **Buy from sellers who disclose treatment.** Reputable dealers state heat-treatment status explicitly. Absence of disclosure should be treated as potential undisclosed treatment.

2. **Match price to grade.** A ruby offered as "natural, unheated, Burma" at $500 per carat is implausible given trade prices; either the quality is far below fine or the claims are inaccurate.

3. **Understand what you are buying.** Fracture-filled ruby is not misrepresented as long as it is disclosed; undisclosed filling at heated prices is the problem. Small crystal-shop tumbles are a different market entirely — they are usually heavily treated or low-clarity material priced per piece, not per carat.

4. **Consider durability alongside price.** Ruby's Mohs 9 hardness makes it one of the most durable gemstones for everyday wear. See [Mohs hardness scale](/learn/mohs-hardness-scale) for context. Durability does not reduce the value gap between treated and untreated material, but it does mean that fine ruby holds up well in jewelry settings.
