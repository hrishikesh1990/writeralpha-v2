# How to Identify Real White Agate and Spot Imitations

Real White Agate is white-dominant banded chalcedony with a Mohs hardness of 6.5–7, a waxy to vitreous luster, and no cleavage. It fractures conchoidally and shows irregular, three-dimensional banding when viewed under magnification. The most common problems are not outright fakes but dyed or bleached specimens sold without disclosure, and other white minerals — particularly howlite and glass — sold under the White Agate name.

## Key Physical Properties to Verify

| Property | White Agate | What it rules out |
|---|---|---|
| Mohs hardness | 6.5–7 | Howlite (~3.5), calcite onyx (~3), most plastics and resins |
| Cleavage | None | Calcite (perfect rhombohedral), feldspar |
| Fracture | Conchoidal to uneven | Glass also fractures conchoidally, so this alone is not diagnostic |
| Luster | Waxy to vitreous when polished | Resin can look glassy; howlite is chalky |
| Transparency | Translucent to opaque; thin edges often translucent | Glass is often uniformly transparent |
| Banding | Irregular, three-dimensional, varying width and translucency | Printed resin or glass shows repeated or uniform patterns |
| Specific gravity | ~2.58–2.64 | Lighter than glass (2.4–4.0 range depending on type); significantly denser than most resins |
| Refractive index | ~1.53–1.54 (spot reading) | Jadeite (~1.65–1.67); calcite (~1.48–1.66) |

## Step-by-Step Field Checks

### Step 1: Look at the banding structure

Hold the piece to a strong light source. Real White Agate usually shows some form of banding, layering, or structural variation — subtle differences in translucency, color intensity, or fine fibrous texture between bands. The bands are typically irregular in width and three-dimensional, not perfectly flat or uniform.

Glass imitations may show molded or swirled patterns that repeat in predictable ways. Resin pieces often have a printed pattern that looks too regular or terminates abruptly at drilled holes. A completely unbanded, uniformly white, glassy piece is more likely to be glass, milky quartz, howlite, or resin than genuine White Agate, though uniformly pale chalcedony without obvious banding does exist and is sometimes marketed as White Agate.

### Step 2: Check for bubbles and mold seams

Examine the interior under a 10× loupe or magnifying glass. Round, spherical bubbles suspended in a homogeneous background are a strong indicator of glass or resin — natural agate can contain cavities, but they are irregular, not perfectly spherical. Mold seams or repeating swirl patterns also indicate manufacture.

### Step 3: Inspect drill holes and edges

On beads or drilled pieces, look at the inside of the drill hole and the edge where drilling begins. Dye often concentrates in pores and fractures, so the interior of a drill hole may show strong color while the surface looks even. A different material exposed at the drilled edge — a white core inside a colored outer layer — indicates a coated or composite piece rather than solid agate.

### Step 4: Assess weight and temperature

White Agate has a specific gravity of approximately 2.58–2.64 and feels noticeably dense for its size. Resin or plastic pieces feel lighter than a genuine stone of the same volume. Glass can feel similarly weighted. Stone of any kind — agate, glass, or howlite — initially feels cool to the touch because it conducts heat away from the skin; temperature alone is not a reliable identifier.

### Step 5: Dye test

Dye detection is simple: press the piece firmly onto a damp white cloth or cotton swab. Natural White Agate transfers no color. If color transfers onto the cloth, the specimen has been dyed with an unstable or surface-applied colorant. Note that this test only detects poorly fixed dye — some treatments use stable dyes or resin impregnation that do not transfer.

## Common Imitations and How to Distinguish Them

### Howlite

Howlite is the most commonly misidentified substitute. It is typically white with gray, brownish, or black spider-web veining — a texture that differs from agate's rhythmic banding. Howlite is much softer (Mohs approximately 3.5) and more porous; it picks up dye easily, which is why dyed howlite is also sold as turquoise or other stones. If the piece can be scratched easily with a steel knife blade, it is not agate.

### Glass

Glass can imitate translucent White Agate convincingly, especially in beads and slices. Look for spherical bubbles, uniform transparency, an absence of natural banding variation, and molded seams. Common glass has a Mohs hardness of around 5–6, meaning agate will scratch it (not the other way around). Deliberate scratch testing on a finished piece is destructive; use it only on an inconspicuous edge or avoid it altogether.

### Calcite onyx / banded calcite

Calcite-based "onyx" is very soft (Mohs ~3), has perfect rhombohedral cleavage visible as flat breakage planes, and will effervesce (fizz) if a small drop of dilute acid is placed on the surface. It feels lighter than agate of equivalent size.

### Resin composites

Resin pieces are lightweight, may warm up slightly in the hand (unlike stone, which stays cool longer), can show casting marks or repeated printed patterns, and will melt or leave a distinctive smell if a hot needle is touched to an inconspicuous edge. They are softer than agate and can be scratched with a steel knife.

## Treatments Common in Commercial White Agate

Most White Agate on the retail market has received at least one of the following treatments:

- **Dyeing**: Porous bands absorb colorant. Disclosed on quality pieces, undisclosed on many budget pieces.
- **Bleaching**: Used to even out natural color variation and produce a cleaner white appearance.
- **Resin impregnation**: Fills pores and micro-fractures to improve durability and surface finish.
- **Coating**: Surface layer to enhance luster or add color.

Treatment does not make a piece fake — it means the base material is genuine agate that has been processed. The issue is undisclosed treatment that affects price or intended use. ## When to Seek Professional Testing

Field checks are indicative but not conclusive. Definitive identification uses a refractometer (refractive index reading), specific gravity measurement, and — most reliably — Raman spectroscopy, which can confirm quartz-family silica and distinguish it from carbonate, opal, glass, and synthetic materials. If authenticity matters for a high-value purchase, request a gemological certificate or test through an accredited laboratory.
