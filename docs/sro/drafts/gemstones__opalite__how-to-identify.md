# How to Identify Opalite and Tell It From Other Stones

Opalite is synthetic glass sold under many names — argenon, sea opal, opal moonstone, and simply "opalite." Identifying it means two things: confirming that a piece marketed as opalite is genuine synthetic glass rather than plastic or a cheaper substitute, and distinguishing it from the natural stones it resembles — primarily natural opal and moonstone. The tests below require no laboratory equipment and can be done at home or at a shop counter.

For guidance on buying crystals confidently online, see [are online crystals real](/learn/are-online-crystals-real). For the distinction between man-made and natural gemstones more broadly, see [lab-created vs natural gemstones](/compare/lab-created-vs-natural-gemstones). Price signals that help confirm identity are covered in [opalite price guide](/gemstones/opalite/price-guide).

## What Opalite Is and What It Looks Like

Opalite is amorphous glass — it has no crystalline structure, no cleavage, and a conchoidal fracture (the same smooth curved break that broken glass shows). Its defining visual characteristic is a dual-tone iridescence:

- Against a **dark background**: opalite appears a distinct blue or blue-violet, sometimes quite vivid.
- Against a **light background**: opalite appears milky white, often with a warm orange or pink glow at the edges.

This blue-against-dark, white-against-light shift is the single most recognizable feature and is consistent across virtually all standard opalite glass. It arises from the Tyndall effect in the glass matrix — the same physical process that makes the sky appear blue.

## Visual Tests You Can Do Without Equipment

### Test 1: Background color shift

Hold the stone against a black or very dark surface, then against a white surface. If the stone shows a clear shift from blue or violet (dark background) to milky white or warm orange-pink (light background), that is a strong indicator of opalite glass. Natural moonstones show adularescence (a rolling, floating light effect inside the stone), which is visually distinct from opalite's flat-to-the-surface color change.

### Test 2: Look for air bubbles

Opalite is glass, and the manufacturing process sometimes traps small spherical air bubbles inside. Examine the stone under magnification or in good light at a close angle. Visible rounded bubbles inside the material confirm glass. Natural minerals do not contain spherical air bubbles.

### Test 3: Temperature

Glass is a thermal insulator — it warms up quickly from body heat and does not feel as cold to the touch as many natural crystals. Hold the stone in your palm for 20 to 30 seconds: if it warms quickly, that is consistent with glass. Natural opal, moonstone, and quartz feel noticeably cooler for longer. This test is indicative, not definitive, as stone size and room temperature affect the result.

### Test 4: Weight

Opalite glass has a density of approximately 2.6 g/cm³. Natural moonstone (orthoclase feldspar) has a density of 2.56–2.59 g/cm³ — very similar and not distinguishable by hand. Natural precious opal ranges from 1.98–2.25 g/cm³ and feels somewhat lighter for its size. If a piece feels heavier than you would expect for its size compared to other pieces, that may indicate a denser glass formulation or the presence of metal in the composition.

### Test 5: Play of color

Precious opal shows a play of color — vivid spectral flashes of red, orange, green, and blue that appear to move inside the stone as you rotate it. Opalite glass does not show play of color. Common opal (natural, non-precious) also lacks play of color, but it tends to be matte or waxy in appearance rather than glassy. If a piece shows vivid spectral flashes, it is not opalite.

## Physical Tests

### Hardness scratch test

Opalite glass has a Mohs hardness of approximately 5.5 to 6. A piece of quartz (Mohs 7) will scratch opalite, as will most steel files. Ordinary glass will not scratch quartz. If you have a scratch testing kit, test the piece against a Mohs 6 pick: opalite glass will be borderline scratched at Mohs 6 and clearly scratched at Mohs 7. Do this test on an inconspicuous area only — the scratch cannot be undone.

### Refraction

Opalite glass has a refractive index of approximately 1.50–1.52, which is standard glass range. Natural moonstone (orthoclase) has an RI of 1.518–1.526 and natural opal ranges 1.37–1.52. These values are close enough that distinguishing opalite from moonstone by RI requires a refractometer and gemological training. For most buyers, visual tests are more practical.

## Common Confusion and Mislabeling

| Material | How to distinguish from opalite |
|---|---|
| Natural precious opal | Shows play of color; more expensive; may be translucent with internal fire |
| Natural moonstone | Adularescence (floating inner light, not surface color); feldspar structure visible under loupe |
| Plastic imitations | Lighter weight; warmer to touch; may show mold marks or seams; scratches very easily |
| Aura-coated glass | Surface iridescence that can be scratched off; opalite's color is internal, not a surface coating |
| Sea glass or plain glass | Lacks the blue-shift iridescence; usually matte or lacks the characteristic Tyndall glow |

## Price as an Identity Signal

Opalite glass retails for roughly $1 to $5 per tumbled stone and $2 to $25 for jewelry pieces (September 2026). A piece sold at significant multiples of these prices and labeled "opalite" warrants closer examination — it may be natural opal or moonstone, or it may be a pricing issue.
