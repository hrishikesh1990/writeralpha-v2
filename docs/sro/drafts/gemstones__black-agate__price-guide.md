# Black Agate Price Guide

Black Agate is among the more affordable gemstones in the crystal market. As of September 2026, polished tumbled stones retail between approximately $2 and $15 per piece, and stretch bracelets between $8 and $20 at retail. Prices are low because agate is abundant and the black color is almost always produced by a standard dyeing treatment rather than being naturally occurring. What drives cost upward is format, size, and quality of polish, not rarity.

## Typical Price Ranges by Format

| Format | Approximate retail range (September 2026) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Tumbled stone (small, 1–3 cm) | $2–8 per piece | Most common format; widely available |
| Tumbled stone (large, 4–6 cm) | $5–15 per piece | Price rises with size |
| Stretch bracelet (8 mm beads) | $8–20 per bracelet | Varies by bead size and setting quality |
| Raw/unpolished specimen | $3–12 per piece | Less polished; good for display |
| Pendant or set jewelry | $10–40+ | Metal setting quality drives price upward |
| Large display piece or slab | $15–60+ | Uncommon; price depends on size and grade |

Wholesale prices are substantially lower. Bulk bracelets were listed at $1.39 per piece (30-piece lots) from wholesale suppliers and $7.95 each at single-unit MSRP from wholesale crystal suppliers as of the dates checked. Retail shops apply typical markups of 100–300% over wholesale.

## What Drives Price

**Treatment status**: Almost all commercial black agate is dyed chalcedony. Natural black agate — colored intrinsically by carbonaceous matter or iron-manganese mineral inclusions — is genuinely rarer and commands a modest premium among collectors, though it is rarely distinguished in mainstream crystal retail. Most sellers do not disclose treatment status.

**Size and weight**: Larger pieces require proportionally more material. A palm-sized polished tumbled stone costs more than a thumbnail-sized one from the same source.

**Polish quality**: Well-polished material with no pit marks, chips, or dull zones commands a higher price than poorly finished material with the same mineral content.

**Format**: Raw rough is typically cheaper than tumbled, which is typically cheaper than cut cabochons, which are cheaper than finished jewelry. Each processing step adds labor cost.

**Seller type**: Crystal specialty shops carry higher retail prices than wholesale platforms. Online direct-from-manufacturer listings sit closer to wholesale prices. Artisan jewelers set prices based on their labor, not just the stone cost.

**Geographic origin**: Origin is rarely specified for commercial black agate. India and Brazil are major producers of commercial-grade dyed agate. Stones with documented locality from notable deposits can attract collector premiums.

## How to Avoid Overpaying

Paying significantly above $15 for a single small tumbled stone of black agate, without a specific rarity claim and evidence to support it, is unlikely to reflect the stone's inherent value. Compare prices across multiple sellers before purchasing a piece above $20.

Ask whether the stone is natural or dyed. A seller who discloses treatment status is more likely to be representing their stock accurately. Most black agate is dyed — that is normal and acceptable — but if a seller claims "all-natural untreated" without supporting documentation, treat the claim with caution. Check the return policy before purchasing online. Images of black stones are easy to misrepresent in terms of surface quality.
