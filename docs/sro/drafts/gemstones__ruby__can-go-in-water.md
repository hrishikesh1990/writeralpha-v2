# Can Ruby Go in Water? What You Need to Know

Untreated and heat-treated ruby can safely go in water. Ruby ranks 9 on the Mohs hardness scale, has no cleavage, and is chemically stable — warm soapy water is a standard and safe cleaning method. The exception is fracture-filled or dyed ruby, where even mild acids (including lemon juice) can damage the enhancement. Before putting ruby in water, identify whether your stone has been treated with lead-glass fracture-filling.

## Why Hardness and Treatment Both Matter

Ruby is corundum (Al2O3) colored by trace chromium. With a Mohs hardness of 9 — second only to diamond — it resists scratching and tolerates water without any structural damage to the mineral itself.

The risk from water is not to the corundum: it is to any enhancement material inside the stone. GIA identifies several treatment categories with different water tolerances:

**Untreated ruby.** No treatment has been applied. Warm soapy water, ultrasonic cleaners, and steam are all safe. The GIA explicitly lists warm soapy water as safe for untreated material.

**Heat-treated ruby.** The large majority of rubies on the market — estimated at roughly 95% — have been heated to improve color and clarity. Heat treatment is stable and permanent. Warm soapy water, ultrasonic, and steam cleaners are all generally safe.

**Fracture-filled ruby (lead-glass filled).** Ruby with significant fractures or cavities is sometimes filled with lead glass to improve apparent clarity and color. The glass filler is not chemically stable in the same way corundum is. GIA states that fracture-filled and cavity-filled stones can be damaged by even mild acids such as lemon juice. Sustained water exposure is also a concern. These stones should be cleaned with a damp cloth only — no soaking, no ultrasonic, no steam.

**Dyed ruby.** Some lower-priced corundum is dyed. Dye can be leached by extended water exposure or warm water. Damp cloth only.

If you have a certificate from GIA, GRS, or another recognized laboratory, it will specify the treatment type. Without a certificate, treat the stone as potentially fracture-filled and use minimal water.

## How to Clean Ruby in Water (Untreated or Heat-Treated)

1. Fill a small bowl with warm (not hot) water and add a drop of mild dish soap.
2. Place the ruby in the water and let it soak for two to three minutes.
3. Use a soft brush (a clean, soft toothbrush works well) to scrub all surfaces, including the back of a set stone.
4. Rinse thoroughly under clean running water.
5. Pat dry with a lint-free cloth.

Avoid: boric acid powder (it etches corundum surfaces), harsh household cleaners, and very hot water if the piece has other gemstones or adhesive settings.

## Salt Water and Salt

Salt water is not recommended even for untreated ruby. Salt is mildly abrasive and, in solution, can work into microscopic cracks over time. More practically, salt water is harder to rinse away from set pieces, and any residue left in a setting can damage metals. For salt-based cleansing in the energetic sense, see [salt-safe crystals](/learn/salt-safe-crystals).

Dry salt burial — a common ritual cleansing method — is not recommended for set jewelry, as salt is hygroscopic and can draw moisture against metal and fillers.

## Sun After Water

Ruby tolerates brief sunlight exposure without fading or damage. After cleaning with water, placing an untreated or heat-treated ruby in morning sunlight for 15 to 30 minutes is physically safe and is used in crystal tradition as a recharging step.
