# Turquoise Price Guide — What It Costs and Why

Turquoise prices range from under $1 per carat for dyed imitations to $300 or more per carat for fine untreated material from premium sources. The single biggest variable is treatment: natural untreated turquoise is rare and commands the highest prices, while stabilized, reconstructed, and dyed material fills most of the retail market at far lower costs. Prices below were checked against retail survey data from July 2026.

## Price Ranges by Grade

| Category | Typical characteristics | Approximate price (Sept 2026) |
|---|---|---|
| Dyed imitation or reconstructed | Dyed howlite, magnesite, pressed chips, or resin-rich composite | $0.50–$5 per carat |
| Stabilized commercial turquoise | Pale or porous natural material impregnated with resin | $2–$20 per carat |
| Good natural or lightly treated | Attractive blue or green, reasonable density and color | $10–$60 per carat |
| Fine untreated turquoise | Even saturated color, strong polish, desirable matrix or clean face | $50–$150 per carat |
| Premium mine-grade material | Sleeping Beauty, Persian, or named American mine material with provenance | $100–$300 per carat |
| Exceptional collector quality | Rare untreated spiderweb, old-stock deposit, historically important provenance | $300–$500+ per carat |

Note: turquoise is also sold by weight in grams (five carats equal one gram), by the cabochon, by the bead strand, and as finished jewelry. Per-carat pricing becomes less meaningful for large carvings or rough slabs, where the work and artistic merit add additional value.

## What Drives Turquoise Price

### Treatment and natural status

Treatment is the most important factor. The vast majority of turquoise on the market has been stabilized with resin, waxed, or had color added. Stabilization is not fraudulent when disclosed — it transforms porous, pale material into durable jewelry-grade stone — but it significantly lowers the price relative to untreated material of the same color. Natural, untreated turquoise of gem quality is genuinely scarce. When sellers claim "natural" without documentation, ask specifically whether the stone has been treated in any way.

For guidance on spotting dyed and reconstructed material before purchase, see [how to identify turquoise](/gemstones/turquoise/how-to-identify).

### Color

Saturated sky blue and robin's-egg blue have historically commanded the highest international demand. The ideal tone is rich but not artificial-looking — a slightly chalky or grayish blue indicates lower density and lower value. Blue-green and green turquoise can also be valuable, particularly from specific named deposits such as Carico Lake (Nevada), Royston (Nevada), or certain Chinese mines where that color is characteristic. Evenness of color matters for plain cabochons; for patterned stones, the interaction between color and matrix is what is being evaluated.

### Matrix pattern

Matrix — the host rock veining running through the stone — does not automatically reduce value. Fine natural spiderweb matrix (thin, even, dark veining creating a web pattern) is actively sought by collectors and can increase value substantially over clean material of the same base color. Random or unstable fracture-based veining reduces value. The key distinction is whether the pattern looks integrated with the stone (natural) or applied to it.

### Mine provenance

Named mine origin adds premium at the collector end of the market. The Sleeping Beauty mine in Arizona — closed since 2012 — produced consistently clean, robin's-egg blue material that remains highly collectible. Persian (Iranian) turquoise from the Nishapur district historically set the standard for blue-green color. American mines with documented production — Kingman, Morenci, Bisbee, Carico Lake — carry premiums when provenance can be verified. Without documentation, mine claims cannot be relied upon.

### Cutting and finishing quality

Well-cut cabochons with symmetrical domes, smooth surfaces, and centered matrix patterns sell at higher per-carat prices than irregularly cut or poorly polished material of equivalent grade. Thin stones, chips, and beads are typically priced lower per carat than larger, well-formed cabochons.

### Hardness and durability

Turquoise has a Mohs hardness of 5 to 6 — softer than many gemstones. Dense, hard natural material (which exists but is rare) is more durable and commands higher prices than soft, porous, and chalky material. Stabilization compensates for porosity but not for low density. For context on how hardness affects gem value and durability, see [Mohs hardness scale](/learn/mohs-hardness-scale).

## How to Avoid Overpaying

1. **Ask for treatment disclosure.** Any reputable seller should be able to state whether a piece is natural, stabilized, waxed, or dyed. Refusal or vague language is a red flag.
2. **Compare price to grade honestly.** Very cheap "turquoise" (bead strands for a few dollars, tumbled pieces at $1–$2 each) is almost certainly dyed howlite or magnesite unless clearly labeled as such. Very expensive pieces without provenance documentation deserve scrutiny.
3. **Know what you are buying.** Stabilized turquoise is a legitimate, durable product that makes up the majority of commercial jewelry — buying it at an appropriate price for its grade is a reasonable choice, not a mistake. Overpaying for it as "natural" is the problem.
4. **Check ethical sourcing.** Turquoise mining involves extraction from arid landscapes and raises questions about Indigenous cultural heritage in the American Southwest context.
