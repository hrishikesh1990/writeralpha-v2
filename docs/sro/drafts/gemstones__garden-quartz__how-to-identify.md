# How to Identify Real Garden Quartz and Spot Fakes

Real Garden Quartz is quartz (SiO2) with natural mineral inclusions — typically chlorite, iron oxides, or feldspar — sealed inside the crystal during growth. The key tests are hardness (it scratches glass), the parallax depth of inclusions when you tilt the stone, and the cool, glassy weight of quartz versus the warmer, lighter feel of glass or resin imitations. Each check is explained below.

## What You Are Looking For

Garden Quartz is also sold as lodolite, scenic quartz, landscape quartz, and shaman quartz. All refer to the same material: clear to smoky-gray quartz containing visible internal inclusions that create landscape-like or mossy scenes within the crystal. The inclusions are what give it value, so most fakes focus on mimicking the internal appearance rather than the external shape.

The most common imitations and problem materials are:

| Material | How it mimics Garden Quartz | Key giveaway |
|---|---|---|
| Glass with embedded material | Paint, foil, or plant matter inside glass | Bubbles; surface feels lighter and warmer; no crystal structure |
| Assembled stone | Genuine quartz cap over a painted or foiled backing | Seam visible on edge; thin appearance of inclusions |
| Dyed clear quartz | Dye injected into cracks or fractures | Color pooled in fractures; too even or too vivid |
| Phantom quartz | Ghost-like growth zones inside quartz | Inclusions form regular geometric outlines, not mossy scenes |
| Moss agate | Green dendritic patterns in chalcedony | Waxier luster; translucent rather than transparent |

## Step 1: Check the Inclusions for Depth

Hold the piece under a single directional light source — a lamp or window — and tilt it slowly. In genuine Garden Quartz, the inclusions sit at different depths inside the crystal, and the apparent position of each element shifts slightly as you tilt (parallax). A green patch near the base appears to move relative to a reddish cloud higher up. This three-dimensional shift cannot be replicated by a flat painting, foil layer, or a photo glued inside glass.

Glass fakes with embedded material tend to look flat — all the "inclusions" appear at the same depth, and the scene does not shift as you tilt.

## Step 2: Test Hardness

Quartz has a Mohs hardness of 7. It scratches glass (hardness about 5.5) and common steel (about 6.5). Take a glass plate or the back of a ceramic tile and try to scratch it with the edge or a point of the specimen. A genuine quartz piece will leave a clear scratch. Glass fakes will not scratch glass — glass scratches glass at roughly equal hardness and does not leave a clean line.

If you are testing a polished sphere or tumbled stone, use a corner of the base rather than the polished face, which may show a slight scratch mark from the test.

## Step 3: Feel the Temperature and Weight

Pick up the piece and hold it in your palm for 10 seconds. Quartz stays cool longer than glass or resin because of its thermal properties and density (2.65 g/cm³). Cheap glass fakes and resin feel lighter and warm up faster. This is not a definitive test alone but is useful alongside hardness and depth checks.

## Step 4: Inspect Inclusions Under Magnification

Use a 10× jeweler's loupe or a phone macro lens. Look for:

- **Natural inclusions:** Irregular edges, variation in density throughout the crystal, tiny voids or growth steps around the inclusion material. Chlorite inclusions look like actual mineral flakes or fibrous masses; iron oxide patches have irregular, diffuse boundaries.
- **Dye:** Color concentrated in fine cracks and fractures, often too evenly saturated, and sometimes visibly different in character from the surrounding material. Greens or reds that look painted on rather than embedded.
- **Glass bubbles:** Round or elongated gas bubbles, often spherical and uniform, are a clear indicator of glass. Natural quartz may contain fluid inclusions, but these are tiny and irregular, not the large spherical bubbles typical of glass.
- **Seams:** On assembled stones, look for a horizontal seam near the base where two pieces have been joined.

## Step 5: Assess Seller Information

Price is a reliable signal — see [Garden Quartz price guide](/gemstones/garden-quartz/price-guide) for typical retail ranges. A polished point selling for $3 with vivid, uniform "landscape" inclusions deserves closer inspection. Ask the seller for the origin (Minas Gerais, Brazil and Madagascar are the primary genuine sources) and whether any treatments have been applied.

For online purchases, request multiple photos taken in daylight from different angles. Avoid listings that show only one image against a vivid colored background. The site-wide guide to [buying crystals online](/learn/are-online-crystals-real) covers how to evaluate sellers. The [comparison of lab-created vs. natural gemstones](/compare/lab-created-vs-natural-gemstones) is a separate question — synthetic quartz exists but is generally sold as such, not as lodolite.

## What Genuine Garden Quartz Looks Like

The [Garden Quartz meaning page](/gemstones/garden-quartz/meaning) describes the typical appearance in detail. In short: a transparent to translucent quartz body, sometimes slightly smoky or milky, containing uneven, landscape-like inclusions — green mossy patches (chlorite), reddish-brown clouds or needles (iron oxide), cream-colored masses (feldspar), or sometimes fine golden needles (rare rutile). No two pieces are alike. The scene should appear genuinely three-dimensional and shift with viewing angle.
