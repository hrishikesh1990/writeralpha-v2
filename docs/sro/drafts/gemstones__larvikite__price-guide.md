# Larvikite Price Guide — What It Costs and Why

Larvikite is an affordable stone by crystal market standards. Tumbled pieces commonly sell for $3 to $15 each; spheres and larger carvings range from $15 to $80 depending on size and quality of schiller. The stone is quarried in substantial quantities in Norway and is not scarce, which keeps prices accessible.

## Why Larvikite Is Modestly Priced

Larvikite is a variety of syenite — an igneous rock — quarried from the Larvik region of Norway on an industrial scale. It has been used for decades as architectural facing stone, countertops, and decorative carvings worldwide. The large volume of extraction means rough material for the crystal and gemstone trade is a byproduct of a much larger stone industry. This supply situation keeps prices low compared with rare gemstones, even though the quality of the schiller effect varies considerably from piece to piece.

Larvikite is not sold by carat weight in the conventional gem market. It is priced per piece for tumbled stones and small specimens, by diameter for spheres, and by weight or dimensions for slabs and larger carvings. The Mohs hardness of 6 to 6.5 makes it moderately durable and practical for jewelry and carvings; see [Mohs hardness scale](/learn/mohs-hardness-scale) for context on what that durability means for daily wear.

Architectural slab material (countertops, tiles) is priced very differently — per square foot, not per piece — and is outside the scope of this guide.

## What Drives the Price of a Specific Piece

**Schiller intensity and coverage:** The silver-blue schiller effect is the primary aesthetic value of larvikite. Pieces with a strong, evenly distributed flash across the whole surface command higher prices than pieces where the schiller is faint, patchy, or visible only from narrow angles.

**Polish quality:** A high-gloss polish that reveals the schiller clearly is worth more than a rough or semi-polished finish. For spheres in particular, the polish uniformity affects how the schiller plays across the curved surface.

**Size:** Larger pieces require more material and more labor to shape and polish, so price scales broadly with size.

**Form complexity:** Carvings and towers require more labor than tumbled stones, and unusual forms (skulls, animals, detailed carvings) carry a premium.

**Seller markup and provenance claims:** Some sellers add significant markup for metaphysical framing, provenance certificates, or branding. The stone itself is the same; you are paying for the presentation.

**Trade name confusion:** Larvikite is sometimes sold under the names "Black Moonstone," "Blue Granite," or "Blue Pearl." "Black Moonstone" in particular is a trade name used inconsistently in the crystal market and causes pricing confusion; genuine moonstone and larvikite are different minerals with different properties. If you are specifically seeking larvikite, check that the description confirms it is from Norway and describes the schiller effect.

## How to Avoid Overpaying

- **Compare across multiple sellers** before buying, particularly for spheres and larger pieces. Price variation for the same quality is significant in the crystal market.
- **Look at the schiller, not just the base color.** A piece sold as larvikite with no visible schiller in the listing photos may be another dark stone.
- **Avoid purchasing labeled "Black Moonstone" unless the seller confirms it is larvikite.** True black moonstone (orthoclase feldspar) exists but is different from larvikite; the trade name overlap causes confusion.
- **Check the identification markers.** See [how to identify larvikite](/gemstones/larvikite/how-to-identify) for practical field tests you can apply to a piece before buying.
- **Understand that ethical sourcing adds to price.** Larvikite from verified Norwegian quarries with clear supply chain documentation is more expensive than unverified material. For guidance on evaluating crystal sourcing claims, see [ethical crystal sourcing](/learn/ethical-crystal-sourcing).
