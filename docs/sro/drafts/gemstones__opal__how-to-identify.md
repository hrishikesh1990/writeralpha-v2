# How to Identify Real Opal — Tests and Red Flags

Real opal is a hydrated silica mineraloid whose play of color comes from light diffracting through stacked silica spheres inside the stone. It can be distinguished from glass and plastic by visual inspection, a refractive index reading, and a specific gravity test. Natural solid opal has no seam; doublets and triplets show a glue line at the girdle. Synthetic opal shows a distinctive "lizard-skin" pattern under magnification.

## Start with Visual Inspection

Before any instrument, examine the stone under good light:

**Play of color direction**: In natural solid opal, play of color is visible from multiple angles and moves smoothly as you tilt the stone. In doublets (opal slice glued to a dark backing) and triplets (opal slice sandwiched between a backing and a glass or quartz cap), the color may look vivid face-on but appears flat or disappears when the stone is tilted past about 30 degrees.

**Depth**: In crystal opal and Ethiopian hydrophane opal, color appears to come from within the stone. In glass imitations, color often looks painted-on or surface-level.

**Pattern under magnification**: Use a 10x loupe. Natural precious opal shows a mosaic of color patches with organic, irregular edges. Synthetic opal — which is real silica opal but laboratory-made — frequently shows a regular "lizard-skin" pattern: color cells separated by straight lines in a columnar structure. This is the most reliable sign of synthetic material.

**Body color**: Common opal without play of color (often sold as pink opal, blue opal, or green opal) should be distinguished from dyed chalcedony or glass sold under the same trade names. Pale pink Peruvian opal is a real stone; bright "bubble gum" pink pieces at very low prices often are not.

## Check the Back and Sides

Turn the stone over:

- A **glue line** running around the girdle (the widest point) indicates a doublet or triplet. The glue line may be invisible from the front but becomes visible when you look at the side at an angle. Assembled stones are not fraud if disclosed, but they sell for a fraction of solid opal — see [opal price guide](/gemstones/opal/price-guide).
- A **flat, black, or reflective back** on a stone with an otherwise rounded face is a sign of a doublet.
- A **glass or quartz dome** visible on the top, with the opal layer visible inside when viewed from the side, indicates a triplet.

For more on lab-created vs natural material across all gemstones, see [lab-created vs natural gemstones](/compare/lab-created-vs-natural-gemstones).

## Physical Property Tests

The following tests can be done at home with basic equipment or by a jeweler:

| Test | Natural solid opal | Glass / plastic | Synthetic opal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Refractive index | 1.37–1.47 | ~1.51–1.52 (glass) | 1.44–1.45 |
| Specific gravity | 1.98–2.25 | ~2.5 (glass) | 1.74–2.07 |
| Feel | Slightly warm | Glass is cooler initially | Similar to natural |
| Under loupe | Organic color patches | Swirl marks, bubbles | Columnar "lizard-skin" |

A gemological refractometer reads RI. A hydrostatic balance measures specific gravity. Both are standard jeweler tools; if you cannot test yourself, a local gemologist can do this in minutes.

**Temperature check**: Glass stays noticeably cooler than room temperature when first picked up; natural opal warms faster in the hand because of its lower density. This is a rough indicator only, not a definitive test.

## Hydrophane Test for Ethiopian Opal

Ethiopian Welo opal is a hydrophane variety — it absorbs water. A quick test: breathe on or briefly moisten a small area of the stone. Natural Ethiopian opal will temporarily cloud or change color as water enters the pores; the effect reverses as it dries. Glass and most other imitations do not respond this way. This test is not appropriate for Australian opal or for opal in jewelry settings.

## Red Flags When Buying

- **Play of color that looks too perfect**: Extremely regular, poster-bright color patches in a symmetrical mosaic suggest synthetic material.
- **Price that seems impossibly low for solid black opal**: Solid black opal from Lightning Ridge rarely retails below several hundred dollars per carat for gem quality. Pieces advertised as "black opal" at $10–$30 each are almost certainly doublets, triplets, or common opal with a dark backing.
- **Seller refuses to disclose construction**: Reputable sellers always state whether a piece is solid, doublet, or triplet.
- **Online photos taken under extreme lighting**: Play of color in natural opal is real but varies with viewing angle. Photographs with very controlled backlit studio lighting can make modest material look extraordinary.
