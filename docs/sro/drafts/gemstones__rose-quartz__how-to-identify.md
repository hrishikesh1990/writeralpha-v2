# How to Identify Real Rose Quartz vs Fakes and Glass

Real Rose Quartz is a quartz variety (SiO2) with Mohs hardness 7, natural inclusions, and a translucency that varies unevenly across the stone. The most common imitations are pink glass and dyed quartzite or synthetic material. Several tests you can do at home narrow the field; a refractometer or gemological lab confirms it conclusively.

## What Real Rose Quartz Looks Like

Rose Quartz gets its pink color from trace amounts of titanium, iron, or manganese within the silicon dioxide crystal lattice. The color is typically pale to medium pink, diffuse, and uneven — stronger in some zones, fading toward edges or inclusions. Translucency varies: some pieces are nearly opaque and milky, others semi-transparent with a soft glow.

A useful diagnostic is the fibrous silk: many rose quartz specimens contain microscopic rutile needles aligned in the crystal. Held under strong light at an angle, these produce a soft sheen or, in high-quality cabochons, a six-rayed star (asterism). Glass and most synthetic imitations do not produce this effect.

## Home Tests

### Temperature test

Real quartz feels noticeably cool when pressed against the inside of your wrist and stays cool longer than glass of the same size. Glass and plastic warm quickly because they are less dense and poorer thermal conductors than crystalline quartz. This test is directional — it distinguishes quartz from glass or plastic but does not tell you whether the quartz has been dyed.

### Hardness test

Rose Quartz is Mohs 7. It will scratch ordinary glass (Mohs ~5.5) with light pressure. Use a piece of glass you are willing to mark — a glass plate or a piece of broken windowpane — and press the corner of the specimen firmly across it. A real quartz piece leaves a clean scratch in the glass; the glass leaves no mark on the quartz. If the specimen scratches easily or leaves powder on the glass, it may be softer material (dyed quartzite, calcite, or synthetic glass).

Do not use this test on polished cabochons where you cannot afford to chip an edge.

### Inclusions and natural imperfection

Hold the stone up to a bright lamp or flashlight. Real rose quartz shows natural inclusions: fine fibrous veils, cloudy zones, feathers, or needle-like rutile threads. The distribution is irregular. A completely flawless, uniformly translucent pink specimen with no internal texture at all is more likely to be glass. Bubbles, which are round or slightly elongated voids, are the diagnostic sign of glass — quartz does not form bubbles.

### Color concentration

Dyed imitations (often dyed quartzite, agate, or howlite) show color that concentrates at fracture lines, grain boundaries, or surface cracks rather than distributing evenly through the crystal. Rotate the stone slowly under bright light and look for pink that pools at visible cracks. Natural rose quartz color is a structural property of the whole crystal, not a stain.

## Professional Tests

A gemologist with a refractometer can measure refractive index. Rose Quartz has RI values of approximately 1.544–1.553 (typical quartz range), distinguishing it from glass (RI ~1.5, single value, isotropic) and synthetic materials with different RI signatures. Specific gravity of ~2.65 is also measurable in a lab.

If you are buying an expensive specimen or a faceted stone for jewelry, professional verification is worthwhile. For guidance on lab-created versus natural material more broadly, see [lab-created vs natural gemstones](/compare/lab-created-vs-natural-gemstones).

## Common Imitations and What Distinguishes Them

| Imitation | How to spot it |
|---|---|
| Pink glass | Bubbles visible under magnification; warms quickly; uniform color throughout; no silk or rutile |
| Dyed quartzite | Color concentrated at grain boundaries and fracture lines; may fade with water or acetone |
| Dyed howlite | Much softer (Mohs ~3.5); chalky surface; won't scratch glass |
| Synthetic rose quartz | Rare; near-flawless; professional RI test distinguishes |
| Plastic | Very light for its size; scratches easily; warms immediately |

## Buying Safely Online

Most rose quartz sold for crystal practice is genuine, but the material is inexpensive enough that imitations occur in the low-end market. Signs of a reputable seller include stone origin disclosure (Brazil, Madagascar, India are the main sources), a return policy, and honest grade labeling.
