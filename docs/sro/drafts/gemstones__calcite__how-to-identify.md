# How to Identify Calcite and Spot Imitations

Real calcite can be identified at home with three tests: a hardness scratch test (Mohs 3 — a copper coin barely scratches it, a steel knife blade does easily), an acid test (a drop of vinegar on the surface causes visible fizzing), and observation of cleavage (broken pieces form flat rhombohedral faces, not curved conchoidal fractures). Transparent specimens show double refraction — a doubled image under a flat piece. Each test is explained below.

## Why Identification Matters for Calcite

Calcite is one of the most common minerals on Earth, which means genuine material is inexpensive. The primary identification problem is not that sellers deliberately pass off glass as calcite — it is more often that colored varieties are misidentified, mislabeled, or artificially dyed to look like rarer or more popular colors. Understanding calcite's distinctive physical properties makes it easy to verify authenticity at home without specialized equipment.

For how price relates to authenticity, see [calcite price guide](/gemstones/calcite/price-guide). For the broader question of online crystal authenticity, [are online crystals real](/learn/are-online-crystals-real) covers the topic.

## The Hardness Test

Calcite defines Mohs 3. In practice:

1. Find a copper coin (a penny or equivalent). Its hardness is approximately Mohs 3–3.5.
2. Press the edge of the coin firmly across an inconspicuous part of the calcite surface and drag.
3. A genuine calcite specimen will show a faint scratch mark. The coin may also leave a copper-colored streak — wipe this away with a damp cloth to see whether it is a streak from the coin or a genuine scratch in the stone.
4. A steel knife blade or nail file (Mohs ~5.5) will scratch calcite easily.

What this rules out: Glass (Mohs ~5.5) will not be scratched by a copper coin. If you cannot scratch the specimen with a coin and a knife cannot scratch it easily, the specimen is not calcite. Quartz (Mohs 7) will resist the coin entirely.

What this does not rule out: Gypsum (Mohs 2) is softer than calcite and can be scratched by a fingernail. Selenite (a form of gypsum) is sometimes sold alongside calcite and looks superficially similar in white or pale forms. Selenite will scratch with a fingernail; calcite will not.

## The Acid Test

Calcite reacts immediately with dilute acids:

1. Use white vinegar (dilute acetic acid) or, if available, dilute hydrochloric acid.
2. Apply one small drop to the calcite surface.
3. Genuine calcite will produce visible bubbling (carbon dioxide gas) within seconds.

The reaction: CaCO3 + 2H⁺ → Ca²⁺ + H₂O + CO₂. This is the standard field identification test for all carbonate minerals.

What this rules out: Glass, dyed quartz, and most silicate minerals do not react with vinegar. If the specimen shows no reaction, it is not calcite.

What this does not rule out: Aragonite and dolomite are also carbonate minerals that react with acid. Aragonite effervesces as readily as calcite; dolomite requires powdering before it reacts noticeably. In practice, calcite sold in the crystal market can be confirmed as calcite (rather than one of its carbonate relatives) if the other tests also fit.

After the test, rinse and dry the surface. Do not leave acid sitting on a specimen.

## The Cleavage Test

Calcite has perfect rhombohedral cleavage in three directions. When a piece of calcite breaks, it produces flat, parallelogram-shaped faces rather than the curved, shell-like surfaces of quartz or glass.

You do not need to break a piece to look for this: examine any existing chips, edges, or broken surfaces. Flat, angled break faces suggest cleavage rather than fracture. If the stone is a tumbled piece with rounded surfaces, this test is less useful — but raw or rough specimens show cleavage clearly.

## The Double Refraction Test (Transparent Varieties Only)

Colorless or pale transparent calcite — often called Iceland Spar — shows one of the strongest double refractions of any common mineral:

1. Place a flat, transparent calcite piece over a printed line or dot.
2. Look through the specimen from above.
3. A genuine calcite piece will show two distinct, separated images of the line or dot.

Glass and quartz do not produce this effect. This test is primarily useful for clear or very pale translucent specimens.

## Common Imitations and Mislabelings

| Material | How it resembles calcite | How to tell apart |
|---|---|---|
| Glass | Smooth, often colored, sold cheaply | No acid reaction; harder (Mohs ~5.5); no cleavage |
| Dyed quartz | Vivid or uniform color, feels similar in weight | No acid reaction; harder (Mohs 7); no cleavage |
| Selenite / gypsum | White or pale, soft | Even softer (Mohs 2 — fingernail scratches it); no acid reaction |
| Aragonite | Structurally similar carbonate | Acid test passes; harder to distinguish without lab equipment |
| Dyed calcite | Genuine calcite but artificially colored | Acid test and hardness pass; look for unnatural color uniformity, color concentrated near surface |

Artificially dyed calcite is the most common issue in the crystal market for colored varieties. Deep, unusually uniform color — particularly in blue, green, or vivid orange pieces — may indicate dye. Rinsing a suspect piece in water may cause light bleeding of color. The [calcite meaning page](/gemstones/calcite/meaning) describes the natural color range to help set expectations. For lab-created vs natural distinctions, see [lab-created vs natural gemstones](/compare/lab-created-vs-natural-gemstones).

## Where to Buy with Confidence

Sellers who provide mineral locality information (country, mine, or region of origin), who label color treatments honestly, and who allow returns are lower-risk sources. Calcite is inexpensive; very low prices are not automatically a warning sign, but unusually vivid color at very low prices warrants a closer look.
