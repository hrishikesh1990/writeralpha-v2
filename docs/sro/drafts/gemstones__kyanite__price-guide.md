# Kyanite Price Guide — What It Costs and Why

Faceted blue kyanite gem material costs approximately $37–$57 per carat at 1 carat weight (VVS clarity, excellent cut), rising to $89–$122 per carat at 5 carats, based on retail market data from April 2026. Tumbled stones sell for roughly $3–$8 each at retail. Raw specimens range from a few dollars for small pieces to $20–$50 for display-quality bladed crystals. Kyanite is generally affordable compared with sapphire, which it can visually resemble.

## What Drives Kyanite's Price

Kyanite pricing is shaped by the same four factors as other colored gemstones, with one additional complication unique to this mineral.

**Color** is the largest single driver. The most valued blue kyanite is a vivid, medium-to-deep blue resembling sapphire, with good saturation and even distribution. Pale or grey-tinged blue trades at a discount. Color zoning — darker at the center, fading toward the edges — is normal for kyanite and, in moderate amounts, does not necessarily reduce value, but heavy zoning into pale or colorless areas does.

**Clarity** matters significantly. Kyanite typically contains inclusions (needle-like growth tubes, fractures along cleavage planes). Eye-clean stones at 1 carat or above are rarer and command a premium. Heavily included stones used for crystal specimens rather than faceted jewelry are sold at specimen prices, not gem prices.

**Cut** is unusually important for kyanite. Its anisotropic hardness — Mohs 4.5 along the blade, 6.5–7 across it — makes cutting difficult: the lapidary must orient the stone to protect the soft axis while maximizing color. A well-oriented, well-cut kyanite is more expensive than a poorly cut stone of similar weight. See [the Mohs hardness scale](/learn/mohs-hardness-scale) for context on how hardness affects cutting and durability.

**Carat weight** scales price per carat upward at larger sizes. Eye-clean, well-colored kyanite above 5 carats is uncommon, which is why price-per-carat rises significantly at that threshold.

**Origin** adds a small premium for Nepalese and Kenyan kyanite, which can show particularly vivid color. Brazilian material, which supplies most of the specimen and tumbled-stone market, is generally priced lower.

## How to Avoid Overpaying

**For faceted gems**: Use an independent price reference such as Gemval before buying. Prices above the reference range may be justified by exceptional color, but ask the seller to explain. Prices dramatically below the reference range for a supposedly high-quality stone are a signal to verify authenticity — see [how to identify kyanite](/gemstones/kyanite/how-to-identify).

**For tumbled stones**: Compare prices per piece rather than per lot. Bulk rates of $3 or less per tumbled kyanite are normal from wholesale-adjacent suppliers; single-piece retail prices above $15 per tumbled stone for a standard palm-size piece are on the high side unless the piece is exceptionally large or gem-quality.

**For specimens**: Price is highly individual and condition-dependent. A clearly documented origin (Brazil, Nepal) from a reputable seller matters more than any price heuristic.

**Watch out for**: Dyed blue quartz or dyed howlite sold as kyanite, particularly in low-price internet listings where photographs are the only description. Genuine kyanite in the specimen market is not especially expensive, so a very low price alone is not evidence of fraud — it is the combination of low price, vague origin, and uniformly vivid color that warrants caution.

## Treatments

Kyanite is not commonly heat-treated or irradiated. Dyed kyanite exists but is less common than dyed substitutes (quartz, howlite) being sold as kyanite. Reputable dealers disclose treatments. For ethical sourcing considerations, see [ethical crystal sourcing](/learn/ethical-crystal-sourcing).
