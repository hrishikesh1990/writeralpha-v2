# Can Emerald Go in Water? What You Need to Know

Emerald can tolerate brief contact with water — a quick rinse or gentle wash with mild soap is the GIA's recommended cleaning method. Prolonged soaking is not recommended, because approximately 90 percent of natural emeralds are fracture-filled with oil or resin, and extended water exposure can penetrate those fillings, causing cloudiness or deterioration of the treatment.

## Why Hardness Is Not the Only Factor

Emerald has a Mohs hardness of 7.5 to 8, which means water alone will not scratch or dissolve it. On the Mohs scale, a mineral rated above 7 is generally considered water-stable in terms of surface abrasion.

However, hardness does not tell the whole story for emerald. The GIA estimates that 90 percent or more of fashioned natural emeralds have been treated — most commonly with cedar oil, synthetic resin, or a substance such as Opticon — to reduce the visibility of internal fractures. These materials fill surface-reaching fissures and improve apparent clarity. When water infiltrates those same fissures during prolonged soaking, it can displace or cloud the filling, permanently changing how the stone looks.

The conclusion: brief water exposure for cleaning is acceptable. Sustained soaking, hot water, and water combined with harsh chemicals are not.

## What Is Safe with Water

| Method | Safe? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Quick rinse under lukewarm water | Yes | Keep the temperature moderate |
| Mild soapy water + soft brush | Yes | GIA-recommended cleaning method |
| Soaking for 10+ minutes | No | Water penetrates fractures, risks clouding oil treatment |
| Hot water | No | Temperature shock can extend fractures and soften resin |
| Salt water | No | Salt is more chemically aggressive; risks treatment and metal settings |
| Ultrasonic cleaner (uses water) | No | Vibration dislodges fracture fillings; GIA advises against it |
| Steam cleaner | No | Heat causes oil to sweat out of fractures |

## Salt Water and Emerald

Salt water is more aggressive toward emerald than plain water for two reasons. First, dissolved salts are mildly acidic and can interact with fracture-filling resins and oils over time. Second, salt residue left behind after the water evaporates can be hygroscopic, drawing additional moisture into surface fractures. Salt-water baths should not be used with emerald. For guidance on which stones tolerate salt, see [salt-safe crystals](/learn/salt-safe-crystals).

## Sun Drying After Water Cleaning

After rinsing emerald, pat it dry with a lint-free cloth and allow it to air-dry fully before storage. Avoid placing it in direct strong sunlight to dry: while brief sun exposure causes no immediate harm to the stone itself, prolonged UV exposure may affect oil treatments over time. For information on sun charging after washing, see [sun-safe crystals](/learn/sun-safe-crystals).

## How to Clean Emerald with Water

1. Use lukewarm (not hot) water.
2. Add a small amount of mild dish soap.
3. Use a soft-bristled brush to gently scrub the stone and setting.
4. Rinse under lukewarm running water.
5. Pat dry immediately with a soft, lint-free cloth.
6. Allow to air-dry fully before storage or wear.

Avoid any commercial jewelry cleaners that contain ammonia, bleach, or alcohol, as these can alter fracture-filling materials.
