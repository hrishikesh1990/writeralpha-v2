# Blue Sandstone Price Guide

Blue Sandstone (aventurine glass, blue goldstone) is an affordable manufactured glass product. Retail prices checked in September 2026 show tumbled stones at roughly $1–3 USD each, bead strands at $5–10 USD, and simple bracelets at $5–15 USD. Spheres and carvings rarely exceed $30–50 USD. Rarity does not drive the price — the material is manufactured and widely available.

## Why Blue Sandstone Is Priced as a Manufactured Material

Blue Sandstone is not a natural mineral. It is aventurine glass — a silica-based glass made by melting copper-bearing compounds under controlled conditions to produce metallic copper inclusions, colored blue with cobalt. Because it is manufactured, its supply is not limited by geological scarcity. Price is driven instead by labor, quality of cut and polish, batch consistency, and seller margin.

The [Blue Sandstone hub page](/gemstones/blue-sandstone) covers physical properties including hardness (~5.5 on the Mohs scale — see the [Mohs hardness scale guide](/learn/mohs-hardness-scale) for context) and specific gravity (2.5–2.8). At hardness 5.5, Blue Sandstone is moderately durable for jewelry but softer than quartz, which affects its long-term surface condition and therefore secondhand value.

Wholesale prices are lower: 1 kg of tumbled stones (roughly 100–150 pieces at 10–20 mm) lists at an MSRP of approximately $30 USD from wholesalers, implying a retail margin of 2–4x typical in the crystal trade.

## What Drives the Price

**Sparkle density and uniformity.** The most valuable Blue Sandstone comes from the center of a production batch, where copper crystals grow largest and most evenly. Pieces from the outer zones of a batch have smaller, duller inclusions or areas with no sparkle at all. Bright, even aventurescence across the whole piece commands a premium.

**Cut quality.** Skilled cabochon cutting and polishing adds labor cost. A well-domed cabochon that maximizes the sparkle from the copper inclusions is worth more than a flat-ground or poorly centered piece.

**Size.** Larger tumbled stones, spheres, and carvings cost more because they require larger, higher-quality sections of rough and more cutting time.

**Seller and market channel.** Crystal boutiques, jewelry shops, and online marketplaces with strong brand positioning charge more than bulk wholesale suppliers. The underlying material cost is the same.

## How to Avoid Overpaying

Blue Sandstone should be affordable. If a piece is priced at the level of natural semi-precious gemstones (above ~$50 for a simple tumbled stone, for example), the price is not justified by the material. The stone is manufactured glass, not a rare mineral.

The most common overpayment scenario is buying Blue Sandstone described vaguely as a "natural crystal" or a "rare blue gemstone" without disclosure that it is man-made glass. Check that the seller discloses the man-made nature of the material — this is required under gem-trade standards. The [how to identify Blue Sandstone guide](/gemstones/blue-sandstone/how-to-identify) explains how to verify the material yourself before buying.

For guidance on ethical sourcing and fair pricing across the crystal market, see [ethical crystal sourcing](/learn/ethical-crystal-sourcing).

## Resale and Secondhand Value

Blue Sandstone does not hold or gain value as an investment. It is a manufactured glass product available in large quantities from industrial producers. Secondhand pieces sell at or below original retail price and typically only command more if the craftsmanship (setting, carving) adds value. Buy it because you want to wear or use it, not as a store of value.
