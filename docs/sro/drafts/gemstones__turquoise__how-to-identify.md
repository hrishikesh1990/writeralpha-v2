# How to Identify Real Turquoise and Spot Fakes

Real turquoise has a Mohs hardness of 5 to 6, waxy to sub-vitreous surface lustre, natural color variation, and irregular matrix veining that runs through the stone. The most reliable at-home test is the acetone swab: genuine turquoise does not release color; dyed imitations do. Common substitutes include dyed howlite, dyed magnesite, plastic, and glass — all identifiable with the checks below.

## Why Turquoise Is So Frequently Faked

Turquoise is one of the most faked gemstones in the retail market. The reasons are structural: fine untreated natural turquoise commands high prices ($50 to $300+ per carat for premium grades), while howlite — a white mineral that accepts blue dye convincingly — costs a fraction of that. Magnesite and calcite are similarly cheap and similarly dyeable. Reconstituted turquoise, made from powdered genuine turquoise material bonded with resin and often dyed, adds a further category that blurs the line between "real" and "fake."

The result is that a high proportion of stones sold as "turquoise" in tourist markets, online retail, and budget jewelry ranges are not what they appear. Understanding the physical tests helps whether you are buying or assessing a stone already in your collection. ## The Common Imitations

| Material | What it looks like | Key tell |
|---|---|---|
| Dyed howlite | Very convincing blue-green; natural grey veining mimics matrix | Acetone releases blue dye; veining too regular |
| Dyed magnesite | Similar to dyed howlite; slightly softer (Mohs 3.5–4.5) | Fingernail can sometimes scratch it |
| Plastic | Can look flawless; no matrix | Lightweight for size; warm to touch; bubbles under loupe |
| Glass | Uniform color and high clarity | Very smooth surface; no natural variation; cold and heavy |
| Reconstituted turquoise | Uses genuine turquoise powder with resin binder | Perfectly repeating matrix pattern; acetone may release dye if color was added |

## Step-by-Step Identification

### 1. Examine color under good light

Move the stone under natural daylight or a neutral white LED. Genuine turquoise almost never displays completely uniform color across the whole surface. Look for subtle mottling — lighter and darker patches within the same cabochon — and for color that shifts slightly at different angles. Dyed howlite tends to look flat, uniformly saturated, and slightly too blue, because the dye sits in surface pores rather than running through the mineral structure.

### 2. Inspect the matrix veining with a loupe

Matrix is the network of host rock — usually brown, black, or gray — that remains bonded to turquoise from its formation. Use a 10x loupe or a phone camera at maximum zoom. In natural turquoise, matrix lines are irregular: varying in width, stopping and starting unpredictably, and integrating with color variation in the stone around them. In dyed howlite, the underlying mineral has its own grey veining, which tends to be more regular and does not interact naturally with color variation. Completely vein-free stones are not necessarily higher quality — a clean, vein-free turquoise does exist (particularly from the old Sleeping Beauty mine in Arizona), but a vein-free blue stone sold cheaply is more often glass or dyed howlite than exceptional natural material.

### 3. Run the acetone swab test

This test eliminates most dyed imitations:

1. Dip a cotton swab in acetone (standard nail-polish remover works).
2. Rub the swab gently on an inconspicuous area of the stone for 20 to 30 seconds.
3. Inspect the swab.

**Clean swab:** The color did not transfer — consistent with genuine turquoise (natural or stabilized).
**Blue color on swab:** The stone is dyed — dyed howlite or magnesite.

Caution: test on a loose stone or a discreet, unset area. Do not test the edge near a metal setting, where residual wax or polish may produce a false result. Do not use this test on stones you do not own.

### 4. Check hardness

Turquoise sits at Mohs 5 to 6 — similar to glass (~5.5). A steel knife blade will just barely scratch it; a copper coin (Mohs ~3) should not. Magnesite, a common substitute, is softer at Mohs 3.5 to 4.5 and can often be marked with a fingernail (Mohs 2.5) or a copper coin. Only perform this test on loose stones or in an area that can accept a minor scratch; never on set or antique pieces.

### 5. Weigh the stone against its size

Turquoise has a specific gravity of 2.6 to 2.8. Plastic imitations feel noticeably light for their size — lighter than the equivalent volume of genuine stone. Glass tends to feel heavier. You do not need a precision scale; comparing a known real stone of similar size against the suspect piece by hand will usually reveal significant differences.

### 6. Check temperature

Natural minerals feel cold initially when held and warm slowly to body temperature. Plastic warms immediately. This is a rough test, not definitive, but a stone that reaches hand temperature in seconds and stays warm is likely plastic or resin.

## What Genuine Turquoise Cannot Tell You About Treatment

All the tests above help separate turquoise from non-turquoise. They do not confirm whether natural turquoise has been stabilized, waxed, dyed, or enhanced.
