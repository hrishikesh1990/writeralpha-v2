# How to Identify Real Kyanite — Tests and Visual Guide

Real kyanite is identified by its combination of bladed crystal habit, anisotropic hardness, and vitreous luster. The single most reliable test is directional hardness: kyanite is distinctly softer along the blade length (Mohs 4.5) than across it (Mohs 6.5–7). No common imitation shares this property. Common substitutes in the trade include dyed blue quartz and glass.

## Step 1: Examine the Crystal Form

Natural kyanite grows in elongated, bladed or prismatic crystals. Raw specimens have a characteristic striated, flat appearance — they look like blue-grey blades, sometimes grouped in fan formations or embedded in quartz or mica schist matrix. The color in genuine blue kyanite tends to be patchy or zoned: deeper blue at the core, often fading to pale blue-grey or colorless at the edges. This uneven color distribution is caused by variable concentrations of iron and titanium in the crystal lattice.

Red flags in a specimen sold as raw kyanite:
- Uniform, saturated color throughout with no zoning
- Rounded, non-bladed form (though tumbled kyanite is an exception)
- No visible crystal structure, striations, or matrix

For what genuine kyanite looks like in detail, see [kyanite meaning and appearance](/gemstones/kyanite/meaning).

## Step 2: Test Directional Hardness

Directional hardness testing works on raw or rough specimens only — it will damage polished pieces.

1. Obtain a steel nail or steel blade (Mohs approximately 5.5) and a piece of quartz (Mohs 7).
2. Attempt to scratch the surface of the suspected kyanite **along** the blade length.
3. Then attempt to scratch **across** the blade width.

**Genuine kyanite result**: The steel scratches the stone easily along the blade (Mohs 4.5), but the stone resists or only barely yields across the blade (Mohs 6.5–7). This directional difference is the single most diagnostic test for kyanite.

**Fake/imitation result**: A steel blade will not scratch glass (Mohs 5.5) easily in either direction (glass is harder). Dyed blue quartz will resist the steel in both directions (quartz is Mohs 7 throughout). Dyed howlite or magnesite will be soft in all directions (Mohs 3.5).

## Step 3: Look for Perfect Cleavage

Kyanite has perfect cleavage parallel to the blade face. If a specimen shows a clean, flat break along its length — rather than a conchoidal (shell-like) fracture like glass — that is consistent with genuine kyanite. Glass breaks conchoidally; dyed quartz also breaks conchoidally.

## Step 4: Check Luster and Transparency

Genuine kyanite has a vitreous (glass-like) to slightly pearly luster on cleavage faces. It is translucent to transparent in thin sections but often appears semi-opaque in thick specimens due to inclusions. Look at the stone at an angle in natural light: it should have a subtle depth and internal structure, not the flat, uniform appearance of dyed glass.

## Common Imitations and How to Spot Them

| Material | What it looks like | How to tell it apart |
|---|---|---|
| Dyed blue quartz | Even blue color, glassy | Uniform hardness throughout (Mohs 7); no directional difference |
| Blue glass | Vivid, even blue; may have bubbles | Conchoidal fracture; uniform hardness; no crystal banding |
| Dyed howlite | Pale blue with grey veining | Very soft (Mohs 3.5); steel scratches easily in all directions |
| Dyed magnesite | Similar to howlite | Soft throughout; often looks chalky |
| Sodalite | Blue, opaque, with white veining | Heavier feel (SG ~2.27); no directional hardness; cubic crystal habit |
| Blue lapis lazuli | Deep blue with gold pyrite flecks | No cleavage blades; pyrite specks visible; heavier |

## Faceted Gemstone Identification

For cut and polished kyanite used in jewelry, directional hardness testing is not practical. In this case, consult a gemologist with a refractometer (refractive index for kyanite: 1.71–1.73, biaxial) and specific gravity measurement (3.53–3.67). These values are distinctive and not easily mimicked by glass or common imitations. For what faceted kyanite costs and what price bands suggest authenticity, see [kyanite price guide](/gemstones/kyanite/price-guide).

Lab-grown kyanite is not commercially produced as a gem material; there is no significant lab-grown market to be aware of. For the broader question of lab-created versus natural gemstones, see [lab-created vs natural gemstones](/compare/lab-created-vs-natural-gemstones).

## Buying Tips

- For raw specimens: buy from sellers who show the matrix. Genuine kyanite in Brazil commonly occurs in quartz-mica schist matrix; a specimen floating in isolation with no matrix and uniform color deserves closer inspection.
- For tumbled stones: color uniformity is expected after tumbling, so rely on the seller's reputation and, where possible, a hardness test on the back of the piece.
- For jewelry: ask for a gem lab report or GIA identification on significant purchases. See [are online crystals real](/learn/are-online-crystals-real) for a general guide to sourcing.
