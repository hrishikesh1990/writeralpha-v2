# How to Identify Real Green Opal

Real Green Opal is a common (non-precious) variety of amorphous hydrated silica with a distinctive waxy to vitreous luster, no play of color, and a green tint from trace nickel or chlorite inclusions. The most common imitations are dyed green quartz or chalcedony, green glass, and plastics. A few simple checks identify genuine material in most cases; gemological confirmation settles ambiguous specimens.

## What Genuine Green Opal Looks Like

[Green Opal](/gemstones/green-opal) is a mineraloid — amorphous (non-crystalline) hydrated silica (SiO2·nH2O). Its key visual and physical characteristics:

- **Luster**: Waxy to subvitreous when polished. Not the high gloss of glass, not the silky sheen of serpentine.
- **Color**: Ranges from pale mint to deep apple green, olive, or moss green. The color is typically even in good-quality pieces but may show subtle variation, banding, or internal mottling. Some specimens show dendritic (plant-like) inclusions.
- **No play of color**: Green Opal is common opal, not precious opal. It does not flash rainbow colors when tilted. A green stone that shows a rainbow play of color is a different material — possibly a precious opal with a green body color.
- **Translucency**: Ranges from opaque to translucent. Many tumbled pieces are semi-translucent at thin edges when held to light.
- **Hardness**: 5.5 to 6 on the Mohs scale. A steel knife (about 5.5) will scratch it with moderate pressure; a piece of quartz (7) scratches it easily.
- **Feel**: Slightly cool to the touch initially (as with most stones vs. glass), though this distinction fades quickly and is unreliable as a sole test.

## What the Green Color Means

The green in genuine Green Opal comes from one of two mechanisms, depending on origin. In Tanzanian and some Peruvian material, trace nickel substitutes into silicate nanostructures, producing a vivid apple green — the same mechanism that colors garnierite. In specimens from Madagascar and Oregon, the green comes from suspended chlorite or celadonite micro-inclusions, iron-magnesium sheet silicates within the opal matrix. The green is natural, not applied. This internal origin of color is one of the key distinctions from dyed imitations.

## Common Imitations and How to Distinguish Them

### Dyed green quartz or chalcedony

Dyed material is the most common substitution in the budget crystal market. Signs of dyeing:

- Color concentrated in surface cracks and fractures, looking artificially saturated at edges
- Uniform, intense color with no natural variation or mottling
- When soaked in acetone briefly and the liquid tested, genuine material releases no color; dyed stone may bleed slightly

Quartz and chalcedony are harder than Green Opal (Mohs 7 vs. 5.5–6), so they will not be scratched by a piece of quartz as opal would be. This hardness difference is a reliable distinguishing test if you have a piece of known quartz.

### Green glass

Glass imitations are often heavier-feeling due to higher specific gravity, show perfectly uniform color with no internal structure, and frequently contain tiny round bubbles visible under magnification. Glass has a conchoidal fracture similar to opal but a glassier, higher luster on polished surfaces. A loupe (10x magnification) is sufficient to see bubbles in most glass fakes.

### Plastic

Plastic is warmer to the touch and does not sustain the initial coolness of genuine stone. It scratches easily with a fingernail (Mohs 2–2.5), far more easily than Green Opal. Under a loupe, plastic often shows flow lines or marks from the molding process.

### Synthetic opal (Gilson)

Synthetic precious opal (Gilson type) is manufactured and sold legitimately with disclosure. It shows an exaggerated, regular play of color unlike natural opal. For green material in the common-opal category, synthetic common green opal is less widely documented in the crystal market than the precious variety, but the same principle applies: disclose is required in ethical trade. For the broader question of lab-created versus natural stones, see [lab-created vs. natural gemstones](/compare/lab-created-vs-natural-gemstones).

## Practical Tests at Home

1. **Loupe inspection (10x)**: Check for bubbles (glass), uniform suspicious color (dye), or flow marks (plastic).
2. **Hardness test**: On an inconspicuous edge, try scratching with a steel knife blade (Mohs 5.5). Genuine Green Opal can be scratched by steel with some effort. Harder imitations (quartz/chalcedony) resist this.
3. **Light test**: Hold the specimen to a bright light. Genuine translucent Green Opal shows subtle internal color variation; dyed or glass material tends to look uniform.
4. **Specific gravity**: Green Opal has a specific gravity of approximately 2.0 to 2.2. This requires water displacement measurement — practical with precise equipment, not by hand.

## When to Consult a Gemologist

Home tests identify the most obvious imitations. For significant purchases — faceted collector pieces, high-value cabochons — a gemological lab can confirm identity through refractive index measurement (opal reads approximately 1.37 to 1.47), specific gravity, and spectroscopy. The GIA and similar accredited labs issue identification reports that provide reliable confirmation.
