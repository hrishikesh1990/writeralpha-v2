# How to Identify Real Larimar vs Fakes and Imitations

Real Larimar is blue pectolite from a single commercial source in the Dominican Republic. It shows an irregular cloud-like or feathered pattern of blue and white, has a Mohs hardness of 4.5–5, and a specific gravity of 2.74–2.90. Common imitations include dyed howlite, blue calcite, dyed agate, ceramic, glass, and resin. No single visual test is definitive; the most reliable assessments combine pattern inspection, hardness, density, and — for high-value pieces — gemological testing.

## What Real Larimar Looks Like

Larimar is a blue variety of pectolite (NaCa₂Si₃O₈(OH)) formed in volcanic rock cavities in the Barahona Province of the Dominican Republic. Its color ranges from pale sky blue through medium blue to deep ocean blue, frequently interrupted by white fibrous, cloud-like, or radial structures. These white patterns arise from the mineral's fibrous crystal aggregate and are an intrinsic part of authentic material.

Key visual markers of genuine Larimar:

- **Irregular pattern.** The blue and white zones intersect in irregular, non-repeating shapes — described variously as clouds, ocean surface, or dendritic. No two authentic pieces are identical.
- **Hematite inclusions.** Small red-brown spots or streaks of hematite within the stone are common in genuine Larimar and serve as an authenticity marker. They are not defects.
- **Fibrous texture.** Under a 10× loupe, genuine Larimar shows a silky or fibrous internal texture from the aggregate pectolite structure. This differs from the smooth uniform interior of glass or the granular interior of ceramic.
- **Translucency at thin edges.** Many genuine pieces transmit light at thin points, showing the internal pattern extending through the depth of the stone.
- **Opaque to translucent.** Larimar is not transparent in facetable form. Anything with gem-quality transparency through its body is not pectolite.

## Common Imitations and How to Distinguish Them

### Dyed howlite and magnesite

Howlite is naturally white with grey veining and is one of the most common materials dyed to imitate Larimar. The key difference is the pattern: dyed howlite shows grey veining that follows the original howlite structure — typically a web of fine cracks rather than the cloudy fibrous pattern of pectolite. The blue dye is often concentrated in the veins and may transfer faintly to a white cotton swab wiped across the surface. Under UV light, dyed howlite may show different fluorescence than genuine Larimar.

### Dyed blue agate and chalcedony

Agate is Mohs 6.5–7, significantly harder than Larimar. A steel knife blade (approximately Mohs 5.5) will scratch genuine Larimar but will not mark agate. If the stone resists scratching by a steel blade on an inconspicuous back edge, it is not Larimar. Agate also shows concentric or parallel banding following its chalcedony structure rather than the cloud-like pectolite pattern.

### Blue calcite and Caribbean calcite

Blue calcite is Mohs 3 — noticeably softer than Larimar. A copper coin (Mohs 3) will scratch blue calcite but should not mark genuine Larimar. Caribbean calcite (a blue calcite and aragonite combination from Pakistan) is also commonly mistaken for Larimar. It is matte or chalky in appearance and lacks the silky fibrous structure of pectolite.

### Glass

Glass imitations may incorporate white swirls to mimic the Larimar pattern. Under a loupe, look for round gas bubbles, curved flow lines, and mold seams — none of which appear in natural pectolite aggregate. Glass is also typically heavier or lighter than genuine Larimar depending on composition, and its surface feels distinctly glassy rather than silky.

### Ceramic and resin

Ceramic imitations can reproduce an opaque blue-and-white pattern effectively. Common giveaways include painted or suspended white patterns that do not continue through the back and edges, repeated identical patterns across multiple beads, and a manufactured uniformity absent from natural stone. Resin may show casting bubbles, mold seams, or polymer translucency between apparent stone fragments.

## At-Home Checks

At-home tests help narrow down the possibilities but are not conclusive on their own. For high-value purchases, gemological laboratory testing is the reliable final step.

**1. Pattern inspection (loupe).** Examine the stone at 10× magnification. Look for fibrous spheroidal texture in the blue areas and mineral boundaries (not paint or concentration of dye) defining the white zones. Check whether the pattern continues at the edges and, if possible, the back. Drill holes are particularly informative: dye or resin concentrates there in imitations.

**2. Hardness test.** On an inconspicuous edge, attempt to scratch the stone with a copper coin (Mohs 3) and a steel pocket knife (approximately Mohs 5.5). Genuine Larimar at Mohs 4.5–5 should resist the copper coin but be scratched by the steel knife. If the steel knife makes no mark, the material is harder than Larimar and is likely agate, chalcedony, or glass.

**3. Streak test.** Drag the stone across an unglazed ceramic tile. Genuine Larimar leaves a white streak. A colored streak indicates a dyed stone or a different mineral.

**4. Weight (specific gravity).** Larimar has a specific gravity of 2.74–2.90. Resin and plastic are noticeably lighter for their size. Glass varies but often feels denser. While a hydrostatic scale provides a precise result, familiarity with handling multiple genuine pieces develops an informal feel for expected weight.

**5. Origin claim.** All commercial Larimar originates from a single mining area in the Barahona Province, Dominican Republic. A seller who claims Larimar from any other country is describing either a different material or a mislabeled stone.

## What Visual Inspection Cannot Establish Alone

Color and pattern are screening clues, not proof. A high-quality ceramic imitation can reproduce a convincing blue-and-white cloud pattern. Conversely, a low-pattern genuine Larimar may look comparatively uniform. The absence of dramatic white webbing does not automatically prove imitation.

Gemological confirmation uses refractive index (RI approximately 1.59–1.65 for Larimar), hydrostatic specific gravity (2.74–2.90), and Raman spectroscopy, which can identify pectolite definitively and distinguish it from calcite, howlite, chalcedony, and manufactured imitations. For any piece above a few hundred dollars, a laboratory report provides certainty that no visual test can.
