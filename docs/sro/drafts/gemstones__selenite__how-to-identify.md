# How to Identify Real Selenite — Tests and Visual Checks

Real selenite is a gypsum variety (CaSO4·2H2O) with Mohs 2.0 hardness. The most reliable quick test: a fingernail scratches genuine selenite. Glass (Mohs ~5.5), plastic, and most hard-stone imitations cannot be scratched this way, but they will scratch selenite. These physical properties make selenite relatively straightforward to verify at home.

## The Core Tests

### Fingernail scratch test

Press your fingernail firmly across an inconspicuous flat surface of the piece. Genuine selenite will show a visible scratch mark. If your nail slides off without marking the stone, it is almost certainly not selenite (or it has a very thick coating or resin finish). This is the single most useful test because selenite's Mohs 2.0 hardness is far below most imitation materials.

**Note:** Your nail may leave a powdery line on some soft stones and then wipe away. Blow away the powder and look for a true groove in the surface, not just residue.

### Cleavage and surface texture

Selenite has perfect cleavage in one direction. Break or chip a piece (if acceptable) and observe the fracture surface: it will be flat and step-like, not conchoidal (shell-shaped as in glass) or irregular. Natural selenite also has a pearly to vitreous luster on fresh cleavage faces. Glass fractures conchoidally.

### Luster and appearance

Selenite and its common relative satin spar have distinctive appearances:

| Variety | Appearance |
|---|---|
| Selenite (transparent) | Clear, glassy; large flat crystals; moon-like reflections |
| Satin spar (fibrous) | White to cream; silky, chatoyant (cat's-eye sheen); fibrous cross-section |
| Alabaster (massive) | Fine-grained; matte; smooth; opaque white to cream |

Most retail "selenite wands" are actually satin spar. Both are real gypsum and both are genuine products; the naming overlap is simply a trade convention.

### Temperature (cool to touch)

Genuine mineral pieces feel cool to the touch and warm slowly in the hand. Plastic imitations warm more quickly and often feel lighter and less dense. Resin fakes may feel warmer immediately.

### Water dissolution test

Place a small, expendable piece in warm water for 30 minutes. Real selenite will show surface pitting and a chalky deposit in the water (dissolved calcium sulfate). Glass and plastic will be unaffected. **Do not use this test on a piece you want to keep** — the damage is cosmetic but real.

### Weight

Gypsum has a specific gravity of approximately 2.3. A selenite piece should feel moderately substantial for its size — not as heavy as quartz or calcite, and noticeably heavier and denser than plastic or thin resin castings.

## Common Imitations and How to Distinguish Them

| Imitation | Key difference from selenite |
|---|---|
| Glass | Much harder (Mohs 5.5+); conchoidal fracture; no cleavage; feels warmer; heavier |
| Clear acrylic/plastic | Lighter; warms in hand quickly; nail does not scratch in the same way (can dent rather than scratch); burns with sharp smell |
| Calcite | Harder (Mohs 3); reacts to dilute acid (fizzes); different fracture habit |
| Quartz | Much harder (Mohs 7); will not be scratched by a nail; conchoidal fracture |
| Dyed alabaster | Same base mineral (gypsum), just colored; genuine gypsum, but color is added |

## Treatments to Know About

Selenite is not typically treated in the way colored gemstones are treated (heating, filling, coating). The main concerns are:

**Dyed material.** Alabaster (massive gypsum) is sometimes dyed in bright colors. Rubbing with a damp cloth on an inconspicuous area will lift surface dye in many cases. Natural selenite color comes from trace mineral inclusions and is not removable with rubbing.

**Resin-coated pieces.** Some very inexpensive pieces have a thin resin coating to improve luster or hold fragile surfaces together. A resin coat makes the surface harder than natural selenite and may resist the fingernail test slightly. Look for bubbles, uneven thickness at edges, or a plastic-like smell when warmed.

**Assembled pieces.** Large "selenite" lamp bases are sometimes made from compressed gypsum powder rather than solid natural selenite. These will still respond to the fingernail and water tests, as the base mineral is the same, but the internal structure differs.

Selenite is abundant and inexpensive — very low prices are therefore not a reliable red flag in the same way they are for rarer gems.
