# Can Blue Tiger's Eye Go in Water?

Blue Tiger's Eye can go in water briefly — a quick rinse under lukewarm tap water is safe and effective for removing dust and oils. It should not be soaked for extended periods, and salt water should be avoided entirely. The stone's quartz base is chemically stable in fresh water, but prolonged immersion and salt create cumulative risks to the surface and, where applicable, to metal settings.

## Why Blue Tiger's Eye Is Mostly Water-Safe

Blue Tiger's Eye is a variety of quartz (SiO2) with a Mohs hardness of 6.5 to 7. Quartz does not dissolve, corrode, or react chemically with fresh water at normal temperatures. This places Blue Tiger's Eye well above the threshold where water contact causes mineral breakdown — stones below a Mohs hardness of about 5 (such as calcite or selenite) can dissolve or pit in water, but quartz-family stones do not.

A 30-second rinse under cool or lukewarm tap water is the standard method for a quick physical clean. The water removes surface oils from handling and environmental dust. Pat the stone dry with a soft cloth afterward and allow it to air-dry fully before storing or setting it in a closed box.

For a full guide on which crystals are and are not safe in water, see [what crystals can go in water](/learn/what-crystals-can-go-in-water).

## When Water Becomes a Problem

### Prolonged soaking

While a brief rinse is harmless, leaving Blue Tiger's Eye submerged for hours or overnight introduces two risks. First, any micro-fractures or existing surface cracks can allow water to wick in slowly, potentially causing the stone to expand and contract with temperature changes in ways that worsen the crack over time. Second, if the piece is set in metal — a ring, pendant, or bracelet with a glued bezel — prolonged soaking can degrade adhesives and, for silver or copper settings, promote tarnishing and corrosion at the join.

### Salt water

Salt water is more corrosive than plain water and should not be used with Blue Tiger's Eye. Salt is mildly abrasive at a granular level and accelerates tarnishing in metal settings. Some practitioners use dry salt as a cleansing medium, but this is also best avoided: salt is hygroscopic and draws ambient moisture toward the stone's surface, which can promote slow surface changes over time in humid environments.

For a detailed guide to which stones are safe with salt, see [salt safe crystals](/learn/salt-safe-crystals).

### Gem elixirs and drinking water

Blue Tiger's Eye should not be placed directly in water intended for drinking or topical use. If you use the crystal elixir practice, use the indirect method: place the stone outside a sealed glass container of water rather than submerging it inside. This avoids any contact between the stone and the water.

## How to Clean Blue Tiger's Eye

1. Hold the stone under lukewarm tap water.
2. Rub the surface gently with clean fingers or a very soft cloth for 20–30 seconds.
3. Pat dry immediately with a clean dry cloth.
4. Set the stone on a dry surface and allow it to air-dry completely before storing or wearing.

For cleaning mounted jewelry, the same approach applies: rinse briefly, dry immediately, and check settings afterward. A dry, soft toothbrush can be used to clean around prong settings or bezel edges — use it before the rinse to loosen any stuck debris, then rinse and dry as above.

## Sun Drying After Rinsing

After a water rinse, placing the stone in 15–30 minutes of morning sunlight to dry is practical and physically safe. Blue Tiger's Eye is an opaque mineral and does not fade noticeably under brief sun exposure. Prolonged exposure over many months may slightly warm or shift the tone of some specimens, particularly those with more color variation.
