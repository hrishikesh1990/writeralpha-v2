# How to Identify Real Labradorite and Spot Fakes

Real labradorite produces its iridescent color (labradorescence) from structural interference between microscopic layers inside the feldspar — an effect that shifts, moves, and appears selectively as the stone changes angle. Glass and resin imitations reproduce a surface flash, but that flash is applied, not structural: it looks flat, uniform, and unchanging from most angles. Watching how the color behaves when you move the stone is the most reliable single test.

## What Labradorescence Actually Is

Labradorite is a plagioclase feldspar, formula (Ca,Na)[Al(Al,Si)Si2O8], with Mohs hardness of 6 to 6.5. Its optical effect — labradorescence — is produced by light interference between exsolution lamellae: alternating microscopic layers of high- and low-calcium feldspar that formed during slow cooling of the host rock. These layers diffract and interfere with light at particular angles, producing the characteristic color flash.

This is a structural property, not a coating. It cannot be painted on, applied as a film, or reproduced exactly with surface treatments. This is why genuine labradorescence and imitation flash behave differently when you observe them in motion.

## Step-by-Step Identification

### Step 1: Watch the flash move

Hold the stone under a single light source (a lamp, a window, or a phone torch) and slowly rotate it. In real labradorite:
- The flash appears, brightens, and then disappears as you pass through specific angles.
- Different areas of the stone activate at different angles; the flash is patchy or zoned, not wall-to-wall.
- The color shifts from blue to green, gold, or purple depending on the angle — not all at once.

In glass and resin imitations, the flash tends to be:
- Visible across the whole face simultaneously, uniformly bright.
- Present at many angles without fading in and out clearly.
- Identical across multiple stones in the same batch — natural material varies stone by stone.

### Step 2: Feel the temperature

Real feldspar is cold to the touch and warms slowly in the hand. Resin and polymer fakes feel room-temperature or slightly warm immediately, because plastic has much lower thermal conductivity than stone.

### Step 3: Check for bubbles, seams, and mold lines

Turn the stone and look at its body, edges, and any drill holes under magnification (a loupe or smartphone macro):
- Rounded, isolated bubbles in the body point to glass or resin.
- Seam lines or mold marks along the edges indicate casting.
- Drill holes in real stone tend to have sharp, clean walls; resin drill holes look slightly melted or have a softened profile.

### Step 4: Look at the back

Real labradorite cabochons are typically cut with a domed top and a flat or slightly curved back showing gray, dark gray, or gray-white feldspar. The labradorescence is produced by internal structure, not by anything applied to the back.

Imitations often have a visibly black, opaque backing material — a separate layer behind the flash effect — that is obvious when the stone is unset.

### Step 5: Test hardness (if you are willing to try)

Labradorite at Mohs 6 to 6.5 cannot be scratched by a copper coin (Mohs ~3) or a steel pocket knife blade (Mohs ~5.5). Resin and polymer fakes, typically Mohs 2 to 4, are scratched easily by a knife. Only test on an inconspicuous spot or the base of the stone, as any scratch is permanent.

### Step 6: UV light (optional)

Real labradorite is inert under shortwave UV light — it does not fluoresce. Resin and polymer fakes often fluoresce pale blue or green under UV. UV flashlights suitable for mineral testing are widely available. This test does not help with glass imitations, which also tend to be UV-inert, so it is a supporting test, not a standalone one.

## Comparison Summary

| Feature | Real labradorite | Glass / resin imitation |
|---|---|---|
| Flash behavior | Shifts and zones with rotation | Flat, uniform, visible from most angles |
| Body temperature | Cold to touch | Neutral or warm |
| Bubbles | None | Often present (resin/glass) |
| Back surface | Gray feldspar | Often black opaque layer |
| UV response | Inert | Often fluoresces (resin) |
| Hardness | Cannot be scratched by knife | Scratched by knife (resin) |
| Batch consistency | Varies stone to stone | Often identical across batch |

## Buying Online

Photographs make genuine labradorescence and imitation flash almost indistinguishable — both look dramatic under a controlled angled light source. Before purchasing online:

- Request a video showing the stone being slowly rotated under natural or lamp light, not a single static image.
- Ask the seller to state the material explicitly (natural labradorite feldspar, glass, resin, or composite).
- Check if the flash fades in and out across the rotation — a sign of real structural interference — or stays constantly bright.
- Read reviews specifically noting whether received stones matched the photos.

Price is also a signal: unusually low prices for heavily flashing stones warrant extra scrutiny.
