# How to Identify Real Blue Lace Agate

Real Blue Lace Agate is a pale, soft blue and white banded chalcedony with a wavy, lace-like pattern. Its color is always subtle — never vivid or saturated — because it comes from light scattering within the stone's structure, not a pigment. Any piece showing bright or intense blue is almost certainly dyed. Four checks — color, hardness, the acid test, and dye in fractures — cover most of what a buyer needs.

## What Genuine Blue Lace Agate Looks Like

Blue Lace Agate is a variety of banded chalcedony (microcrystalline silicon dioxide, SiO2). Its defining visual characteristics are:

- **Color:** Pale to medium blue, often described as sky blue or powder blue. White bands or zones alternate with the blue in a wavy, flowing pattern. The stone is not uniformly blue — the banding is always visible and never perfectly regular.
- **Pattern:** Wavy, lace-like concentric or parallel bands. The bands may be very fine or broader, but they always have a soft, undulating quality. Straight, hard-edged bands are not typical.
- **Surface when polished:** Waxy to vitreous luster. Polished surfaces feel smooth and slightly cool to the touch.
- **Translucency:** Translucent to opaque. Holding a thin piece up to bright light may reveal a faint glow through the paler zones.
- **Specific gravity:** Approximately 2.6. Blue Lace Agate feels noticeably heavier than plastic or resin of the same size and slightly heavier than glass (SG approximately 2.5), though the difference is subtle.

The color of genuine Blue Lace Agate is believed to result from Rayleigh scattering of light by fine inclusions or structural features within the chalcedony, an optical effect rather than a chemical pigment. This is why the blue is always pale and diffuse, not intense.

## Step-by-Step Identification Checks

### 1. Check the color intensity

Genuine Blue Lace Agate is pale and soft. If the blue is vivid, saturated, or evenly distributed without any paler zones, the material is likely dyed. Dyed agate is common in the trade: the agate trade has a well-documented history of dyeing material to enhance or imitate natural colors. A rich, commercial-looking blue should raise immediate suspicion.

### 2. Look for dye in fractures or pits

Examine the surface under a 10x loupe or a magnifying glass, looking especially at any small fractures, pits, or surface irregularities. Dye concentrates in these areas because the porous microstructure of chalcedony draws the solution in. A genuine stone will show no color concentration in fractures; a dyed stone will often show a deeper blue line running along cracks or a pool of color in surface pits.

### 3. Hardness test

Blue Lace Agate has a Mohs hardness of 6.5 to 7, which means it can scratch window glass (Mohs approximately 5.5) and a steel pocket knife blade (Mohs approximately 5.5 to 6.5). Imitation materials made of glass, plastic, or resin will behave differently: glass is similar in hardness but will not have the banding; plastic scratches easily with a metal point. Scratch an inconspicuous edge against a glass tile or the unglazed base of a ceramic tile — a genuine stone leaves a mark on the ceramic and is not scratched by it.

### 4. Acid test (distinguishes from Blue Calcite)

Blue Calcite is a common look-alike sold alongside Blue Lace Agate. Calcite is a calcium carbonate mineral (CaCO3) that fizzes when a drop of household white vinegar is applied to a raw, unpolished surface. Blue Lace Agate is silicon dioxide and produces no reaction whatsoever. This test is reliable and requires only vinegar; apply one drop to a small unpolished area and watch for any bubbling. If it fizzes, the stone is not Blue Lace Agate.

### 5. Scratch resistance compared to look-alikes

| Stone | Mohs Hardness | Reaction to vinegar | Banding |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blue Lace Agate (genuine) | 6.5–7 | None | Wavy blue/white |
| Blue Calcite | 3 | Fizzes | None or faint |
| Angelite | 3.5 | None | None; opaque white-blue |
| Blue Chalcedony | 6.5–7 | None | No banding; uniform |
| Dyed agate | 6.5–7 | None | Dye in fractures; vivid color |
| Glass | ~5.5 | None | No banding; bubbles under magnification |

### 6. Temperature response

Genuine stone feels cool to the touch when first picked up. Plastic and resin quickly warm to body temperature. This is a rough initial test, not definitive on its own, but useful combined with the others.

## When to Be Most Cautious

Supply of high-quality Blue Lace Agate from the primary Namibian source has been constrained since the main mine reduced output. This has driven up prices for genuine material and created more incentive to substitute dyed agate or other blue stones. The presence of very cheap Blue Lace Agate — particularly in large quantities at very low unit prices — is a flag worth investigating.
