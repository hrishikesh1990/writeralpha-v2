# White Agate Price Guide — What to Expect and Why

White Agate is an abundant, affordable stone. Tumbled pieces retail for $1–$5 each; cabochons range from under $1 to $30 per carat depending on clarity and treatment. Dyeing, bleaching, and resin impregnation are widespread and affect price. Prices below were checked against retail and wholesale listings in September 2026 and vary by grade, seller, and region.

## Typical Price Ranges by Form

| Form | Typical retail range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Small tumbled stone (20–35 mm) | $1–$5 per piece | Most common form; majority is dyed or bleached |
| Wholesale tumbled (per kg, ~60–80 pieces) | $19–$40 per kg | Wholesale only; retail markup typically 3–5× |
| Cabochon (per carat) | $1–$30 per carat | Wide range driven by clarity, translucency, and treatment |
| Beads (per strand) | $5–$20 per strand | Depends on bead size, uniformity, and treatment |
| Polished slice or slab | $5–$40 per piece | Size and pattern drive price |
| Geode or large display piece | $20–$150+ per piece | Highly variable; size and interior crystal coverage matter |
| Raw or rough material | $5–$25 per pound | Lowest entry point; quality is unverified until cut |

## What Drives White Agate Pricing

### Abundance

White Agate is one of the most common gem materials globally. Agate deposits occur in volcanic rock worldwide, and pale or white-dominant material is not rare. This keeps baseline prices low. It is not a scarce commodity like alexandrite or fine demantoid garnet, and the price does not behave like one.

### Treatment

The single factor that most distorts White Agate pricing is treatment. Dyeing, bleaching, and resin impregnation are standard industry practice and are carried out at scale, particularly on material imported from Brazil, India, and China. Treated material that is clearly disclosed as such is fairly priced. The concern for buyers is undisclosed treatment on pieces priced as if they were natural, untreated material.

Undyed, naturally white chalcedony with clean translucency and visible natural banding is genuinely less common than treated white material and warrants a modest premium. A seller who cannot state the treatment history of a piece is worth approaching with caution. For how to tell treated from untreated material, see [how to identify White Agate](/gemstones/white-agate/how-to-identify).

### Clarity and translucency

Within any price tier, pieces with good translucency — where light passes through thin edges or the stone glows slightly when held to a lamp — are more desirable than fully opaque material of the same size. Natural banding that creates visual interest (fortification patterns, waterline banding, concentric rings) adds value, as does the quality of polish.

### Size

Larger pieces of consistent quality are proportionally more expensive than small ones. A 10-gram tumbled stone is easily sourced; a 500-gram polished sphere with even color and good translucency is significantly less common and commands a higher price. Very large, museum-quality specimens are priced individually and are outside the typical retail range.

### Cut and form

Raw rough is the least processed and least expensive form. Tumbled stones add one processing step. Cabochons add lapidary labor. Faceted pieces (less common for agate) add more. Custom carvings and art pieces incorporate significant artisan time and can reach several hundred dollars regardless of the underlying material cost.

### Origin

While agate origin is not as commercially significant as it is for rubies or sapphires, certain localities are recognized by collectors. Brazilian banded agates, Lake Superior agates, and material from specific African deposits can command a premium among collectors. For retail crystal buyers, origin rarely affects price significantly, and claimed origins are often unverifiable.

## How to Avoid Overpaying

1. **Know the treatment status.** Ask directly whether the piece is dyed, bleached, or resin-treated. A reputable seller can answer this. If the piece has unnaturally vivid or even color, assume it has been treated until confirmed otherwise.
2. **Compare tumbled stone prices.** Paying $10–$15 each for small tumbled White Agate stones is toward the high end of what the market supports, unless the piece is notably large, high-clarity, or certified untreated.
3. **Check the durability context.** White Agate has a Mohs hardness of 6.5–7, which is appropriate for most jewelry uses and everyday display. Understanding [Mohs hardness](/learn/mohs-hardness-scale) helps calibrate what a stone is actually worth for a given use case.
4. **Assess the sourcing story.** Fair-trade and ethically sourced labeling adds to retail cost, but provides transparency about the supply chain. For a full discussion of what ethical sourcing means in the crystal market, see [ethical crystal sourcing](/learn/ethical-crystal-sourcing).
5. **Buy from sellers who show drill holes and actual batch photos.** Retouched product photography hides treatment evidence. A seller confident in the material shows it from multiple angles, including the interior of drill holes on beads.

## What You Cannot Verify at Home

Treatment type and extent, origin, and gemological grade all require instrument testing to confirm. Refractive index measurement, Raman spectroscopy, and fluorescence testing under UV can separate natural untreated material from treated specimens. For high-value purchases, a certificate from an accredited gemological laboratory is the only way to be certain. For everyday crystal practice, the cost of laboratory testing typically exceeds the value of the stone itself, so the practical approach is to buy from disclosed, reputable sources rather than seeking laboratory confirmation for every piece.
