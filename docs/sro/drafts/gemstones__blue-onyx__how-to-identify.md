# How to Identify Real Blue Onyx

The most important thing to know first: most Blue Onyx sold today is genuine chalcedony that has been dyed to achieve the blue color. Dyed chalcedony is real stone — it is not fake. What matters is distinguishing genuine stone (dyed or naturally colored chalcedony) from outright imitations such as glass, resin, or plastic. A secondary question is whether a piece is natural-color or dyed.

## Step 1: Understand What You Are Looking For

Blue Onyx is a banded variety of chalcedony (microcrystalline quartz, SiO₂) with:

- Mohs hardness: 6.5–7
- Density: 2.58–2.64 g/cm³
- Luster: waxy to vitreous
- Appearance: parallel or curved banding in blue, white, and grey tones
- Color origin: either natural (Tyndall-effect light scattering in fine-grained material, found mainly in Argentina) or dye-enhanced (the majority of commercial material)

Common imitations are glass, dyed resin, and plastic. Common confusions are blue chalcedony (similar material, less banding), blue agate (curved rather than parallel banding), and blue calcite (softer, at Mohs 3).

## Step 2: Temperature Test

Genuine stone feels noticeably cool to the touch when you first hold it, and warms slowly. Glass also starts cool but warms slightly faster. Plastic and resin reach room temperature almost immediately — they feel warm from the first touch.

Hold the piece for 30 seconds, then press it against the inside of your wrist. If it still feels cool, it is likely stone. If it already feels warm or room-temperature, plastic or resin is probable.

## Step 3: Hardness Check

Blue Onyx (Mohs 6.5–7) will not be scratched by a steel nail or penknife blade (Mohs ~5.5–6.5), but it will scratch glass (Mohs ~5.5) and it will be scratched by a piece of quartz (Mohs 7). Glass imitations have a Mohs of roughly 5.5 and will be scratched by a steel nail.

To test: attempt to scratch an inconspicuous area of the stone with a steel nail or a piece of quartz. If the surface scratches easily, the piece is unlikely to be genuine chalcedony. Examine the mark under magnification — if it is a white powder mark rather than a groove, you may have scratched surface dye or polish rather than the stone itself; wipe away the powder and check whether a groove remains.

Do not use this test on finished jewelry pieces unless you have the seller's permission or are testing a surface that will be hidden.

## Step 4: Look at the Banding

Genuine Blue Onyx shows parallel or slightly curved bands of varying color — alternating blue, white, and grey zones. Under a loupe:

- **Genuine chalcedony** shows natural variation within bands: subtle color gradations, micro-inclusions, and slightly uneven band edges.
- **Glass** has no banding; it may show bubbles or swirl patterns under magnification.
- **Dyed agate or chalcedony** shows banding, but the color may concentrate along natural pore lines and appear more saturated at the edges of lighter zones (a tell-tale of dyeing).

Perfectly uniform, deep blue color with no banding is unusual for natural chalcedony and may indicate a solid dyed specimen where banding is obscured by the dye, or it may indicate a non-stone imitation.

## Step 5: Check the Weight

Genuine chalcedony (density 2.58–2.64 g/cm³) feels noticeably heavier than plastic for the same size. Resin varies but is often lighter than stone. Glass has a density of about 2.5 g/cm³ — close to chalcedony — so glass cannot be ruled out by weight alone.

## Natural Color vs. Dyed: Can You Tell?

Distinguishing natural-color Blue Onyx from dyed material without laboratory equipment is difficult. Some clues:

- Dyed specimens often show more intense blue in zones that correspond to natural porosity (cracks, pore lines, banding edges)
- Natural-color specimens from Argentina tend to have a softer, more diffuse blue with some translucency
- Very uniform, deeply saturated blue across the whole piece almost always indicates dyeing

Definitive identification requires spectroscopic testing (UV-Vis or FTIR). For buying purposes, ask sellers directly about treatment disclosure — reputable sellers will state whether material is dyed. For more on price differences between treated and natural-color material, see [Blue Onyx price guide](/gemstones/blue-onyx/price-guide).

## Red Flags When Buying

- No mention of treatment on the product listing (most blue onyx should disclose dyeing)
- Price dramatically below market for the described quality — see [price guide](/gemstones/blue-onyx/price-guide)
- Perfectly uniform, deep color with no visible banding (may be glass or resin)
- Warm to the touch immediately (likely plastic or resin)
- Much lighter than expected for its size (likely resin or plastic)
