# How to Identify Real Sapphire: Tests and Red Flags

Real sapphire is corundum (Al₂O₃) with a Mohs hardness of 9, a specific gravity of approximately 4.00, and a refractive index of 1.762 to 1.770. Common substitutes include glass, synthetic (lab-grown) sapphire, blue topaz, iolite, tanzanite, and blue spinel. At home, a scratch test and a loupe examination can narrow the field; definitive identification requires a gemologist or a laboratory report from GIA, Gübelin, or a comparable institution.

## The Main Substitutes and Simulants

Understanding what is being sold as sapphire is the first step. Several distinct materials are sold in its place, sometimes deliberately and sometimes through confusion:

**Glass.** The most common and least expensive substitute. Blue glass can be very convincing in mass-market jewelry. Glass is significantly softer (Mohs 5–6), lighter (SG around 2.5), and often shows gas bubbles or swirl marks under magnification. Star-pattern glass imitations have been identified by GIA; unlike genuine asterism, the star in these pieces is molded into a fixed shape and does not move as the light source changes.

**Synthetic (lab-grown) sapphire.** Chemically and structurally identical to natural sapphire — the same Al₂O₃ composition, Mohs 9, and RI. Flame-fusion synthetics (the oldest and cheapest method) show curved growth lines and curved color bands rather than the angular growth patterns of natural corundum. Hydrothermal synthetics may show nail-head inclusions. No at-home test reliably separates natural from synthetic sapphire; laboratory analysis (FTIR spectroscopy, trace element testing) is required.

**Blue topaz.** Topaz (Al₂SiO₄F₂) has a Mohs hardness of 8, lower than sapphire, and an SG of approximately 3.53 — lighter than sapphire. Blue topaz has perfect cleavage, meaning it can split cleanly if struck. Sapphire has no cleavage. The RI of blue topaz (1.619–1.627) is measurably lower than sapphire's.

**Iolite.** Iolite (Mg,Fe)₂Al₄Si₅O₁₈ has a striking violet-blue color but Mohs hardness of 7 to 7.5 and a notably lower SG (2.57–2.66). Its strong pleochroism — showing different colors (blue, violet, yellow-gray) from different viewing angles — is distinctive and visible to the naked eye.

**Tanzanite.** Tanzanite (a blue-violet zoisite) has Mohs 6 to 7 and strong pleochroism (blue, violet, burgundy). It is much softer and lighter than sapphire and significantly more expensive for fine quality.

**Blue spinel.** Natural blue spinel (MgAl₂O₄) is a singly refractive mineral — a refractometer will show a single RI rather than the double reading corundum gives. Spinel has Mohs 7.5 to 8.

## At-Home Tests: What They Can and Cannot Tell You

**Scratch test.** Sapphire at Mohs 9 will scratch glass (Mohs 5.5), a steel file (Mohs 6.5), and quartz (Mohs 7). If a stone marketed as sapphire cannot scratch glass or is itself scratched by quartz, it is not sapphire. Use a hidden area of the stone and wipe away debris to check whether a genuine scratch occurred. This test does not distinguish natural sapphire from synthetic sapphire.

**Weight (specific gravity).** Sapphire at SG 4.00 is noticeably denser than glass (SG ~2.5) and blue topaz (SG ~3.53). A hydrostatic weighing test — weighing the stone in air and then suspended in water — gives a precise SG. For loose stones, the difference is perceptible by hand: sapphire feels heavy for its size. This test does not distinguish natural from synthetic.

**Loupe examination.** A 10× jeweler's loupe reveals features invisible to the naked eye. Natural sapphire typically contains characteristic inclusions: rutile silk (fine needle-like inclusions of rutile), fingerprint inclusions (healed fractures), zircon halos, and color zoning in angular patterns. Glass shows gas bubbles and swirl lines. Flame-fusion synthetic sapphire shows curved striae — a very distinctive curved banding absent in natural material. The star in glass imitations is fixed; in genuine star sapphire, the star moves across the dome as the light source moves.

**Temperature.** Genuine sapphire is a poor heat conductor and feels cool to the touch, slower to warm than glass. This is a rough indication only and is not diagnostic.

## Treatments to Know About

Most sapphires on the market — estimates range from 90 to 95 percent — have been heat-treated to improve or stabilize color. Heat treatment is considered acceptable in the trade when disclosed; it does not make a stone "fake." Unheated sapphire commands a significant price premium.

Fracture-filling with glass improves apparent clarity but adds an additional layer of treatment. Beryllium diffusion (lattice diffusion treatment) can introduce color deep into the stone or to its surface only, affecting value significantly. A laboratory report identifies treatments and is essential for any significant purchase.

Dyed sapphire — corundum with dye introduced through fractures — is rare but exists, often in low-cost goods with obvious fractures. Dye pools visibly in fractures under magnification.

## When to Go to a Laboratory

Any sapphire purchase above a few hundred dollars warrants a report from an independent laboratory. GIA, Gübelin, SSEF, and GRS are the most widely recognized for sapphire. The report will state: species (corundum confirmed), variety (natural), treatment status (no indications of heating / indications of heating / fracture filling), and for premium material, country of origin.
