# Selenite Price Guide — What It Costs and Why

Selenite is one of the least expensive crystals in retail trade. It is gypsum (CaSO4·2H2O), an abundant mineral found on every continent, and most commercial material comes from large deposits in Morocco and Mexico. As of September 2026, small wands sell for $6–$25, slabs for $15–$120, and bulk pieces for $5–$25 per pound. High price tags on standard selenite are a red flag worth investigating.

## Why Selenite Is Affordable

Selenite (and its fibrous relative satin spar, which dominates the wand market) is extracted in large quantities from open surface deposits and mines. Morocco supplies the bulk of retail wands and palm stones sold in the United States and Europe. Gypsum is also among the most commonly quarried industrial minerals in the world — used in drywall, cement, and agriculture — so the mining infrastructure is well-established.

Selenite's low Mohs hardness (2.0) means little cutting or polishing effort is needed for raw pieces, and even shaped wands require minimal tooling. These factors combine to keep production costs low across the supply chain.

## What Drives Price

**Size.** The single strongest price driver for selenite. A 6-inch wand and a 24-inch wand may share the same origin and quality but differ in price by 3 to 5 times. Slabs priced by surface area show a similar pattern.

**Finish.** Rough, unpolished pieces are typically cheaper per unit than smoothly polished forms. A polished slab may cost two to four times more per square foot than an equivalent rough piece.

**Transparency and clarity.** True transparent selenite crystals — large, clear, glassy — are rarer than the fibrous satin spar and alabaster forms and command modest premiums among collectors. Inclusions, fractures, and visible cloudiness lower value.

**Formation uniqueness.** Naturally formed crystal groupings, hourglass-pattern selenite (formed in saline lakes), and desert rose gypsum are all priced somewhat above standard wands for their visual appeal.

**Seller overhead.** A hand-curated crystal shop with retail premises prices higher than a bulk online importer, which prices higher than a direct wholesaler. This markup reflects selection, presentation, and service — not necessarily material quality.

## How to Avoid Overpaying

**Know the typical range.** A standard 12-inch satin spar wand for $35 from a boutique shop is roughly double the mid-market price. You are paying for curation and presentation, not rarer material.

**Check what you are buying.** Most retail "selenite" wands are satin spar — the fibrous variety. Both are genuine gypsum, but the naming convention differs from mineralogical precision. Neither is rare; both should be affordable. See [how to identify selenite](/gemstones/selenite/how-to-identify) for how to verify what you have.

**Be skeptical of extraordinary prices.** Because selenite is abundant, a $150 standard wand is not justified by material scarcity — check what you are actually being sold.

**Be equally skeptical of prices that seem impossibly low.** Glass, acrylic, or resin pieces can be cut to look like selenite wands and sold at a lower price point. At $1–$2 for a large wand, verify the piece with the fingernail test described in the identification guide.

## Price vs. Hardness: A Durability Note

Selenite's low price partly reflects its low durability. At Mohs 2.0, it is one of the softest minerals in common gem trade — see [Mohs hardness scale](/learn/mohs-hardness-scale) for context. A selenite wand will scratch on contact with virtually any hard surface and will deteriorate in damp environments. For display pieces, this matters less. For daily-wear jewelry or wearable pieces, the combination of low hardness and water solubility means selenite has a shorter lifespan than harder alternatives.

## Sourcing and Ethics

Selenite is not currently subject to significant documented concerns about conflict sourcing or labor abuses specific to the mineral, but gypsum mining, like any extractive industry, has localized environmental impact. Moroccan material dominates the European and U.S. market. For guidance on evaluating crystal suppliers and sourcing practices, see [ethical crystal sourcing](/learn/ethical-crystal-sourcing).
