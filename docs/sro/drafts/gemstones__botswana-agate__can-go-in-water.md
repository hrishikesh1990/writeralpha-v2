# Can Botswana Agate Go in Water?

Yes. Botswana Agate is safe in water. It is a microcrystalline quartz (SiO2, chalcedony) with a Mohs hardness of 6.5–7 and no soluble components. Brief rinsing or soaking under cool water poses no physical risk to the stone. The practical limits are about prolonged soaks, thermal shock, and the stone's setting rather than the material itself.

## Why Botswana Agate Is Water-Safe

All agate is a variety of chalcedony, which is microcrystalline silicon dioxide — the same chemistry as quartz, just in a finer crystal structure. Silicon dioxide does not dissolve in water at room temperature, does not react with plain water, and does not release any harmful substances when wet. Botswana Agate's Mohs hardness of 6.5–7 means it is harder than steel (Mohs ~5.5) and not susceptible to surface damage from normal water contact.

This puts Botswana Agate well into the water-safe category. Compare this to minerals that genuinely should not go in water: selenite (Mohs 2, dissolves slowly), pyrite (oxidizes with moisture), malachite (copper content, mildly toxic in water infusions), or halite (dissolves readily). Botswana Agate shares none of these vulnerabilities. For a broader guide to which crystals can and cannot go in water, see [what crystals can go in water](/learn/what-crystals-can-go-in-water).

## What to Avoid

Being water-safe does not mean no limits apply:

**Temperature extremes.** Banded agates often contain fine internal fractures along banding planes, as the bands form at slightly different rates and compositions. Sudden temperature changes — such as moving from a cold rinse directly to very hot water — can stress these micro-fractures. Use cool to lukewarm water and avoid thermal shock.

**Prolonged salt-water soak.** Short exposure to mild salt water is not harmful. Extended soaking (hours to days) is unnecessary and risks salt crystals working into surface micro-fractures as the water evaporates, which may reduce polish over time. Plain water is equally effective for physical cleaning and does not carry this risk. For stones where salt is a cleansing choice, see [salt-safe crystals](/learn/salt-safe-crystals).

**Settings and adhesives.** If your Botswana Agate is set in jewelry using glue, foil backing, or resin, prolonged soaking can loosen those adhesives regardless of the stone's own water tolerance. Clean set pieces quickly with a damp cloth rather than soaking them.

**Ultrasonic and steam cleaners.** These are not water-contact issues, but mechanical and thermal ones. Avoid both for banded agates — the vibration and heat can open existing micro-fractures.

## How to Clean Botswana Agate with Water

1. Hold the stone under cool or lukewarm running water for 30 to 60 seconds.
2. If needed, apply a small drop of mild liquid soap and scrub gently with a soft brush.
3. Rinse thoroughly under running water.
4. Pat dry with a clean cloth.
5. Allow to air-dry fully before storing or placing in a closed container.

This process is safe for loose tumbled stones, raw pieces, and most polished specimens. For water as one part of a broader cleansing routine, see [how to cleanse Botswana Agate](/gemstones/botswana-agate/how-to-cleanse).

## Can Botswana Agate Go in the Sun After Washing?

Yes. After rinsing and drying, brief sun exposure — 15 to 30 minutes of morning or indirect sunlight — poses no physical risk to Botswana Agate. Its natural gray-pink-peach palette is not caused by light-sensitive pigments or dyes in natural material, and the stone is opaque so UV does not penetrate to cause internal damage. Avoid leaving it in direct midday sun for hours, which can cause any surface to heat unevenly; this is not a chemistry issue, just a thermal one. See [sun-safe crystals](/learn/sun-safe-crystals) for a broader guide.

## Crystal Water Infusions

Some practitioners make crystal-infused water by placing stones in or near drinking water. Botswana Agate is chemically inert and does not release harmful substances into water, so it is not toxic in this sense. That said, no scientific evidence supports crystal infusions having any therapeutic effect beyond placebo, and crystals — however water-safe — are not substitutes for medical or nutritional guidance.
