# How to Identify Real Black Moonstone

Real Black Moonstone shows a soft, floating internal glow — adularescence — that shifts beneath the surface as you tilt the stone. Glass and dyed imitations lack this effect or produce only surface glare. Hardness around 6 to 6.5, feldspar cleavage faces, and natural internal texture are the three physical checks that separate genuine material from common substitutes.

## Understanding the Trade Name Issue

"Black Moonstone" is not a precise mineralogical term. It is a trade name applied to dark feldspar material that produces a sheen. Laboratory analysis of specimens from Madagascar — the main commercial source — has found the material to be microcline feldspar (KAlSi3O8), an alkali feldspar closely related to classic orthoclase moonstone. Some dark material sold under the same name is dark labradorite or larvikite, which are also feldspars but have different optical effects and slightly different mineralogy.

This trade-name ambiguity matters when you are trying to identify what you have purchased. A reputable seller will specify the mineralogy or at minimum the origin. For a broader look at what honest stone purchasing looks like, see [are online crystals real](/learn/are-online-crystals-real) and the [price guide](/gemstones/black-moonstone/price-guide) for price signals that indicate genuine material.

## Step 1: Check the Sheen Under Indirect Light

Hold the stone under a single indirect light source — natural daylight from a window is ideal — and tilt it slowly in different directions. Real Black Moonstone or labradorite-variety black moonstone should show:

- A soft, billowing or rolling glow beneath the surface (adularescence) — this looks like a cloud or light trapped inside the stone, not a sharp metallic reflection.
- Movement of the glow as the stone tilts — the brightest point shifts as the angle changes.

Glass imitations may produce a surface sheen or reflection but it sits on the surface rather than appearing to float below it. Dyed black glass or dyed quartz produces no directional glow at all under tilting.

A strong, multi-colored flash spanning the whole face — vivid blue, green, gold — suggests labradorite rather than moonstone. Larvikite shows scattered plate-like silver flashes in a granular rock texture. True Black Moonstone has a more diffuse, quieter glow.

## Step 2: Test the Hardness

Black Moonstone (feldspar) has Mohs hardness 6 to 6.5. Practical test:

- A steel knife blade (Mohs approximately 5.5 to 6.5) will typically just barely scratch feldspar or leave no mark.
- A piece of quartz (Mohs 7) or a quartz-bearing tile will scratch the feldspar surface easily, so avoid scraping tiles against a specimen you care about.
- Glass (Mohs approximately 5.5) should be scratched by the stone rather than scratching it.

Test only on an inconspicuous spot and wipe away powder before reading the scratch — powder from the testing material can look like a scratch on the stone.

## Step 3: Look for Feldspar Cleavage

Feldspar has two well-developed cleavage directions at approximately 90 degrees. On a rough or broken specimen this produces flat, glassy reflective faces that catch light at precise angles — different from the rounded conchoidal fracture of glass or obsidian. On a polished cabochon the cleavage is hidden, but the edges of the piece may show small stepped chips along these planes rather than curved shell-like fractures.

## Step 4: Check for Natural Inclusions

Genuine Black Moonstone from Madagascar often contains small peppery inclusions, color zoning, or irregular patches of peach or salmon color alongside the dark ground color. A uniformly black, perfectly transparent surface with no variation is more consistent with dyed glass or onyx than with natural feldspar.

## Common Substitutes and How to Distinguish Them

| Material | Appearance | Key distinction from Black Moonstone |
|----------|-----------|--------------------------------------|
| Glass / dyed glass | Uniformly black, possible surface glare | Warmer to the touch, no internal floating glow, no cleavage faces |
| Black obsidian | Glassy black, possible sheen | Volcanic glass — conchoidal (shell-like) fracture, no feldspar cleavage, no rolling glow |
| Dark labradorite | Vivid blue-green-gold flash | Flash is stronger, more color-zoned, more metallic; still a feldspar |
| Larvikite | Dark rock with silver-blue flecks | Rock texture visible — granular, not a single mineral cabochon |
| Black onyx | Uniformly black, high polish | Chalcedony — no directional glow; almost always dyed; harder at 6.5-7 |
| Dyed black quartz | Very dark, no sheen | Harder than feldspar (Mohs 7), no adularescence, color may pool in cracks |

## When in Doubt

A reputable gemological laboratory can identify feldspar varieties with refractive index measurement and, for confirmation, Raman spectroscopy or XRD analysis. For a stone worth significant money, this is worth the cost. For lower-cost tumbled pieces, the sheen character, cleavage faces, and natural inclusions are sufficient for a working identification in most situations.
