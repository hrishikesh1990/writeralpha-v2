# How to Identify Real Peridot — Testing for Fakes

Real peridot has extremely strong double refraction: look straight down through a faceted stone and you will see two distinct images of each back facet. This doubling, combined with characteristic lily-pad inclusions and a vitreous lime-green color, separates genuine peridot from most imitations. Green glass — the most common substitute — shows no doubling and no natural inclusions.

## The Birefringence Test (Facet Doubling)

Peridot's birefringence of 0.035 to 0.038 is among the highest of any common gemstone. The practical result is visible without equipment: hold a faceted peridot above a light source and look down through the table facet (the flat top). The edges of the back facets appear doubled — you will see two lines where there should be one. Tilt the stone and the effect shifts but does not disappear.

Green glass, synthetic spinel, and synthetic corundum (the most common peridot imitations noted in gemological literature) are singly refractive or have weak birefringence and will not show this doubling. If you cannot see any doubling at all in a stone sold as peridot, treat that as a strong warning sign.

## Inclusions Under Magnification

Examine the stone under a 10x jeweler's loupe or a strong magnifying glass. Genuine peridot typically contains:

- **Lily pads** — disk-shaped, reflective features caused by stress fractures radiating from small chromite crystals. These are the most diagnostic inclusion type.
- **Black chromite octahedra** — small, dark, multifaceted crystals visible under magnification.
- **Minute fluid droplets** or needle-like features in some specimens.

A stone that is completely free of inclusions at 10x magnification and sold at a suspiciously low price deserves extra scrutiny: perfect clarity in peridot is possible but uncommon in natural material. Synthetic or glass material typically lacks the organized natural inclusions described above.

## Color and Visual Checks

Peridot's yellow-green to lime-green color comes from iron (Fe²⁺) in its crystal structure — it cannot be heat-treated to alter or improve its color. A stone marketed as peridot in an unusually deep blue-green or pure emerald-green is almost certainly a different mineral. The natural range runs from yellowish-green through olive to brownish-green; the most prized color is a vivid, pure lime green.

Check for "windowing" — a pale, washed-out area in the center of the stone when viewed face-on — which can result from poor cutting. This does not identify a fake, but it does indicate low-quality cutting that reduces the stone's visual appeal and value.

## Hardness Check (Use With Caution)

Peridot has a Mohs hardness of 6.5 to 7. Common soda-lime glass used in cheap imitations sits around 5.5 to 6 and can be scratched by a steel file. However, the hardness test is destructive and not recommended for finished gems or jewelry. Use it only on raw material of uncertain identity where a small scratch in an inconspicuous area is acceptable. Note that some synthetic simulants (spinel, corundum) are harder than peridot and will pass a basic hardness check.

## Common Fakes and Imitations

| Imitation | How to distinguish |
|---|---|
| Green bottle glass | No facet doubling, no natural inclusions, may show bubbles |
| Synthetic corundum / spinel | No facet doubling, different refractive index; may show curved growth lines under magnification |
| Synthetic peridot (lab forsterite) | Very rare at retail; facet doubling present but inclusions absent; requires a lab report to distinguish |
| Dyed green chalcedony | No doubling; dye may concentrate along grain boundaries visible under magnification |

Gemdat notes that imitations are often made from corundum or spinel syntheses and that evergreen bottle glass can be mistaken for peridot.

## When to Use a Gemologist

If a stone is expensive, arrives without provenance, or you cannot verify it with the above checks, a gemological identification from GIA, AGL, or AGTA provides definitive results. A GIA identification report costs approximately $50–$80 and confirms mineral identity, origin claims, and whether any treatments are present. For large purchases — over $150 for the stone — this is worthwhile.
