# How to Identify Real Fluorite — Tests That Work

Real fluorite has a Mohs hardness of 4, perfect cleavage in four directions, and a specific gravity of around 3.2. Many specimens also fluoresce blue or purple under ultraviolet light. Common substitutes — glass, dyed calcite, and quartz — each fail at least one of these tests. No single test is definitive on its own; use two or three together for confidence.

## Test 1: Hardness

Fluorite defines the 4 point on the Mohs scale. A steel nail or steel file (approximately Mohs 5.5 to 6.5) will scratch fluorite with moderate pressure. Copper (Mohs 3) will not scratch fluorite — fluorite will scratch copper instead.

To test: drag a steel nail across an inconspicuous part of the surface. If it leaves a clear scratch, the stone is Mohs 4 or softer. If the nail slides off without marking, the stone is harder than Mohs 5.5 — which means it is not fluorite.

Common imposters that fail this test:
- **Glass**: glass is typically Mohs 5.5 to 6, so it resists the steel nail better than fluorite
- **Quartz** (including dyed quartz): Mohs 7, considerably harder than fluorite and harder to scratch with a nail
- **Calcite**: Mohs 3, softer than fluorite — a fingernail (Mohs 2.5) nearly scratches calcite, which is softer than fluorite

## Test 2: Cleavage

When fluorite breaks, it produces flat, triangular or octahedral faces — four perfectly flat planes at specific angles. This is because of its perfect octahedral cleavage. Glass and quartz do not cleave this way; they fracture with curved, shell-like (conchoidal) surfaces.

If you can examine a broken edge or a natural face on a rough specimen, look for flat, mirror-like triangular surfaces meeting at consistent angles. These are cleavage planes and are diagnostic of fluorite. Rounded or irregular fracture surfaces suggest glass or quartz.

Do not deliberately break a stone to test it. This test applies when examining rough material with naturally broken surfaces.

## Test 3: UV Fluorescence

Fluorite is one of the most commonly fluorescent minerals. Many specimens glow brightly blue, purple, or green under a UV (ultraviolet) lamp or UV torch. The intensity and colour of fluorescence varies by specimen and origin — not all fluorite fluoresces, and some does not fluoresce at all — so this test is useful as supporting evidence, not proof.

Glass typically does not fluoresce. Dyed calcite may fluoresce but usually red or pink rather than blue. Quartz may show weak fluorescence. A vivid blue or purple glow under UV is a strong positive indicator for fluorite.

Inexpensive UV torches (365 nm wavelength) are available from photography and mineral supply retailers.

## Test 4: Weight (Specific Gravity)

Fluorite has a specific gravity of approximately 3.2, meaning it is about 3.2 times the weight of an equal volume of water. Glass is lighter (specific gravity ~2.5). Quartz is also lighter (~2.65). Calcite is close to fluorite (2.7), so weight alone does not distinguish fluorite from calcite well, but it does help separate fluorite from glass.

A fluorite tumbled stone will feel noticeably denser in the hand than a glass bead of the same size. This is a rough comparison, not a precise test, but experienced handlers notice the difference quickly.

## Test 5: Temperature and Feel

Fluorite feels cool to the touch initially, as do most real minerals. Glass also feels cool, so this test is not definitive on its own. Plastic imitations, however, tend to warm up much faster in the hand and feel lighter and less dense. If a colourful piece warms to body temperature within a few seconds of handling and feels light for its size, it is likely plastic.

## Price as a Signal

Fluorite is an abundant and affordable mineral. Tumbled stones from reputable sellers typically cost $2 to $15 each depending on size; faceted stones run $3 to $8 per carat at standard quality. Stones priced below $0.50 each in quantity lots, sold without any provenance, may be glass, dyed calcite, or plastic. Unusually low prices for claimed high-quality pieces are worth scrutinising.
