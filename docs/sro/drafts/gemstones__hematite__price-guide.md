# Hematite Price Guide: What It Costs and Why

Hematite is one of the least expensive gemstone minerals in the retail crystal market. Cut and polished material typically ranges from $0.02 to $5 per carat — prices checked September 2026 against 2023 retail evidence, and vary by grade, form, and seller. Bulk raw ore is essentially a commodity worth very little per piece; quality specimens and specialty forms command more.

## Why Hematite Is So Affordable

Hematite (Fe₂O₃) is among the most abundant minerals on Earth's surface and in the shallow crust. It is the world's primary iron ore, mined at industrial scale in China, Australia, Brazil, India, Russia, and elsewhere. This abundance means gem-grade and crystal-grade material is continuously available on global markets at low cost. Scarcity — which drives prices in gems like alexandrite or paraíba tourmaline — is simply not a factor for hematite.

The stone's Mohs hardness of 5 to 6.5 and opaque appearance also position it in the lower value tiers of the gem market. It is suited to cabochons, tumbled stones, and beads rather than faceted gems, which keeps it in lower price bands. For context on how hardness relates to durability and market positioning, see [Mohs hardness scale](/learn/mohs-hardness-scale).

## Price by Form

| Form | Typical retail range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Raw or rough specimen | $1–$10 per piece | Highly variable by size and quality |
| Tumbled stone | $1–$5 per piece | Most common retail form |
| Beads (per strand) | $3–$15 per strand | Depends on bead size and count |
| Cabochon | $1–$8 per piece | Polish quality and grade affect price |
| Specular or micaceous | $5–$30+ per piece | Premium for high-quality display pieces |
| Kidney ore specimen | $10–$80+ per piece | Collector-grade; form and size dependent |

These are broad retail ranges based on available evidence from crystal retail sources (checked September 2026). Premium dealers, artisan jewelry settings, and collectors' markets price above these figures. Bulk suppliers and online marketplaces vary widely and do not always follow gem trade norms.

## What Drives the Price

- **Variety and form**: Specular hematite (silver, shiny, with a micaceous or flaky appearance) and kidney ore (rounded, botryoidal) are collector forms priced above ordinary tumbled stones.
- **Polish quality**: A high-mirror finish adds noticeably to the retail price of cabochons and tumbled pieces.
- **Iridescence**: Some hematite displays a natural rainbow iridescence; this is uncommon and commands a collector premium.
- **Size**: Larger single specimens — well-formed crystals, large kidney ore — are rarer and priced accordingly.
- **Setting**: Hematite set in silver or gold jewelry carries a premium for the metalwork, not the stone itself.
- **Provenance**: Museum-quality or locality-specific pieces (e.g., specimens from Cumberland, England or Elba, Italy) attract higher prices from collectors.

## Spotting Overpriced or Fake Material

"Magnetic hematite" is a man-made ferrite (not genuine hematite) frequently sold in gift and tourist shops, sometimes at prices implying rarity. If a piece is strongly attracted to a common magnet, it is not natural hematite. Paying for these products is not a problem if you want them, but paying real-hematite prices for ferrite under a misleading label is overpaying. For tests that confirm genuine material, see [how to identify hematite](/gemstones/hematite/how-to-identify).

Because genuine hematite is so inexpensive, unusually high prices for ordinary tumbled stones or simple beads are a warning sign worth investigating. Reputable mineral dealers, geological society sales, and established crystal retailers are more reliable than generic gift outlets or marketplaces with no provenance information. For broader sourcing guidance, see [ethical crystal sourcing](/learn/ethical-crystal-sourcing).
