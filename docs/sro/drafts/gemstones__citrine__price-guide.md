# Citrine Price Guide — What It Costs and Why

Citrine is one of the more affordable colored gemstones on the market. Heat-treated citrine — which accounts for the majority of retail supply — typically retails for $4 to $12 per carat. Natural, untreated citrine commands significantly more, starting around $40 per carat for quality pieces. Retail prices checked September 2026 against GemSelect market data (based on 2025 figures).

## The Treatment Factor: Why Price Varies So Much

The single largest price driver for citrine is whether the stone's color is natural or produced by heat treatment. Understanding this distinction is essential before buying.

**Heat-treated citrine** is produced by heating amethyst or smoky quartz — both varieties of the same quartz mineral (SiO₂) as citrine — to temperatures of roughly 470–560°C. This converts the purple or grey color to yellow or orange. The result is physically identical to natural citrine in terms of chemistry and durability (Mohs 7), and heat treatment is standard and accepted in the gem trade. Most orange and reddish-orange citrine on the market, including most raw clusters with white bases sold online and in crystal shops, falls in this category.

**Natural citrine** is rarer. It forms in specific geological conditions — hydrothermal quartz veins and granitic pegmatites — and its pale to golden yellow color occurs without human intervention. Brazil, Spain, Russia, and Madagascar are primary sources.

Because natural citrine is rarer and harder to source accurately, prices are substantially higher and the claimed status is harder to verify without a gemological report from an accredited lab such as GIA or AGL.

For help telling natural from heat-treated at home, see [how to identify citrine](/gemstones/citrine/how-to-identify).

## Key Price Drivers

### Color

Color is the dominant value factor. The gem trade recognizes several grades:

- **Madeira citrine**: Deep reddish-orange to amber, the most prized and expensive color range. The name comes from the wine. Genuine Madeira-colored natural citrine is relatively rare.
- **Rich golden yellow**: Saturated, warm yellow without brown or grey tints. Desirable in both natural and treated material.
- **Pale yellow**: Common in natural citrine; lower price per carat but often preferred by buyers who want assured natural origin.
- **Brownish or smoky orange**: Lower commercial desirability; typically lower price.

### Clarity

Citrine is a Type II gemstone in the GIA clarity classification system, meaning eye-visible inclusions are expected but high-clarity stones command premiums. For display or collector purposes, slight inclusions are generally acceptable. For faceted jewelry, eye-clean stones are preferred and priced higher.

### Cut and carat weight

Citrine is produced in large crystals, so very large stones (over 10 carats) do not carry the exponential per-carat premiums seen in rarer gemstones. A well-cut stone shows better color saturation and light return than a poorly proportioned one of the same carat weight. Custom and precision cuts command modest premiums over standard commercial cuts.

### Origin claim

Stones sold as "Brazilian citrine" or "Rio Grande citrine" may command small premiums based on origin, but these claims are difficult to verify without trace-element analysis. For most buyers, origin is less important than treatment disclosure and visual quality.

## Durability and Value for Daily Wear

Citrine's Mohs hardness of 7 means it is durable enough for most jewelry applications with normal care. It is more susceptible to scratching than sapphire (Mohs 9) or diamond (Mohs 10) and should not be stored loose alongside harder stones. For a full explanation of what Mohs ratings mean in practice, see [the Mohs hardness scale guide](/learn/mohs-hardness-scale).

Its durability-to-price ratio makes it a popular choice for large statement pieces in rings, pendants, and earrings where the size of a sapphire or emerald would be prohibitively expensive.

## How to Avoid Overpaying

1. **Ask about treatment.** Any reputable seller will disclose whether the stone is natural or heat-treated. If a seller cannot answer, treat the stone as heat-treated for pricing purposes.
2. **Compare per-carat prices.** If a stone is presented as "natural citrine" but priced at $5 per carat, it is almost certainly heat-treated or mislabeled.
3. **Be skeptical of very saturated orange-red clusters at low prices.** These are typically heat-treated amethyst clusters, which are not fraudulent but are not natural citrine either.
4. **Request a certificate for high-value purchases.** For any individual stone above roughly $200–$300, a certificate from GIA, AGL, or equivalent adds verification of treatment status and origin.
5. **Check multiple sources.** Price comparison across platforms (specialist gem dealers, online marketplaces, local gem shows) helps establish the current market range.
