# Lapis Lazuli Price Guide: What It Costs and Why

Lapis lazuli is not an expensive stone overall, but truly fine material is rare and commands significant premiums. Commercial-grade material sells for well under $10 per carat. High-quality Afghan lapis with intense uniform color and no visible calcite can reach $100 to $150 per carat or more at retail. What drives the difference is almost entirely color quality, calcite content, and treatment status.

## What Drives Lapis Lazuli Price

Lapis lazuli is a rock — an aggregate of lazurite, calcite, and pyrite — not a single transparent mineral. Price-per-carat comparisons between pieces only make sense when those pieces are comparable in composition, treatment, and form. GIA evaluates lapis lazuli for identification purposes but does not issue a standardized commercial grade.

**Color** is the primary price driver. The most valued lapis is rich, medium-dark royal blue or slightly violetish blue, even and well-saturated across the full surface. GIA describes this as the Afghan or Persian grade. Pale, grayish, or greenish blue is less valuable. Excess pyrite creates a dull greenish cast — the most reliable visual marker of lower-grade material.

**Calcite content** is the second major factor. Top-quality lapis has no visible calcite. White streaks, veins, or patches break up the uniform blue surface and reduce value. GIA identifies three commercial grades in order of value: Afghan (intense uniform blue, little or no calcite or pyrite), Russian/Siberian (various blues, some pyrite, possibly calcite), and Chilean (often greenish-tinged with obvious calcite matrix).

**Pyrite distribution** also matters. Small, attractively scattered pyrite flecks do not reduce value and can enhance visual appeal. Dense or large pyrite, or pyrite producing discoloration at its edges, reduces value.

**Treatment** has a strong effect. Natural-color, untreated lapis commands the highest prices. Dyed material — where weak color or pale calcite has been masked with blue dye — should be priced substantially lower and must be disclosed. Waxed or resin-impregnated material is also common. For help identifying whether a piece is dyed, see [how to identify lapis lazuli](/gemstones/lapis-lazuli/how-to-identify).

**Origin** carries a premium. Afghan material from the Sar-e-Sang mines in Badakhshan — the world's oldest continuously worked gemstone source — consistently commands higher prices than Chilean or Siberian material. Chilean lapis is often more heavily calcite-mottled and commonly used for decorative carvings rather than fine jewelry.

## Indicative Price Ranges (September 2026)

| Quality level | Description | Approximate range per carat |
|---|---|---|
| Low grade | Dull or uneven color, heavy calcite, possibly fractured | Under $1–$10 |
| Mid-grade | Good blue, moderate calcite, some pyrite | $10–$50 |
| High quality | Deep intense blue, minimal calcite, fine pyrite | $50–$100 |
| Premium Afghan | Uniform violetish royal blue, no visible calcite, untreated | $100–$150+ |

These are indicative per-carat ranges for polished material, checked against September 2026 retail sources. Prices vary by seller, format, and provenance. Large rough pieces used for carvings and decorative objects are typically priced differently from polished gemstones. Tumbled stones and beads are usually sold by the piece or strand at low absolute prices, even for reasonable quality material.

## How Size Affects Price

Unlike many colored gemstones, lapis lazuli rough can occur in multi-kilogram blocks, so large size does not automatically command a rarity premium the way it does for rarer gems. The price premium comes from maintaining rich, uniform color across a larger area. A smaller, flawless Afghan cabochon can cost more per carat than a much larger piece of calcite-mottled lower-grade material.

## Grade Labels Are Not Standardized

Retailers use labels such as "AAA" or "5A" with no shared standard. One seller's AAA may contain visible calcite and pyrite; another reserves the label for pyrite-free, untreated uniform blue Afghan material. Compare pieces directly on the criteria that matter: color depth and uniformity, calcite content, pyrite distribution, treatment disclosure, and provenance — not on seller-defined labels alone.

## How to Avoid Overpaying

Buy from sellers who disclose treatment status explicitly. Dyed material sold at untreated prices is a common issue in the trade. For high-value purchases, ask for a gemological lab report — GIA issues colored stone identification reports that confirm material identity and note detectable treatments.

For context on durability: lapis lazuli's hardness of Mohs 5–6 makes it softer than many jewelry stones, which affects wearability and long-term value in rings and bracelets exposed to daily wear.
