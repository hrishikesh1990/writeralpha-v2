# Moss Agate Price Guide — What It Costs and Why

Moss Agate is one of the more affordable semi-precious stones on the market. Loose cabochons range from roughly $1 to $50 per carat depending on pattern clarity and transparency; tumbled stones sell for $3–$25 per piece; and finished jewelry in silver settings runs $30–$150, rising to $300 or more for gold settings. Pattern quality — not carat weight — is the dominant price driver.

## What Drives Moss Agate Value

Moss Agate has no standardized gemological grading system the way diamonds do. Its value is almost entirely aesthetic: the quality and legibility of the mineral inclusions inside the stone.

**Pattern clarity and complexity** is the most important factor. Inclusions that branch distinctly into fern-like or landscape forms against a clear or translucent background command significantly higher prices than stones where inclusions are blurry, sparse, or indistinct. The most prized specimens — sometimes called "scenic" Moss Agate — show detailed miniature landscapes inside the stone.

**Transparency of the base material** matters because it allows the inclusions to appear three-dimensional. A cloudy or opaque base obscures the pattern and lowers value. The clearest material, where inclusions appear to float in the stone, achieves the highest per-carat prices.

**Color** also contributes: vivid, deep forest green inclusions against a colorless or pale grey base attract more interest than dull or muddy greenish-brown coloring. Red and brown tones (from iron oxidation in the hornblende or chlorite) are acceptable and sometimes prized for variety.

**Origin** is a secondary factor. Montana Moss Agate (from the Yellowstone River drainage) has a strong collector following and commands modest premiums for top specimens. India and Brazil supply the bulk of commercial material.

**Size** matters at the extremes: very large, high-quality specimens are genuinely rare and priced accordingly.

Moss Agate's hardness (Mohs 6.5–7) is relevant to value because it is durable enough for daily-wear jewelry. For context on what that hardness means in practice, see [the Mohs hardness scale guide](/learn/mohs-hardness-scale).

## How to Avoid Overpaying

**Check the pattern, not just the price.** A stone with a vivid, legible landscape pattern is worth more than a same-size stone with murky inclusions — even if both are sold as "Moss Agate."

**Ask for photos of the actual stone.** Stock images are common in online retail. Sellers of higher-quality material generally photograph individual specimens.

**Know that dyed material exists.** Lower-grade chalcedony is sometimes dyed and sold as Moss Agate. Dyed material is cheaper and its color can fade. For how to tell dyed from natural stones, see [how to identify Moss Agate](/gemstones/moss-agate/how-to-identify).

**Factor in the metal when buying jewelry.** For finished pieces, the metal setting — especially gold — often accounts for more of the price than the stone itself. A $400 gold ring may contain a $15 stone; that is not necessarily a bad purchase, but knowing this helps set expectations.

**Consider source.** For questions about responsible sourcing and what to look for from crystal sellers, see [ethical crystal sourcing](/learn/ethical-crystal-sourcing).

## Is Moss Agate Worth Buying as an Investment?

Moss Agate is not generally considered a financial investment vehicle. It does not appreciate rapidly or predictably, and there is no liquid secondary market the way there is for diamonds, rubies, or sapphires. Its value as a purchase lies in its aesthetics, durability, and accessibility as a stone for everyday jewelry or display.
