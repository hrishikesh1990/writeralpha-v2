# Blue Lace Agate Price Guide

Blue Lace Agate cabochons typically sell for $8 to $30 per carat at retail, with tumbled stones in the $3 to $8 range per piece. Premium jewelry-grade specimens from confirmed Namibian origin command higher prices. Supply constraints from the primary Namibian source have pushed prices upward since 2024, making very cheap Blue Lace Agate worth scrutinizing for authenticity.

## Typical Retail Price Ranges

The following ranges are based on retail market data checked in September 2026. Prices vary by grade, cut, seller, and origin.

| Form | Typical retail price |
|---|---|
| Tumbled stone (10–20 g) | $3–$8 per piece |
| Cabochon (5–10 carats) | $15–$40 per cabochon |
| Cabochon (per carat, mixed grades) | $8–$30 per carat |
| Jewelry-grade beads (per strand) | $10–$40 depending on bead size and quality |
| Raw/rough specimen | $5–$20 per piece (small); higher for notable specimens |

These are retail ranges, not wholesale. A single stone's price can fall above or below these ranges depending on the quality factors described below.

## What Drives the Price

### Origin

The primary historical source for Blue Lace Agate was the Ysterputs Mine in the Karas Region of Namibia. This material is regarded as the reference standard: pale blue with well-defined lace banding, often with fine detail and translucency. Material confirmed to come from Namibian origin commands a premium over material from other sources, which includes deposits in Kenya, Malawi, and other locations producing similar but not always identical material.

### Supply constraints

The Ysterputs Mine has operated intermittently and with reduced output. Market data from 2024 to 2025 shows price increases of roughly 28 to 35% for premium Blue Lace Agate relative to prior years as supply tightened. This supply pressure is ongoing and means that genuinely high-grade Namibian Blue Lace Agate is increasingly expensive and relatively scarce at mass-market prices.

### Color and banding

The most commercially valued Blue Lace Agate has a clear, well-defined pale blue color with fine, crisp white banding visible across the full piece. Stones with muddy, uneven, or brownish zones are less desirable and priced lower. Stones with very tight, intricate banding are generally valued above those with coarser or less defined patterns.

### Clarity and translucency

Material with some translucency — where light passes faintly through the paler zones — is typically preferred and priced higher than fully opaque material of otherwise similar quality.

### Treatment status

Genuinely untreated Blue Lace Agate commands a premium over dyed material. Much blue agate sold inexpensively in the market is common grey or white agate that has been dyed blue using chemical processes — a well-documented trade practice. Dyed material is not intrinsically harmful, but it is not the same thing as natural Blue Lace Agate and should be priced accordingly. For how to distinguish natural from dyed material, see [how to identify Blue Lace Agate](/gemstones/blue-lace-agate/how-to-identify).

### Cut quality

A well-cut cabochon that maximizes the display of banding and sits evenly in a setting is worth more than a roughly shaped piece of similar weight. Professionally polished material with a high luster commands more than tumbled stones.

## How to Avoid Overpaying

**Check that color is natural.** Natural Blue Lace Agate is pale and soft, never vivid. If a piece is priced as Blue Lace Agate but shows a saturated, bright blue, it is almost certainly dyed agate. Dyed material is not worth Blue Lace Agate prices.

**Ask about origin.** Sellers with verified Namibian-origin stock will typically say so. Generic descriptions such as "natural blue lace agate" without provenance information are not confirmation of Namibian origin. Other origins can produce quality material, but the price should reflect that.

**Compare unit prices.** Very low prices — for example, $1 to $3 per carat for material described as premium Blue Lace Agate — warrant examination. Given current supply constraints, genuinely high-grade material is not cheap.

**Understand what you are buying.** Tumbled stones are an appropriate and honest product for most uses and are priced accordingly. Paying cabochon-grade prices for tumbled material is overpaying; paying tumbled-stone prices for what is described as a high-grade lapidary cabochon should prompt scrutiny.

**Factor in durability.** At Mohs 6.5 to 7, Blue Lace Agate is durable enough for everyday jewelry. The [Mohs hardness scale](/learn/mohs-hardness-scale) explains what this means in practical terms for wear and care. A well-cared-for piece retains its value and appearance over time, making the purchase decision partly about long-term use.
