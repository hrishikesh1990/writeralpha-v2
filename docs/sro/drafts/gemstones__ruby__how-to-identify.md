# How to Identify Real Ruby — Tests and Red Flags

Real ruby is a red variety of corundum (Al2O3) hardened to Mohs 9 by chromium substitution in its crystal lattice. The most reliable identification uses laboratory gemological equipment. At home, you can screen for obvious fakes using hardness, color, UV fluorescence, and inclusion examination — but a GIA, GRS, or equivalent certificate is the only definitive answer, particularly for any purchase above a few hundred dollars.

## What You Are Actually Identifying

The term "ruby" in commerce covers several distinct materials, and the risks differ:

| Material | What it is | Key indicators |
|---|---|---|
| Natural ruby | Red corundum (Al2O3 with Cr) | Inclusions, strong red UV fluorescence, Mohs 9, refractive index 1.762–1.770 |
| Heat-treated natural ruby | Natural ruby with improved color/clarity | As above; treatment is normal and does not make it fake |
| Fracture-filled ruby | Natural ruby enhanced with lead glass | Visible gas bubbles under magnification, orange flash effect |
| Synthetic (lab-grown) ruby | Chemically identical to natural, grown in a lab | Curved striae, no natural inclusions; less valuable, not fake as such |
| Glass imitation | Not corundum; glass colored red | Air bubbles, Mohs ~5.5, no UV fluorescence |
| Red spinel, garnet, or glass sold as ruby | Different minerals | Lower hardness, different refractive index, different inclusions |
| Dyed corundum | Low-quality corundum with artificial color | Color concentrated in fractures, removed by acid |

## At-Home Screening Tests

At-home tests can rule out obvious imposters but are not substitutes for laboratory testing.

### Hardness test

Ruby ranks 9 on the Mohs scale. A piece of glass (Mohs ~5.5) will be easily scratched by ruby, and ruby itself will not be scratched by a steel file (Mohs ~6.5) or a piece of quartz (Mohs 7). If the stone scratches easily, it is not corundum.

Caution: scratch tests can damage the stone's surface. Perform on an inconspicuous area only and not at all on valuable pieces. Use a dedicated, known-hardness testing kit rather than household objects.

### UV fluorescence

Under a long-wave UV lamp (blacklight), natural ruby from most origins glows a strong red to orange-red due to chromium fluorescence. Glass imitations show no fluorescence or a different color. Some iron-rich rubies from Thailand glow weakly or not at all, so absent fluorescence does not by itself rule out natural ruby — but strong red fluorescence is a positive indicator. Synthetic ruby also fluoresces strongly, so this test does not separate natural from lab-grown.

### Visual examination with a loupe

Use a 10x loupe in good lighting.

**Natural ruby inclusions:** Natural rubies typically contain fine needle-like inclusions (rutile silk), fingerprint-shaped healing fractures, or tiny crystal inclusions. These are signs of natural origin.

**Fracture-filled ruby indicators:** Look for an iridescent flash (often orange or blue) inside the stone when tilted under reflected light. This "flash effect" is caused by the lead-glass filler and is a diagnostic feature. Bubbles inside the filling may also be visible.

**Glass imitation indicators:** Round or elongated gas bubbles, swirling flow patterns, and a noticeably lower lustre than corundum.

**Synthetic ruby:** Often shows curved growth lines (curved striae) rather than the straight or angular growth patterns of natural corundum. This requires experience and magnification above 10x to assess reliably.

### Temperature

Natural corundum feels cool to the touch and warms slowly. Glass warms more quickly to body temperature. This is a rough guide only, not a definitive test.

## Limitations of Home Testing

Home tests can confidently identify obvious glass imitations and very low-quality materials. They cannot reliably:
- Distinguish natural from synthetic corundum
- Identify fracture-filling in well-filled stones without the characteristic flash
- Confirm treatment status
- Determine geographic origin (which affects value significantly)

For any purchase of significant value, a certificate from GIA, GRS, Gübelin, or SSEF is essential. See [ruby price guide](/gemstones/ruby/price-guide) for why origin and treatment status affect value so dramatically.

## Buying Online and From Unknown Sources

The most common substitutions encountered in the retail crystal and gemstone market are: glass imitations sold as ruby, red garnet sold as ruby, and fracture-filled ruby sold without disclosure of treatment. See [are online crystals real](/learn/are-online-crystals-real) for broader guidance. Key indicators of a trustworthy seller: they provide laboratory certificates for any piece priced over $200, they disclose heat treatment explicitly, and they clearly distinguish between natural and lab-grown material. See [lab-created vs natural gemstones](/compare/lab-created-vs-natural-gemstones) for that distinction.

Lab-grown ruby is chemically identical to natural and not a "fake" — it is a different product, legitimately sold at much lower prices. The issue is when lab-grown or glass material is sold as natural without disclosure.
