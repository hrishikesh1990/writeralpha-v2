# Rainbow Hematite Price Guide

Rainbow Hematite tumbled stones retail for approximately $2 to $5 per piece at US crystal shops and online sellers as of September 2026. Bulk wholesale bags (1 lb) typically run $15 to $20. Natural iridescent specimens command a modest premium over the titanium-coated material that dominates the bulk market. Price alone does not identify material type, but understanding what drives the price helps you buy what you are actually paying for.

## Typical Retail Prices (September 2026)

| Form | Typical retail range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Tumbled stone, single piece (0.75–1.25 in) | $2–$5 USD | Most common form; range spans natural and coated |
| Bulk tumbled, 1 lb bag | $15–$20 USD | Often titanium-coated magnetic material |
| Small raw/platy specimen | $5–$15 USD | Varies widely with size and iridescence quality |
| Display-quality natural specimen | $15–$50+ USD | Natural nano-film iridescence; pricing evidence limited |
| Rainbow hematite jewelry (bracelet/pendant) | $8–$25 USD | Setting quality and stone size drive the range |

These prices reflect US retail sellers checked in September 2026. Prices vary by seller, geographic market, and the grade of material.

## What Drives the Price

**Natural vs. titanium-coated.** The most significant price driver is material type. A large share of commercially sold "rainbow hematite" is titanium-coated hematite — ordinary hematite given an applied metallic coating to produce brilliant iridescence. This material is abundant, cheap to produce, and widely available in bulk at low prices. Natural Rainbow Hematite — hematite whose iridescence comes from a naturally formed aluminum phosphate nano-film (Rossman & Ma, 2025) — is less common in the bulk market and commands a modest premium. See [how to identify Rainbow Hematite](/gemstones/rainbow-hematite/how-to-identify) for tests that separate the two.

**Intensity and coverage of iridescence.** Within natural specimens, pieces with vivid, full-surface color play are worth more than those with patchy or faint iridescence. The colors are produced by a surface coating only a few hundred nanometers thick — quality varies across specimens from the same deposit.

**Size and form.** Larger tumbled stones and display-quality platy specimens cost more per piece than small tumbles. Weight is a less useful pricing metric than size because hematite is unusually dense (approximately 5.26 g/cm3), so a small piece already weighs considerably more than a comparable crystal of the same size.

**Source and sourcing transparency.** Most commercial Rainbow Hematite originates from Minas Gerais, Brazil, which hosts large deposits of iridescent hematite. Sellers who provide sourcing information — state, mine, region — typically charge slightly more than bulk importers who do not. For guidance on evaluating sourcing claims, see [ethical crystal sourcing](/learn/ethical-crystal-sourcing).

**Hardness and durability.** At Mohs 5 to 6, Rainbow Hematite is a moderately hard stone — durable enough for pendants and earrings, but the iridescent surface film abrades easily in rings and bracelets with daily wear. This affects long-term value: a ring stone will lose its rainbow quickly. For context on hardness and what it means for durability, see [Mohs hardness scale](/learn/mohs-hardness-scale).

## How to Avoid Overpaying

**Verify material type before buying.** Strongly magnetic pieces at very low bulk prices are almost certainly titanium-coated. If you want natural iridescent material, look for sellers who specifically describe it as non-magnetic and natural. The price difference between natural and coated is not enormous for tumbled stones ($1–$3 per piece at most), but paying natural-specimen prices for treated bulk material is worth avoiding.

**Compare per-piece cost in bulk listings.** A 1 lb bag typically contains 20 to 40 tumbled stones depending on size, giving a per-piece cost of $0.40 to $1.00 at wholesale. Retail markup to $2–$5 per piece is standard. Prices significantly above $5 per tumbled stone are only justified for particularly vivid or large pieces.

**Buy from sellers who describe what they are selling.** Listings that clearly state whether material is natural or coated, magnetic or non-magnetic, and give origin information are lower risk than vague listings. A seller who discloses that the material is titanium-coated is being honest; one who calls it "natural" without supporting detail is worth questioning.
