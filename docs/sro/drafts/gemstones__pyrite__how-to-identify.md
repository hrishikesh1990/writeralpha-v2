# How to Identify Real Pyrite from Fakes and Lookalikes

Real pyrite can be identified by four properties that glass, resin, and gold cannot replicate together: a greenish-black streak on unglazed porcelain, a hardness of 6 to 6.5, a specific gravity around 5.0, and — in natural specimens — striated cubic or pyritohedral crystal faces. None of these tests damages the stone in ordinary use.

## The Streak Test: The Most Reliable Single Check

Pyrite leaves a greenish-black to black streak when dragged across unglazed porcelain (the back of a standard ceramic tile). Gold leaves a golden-yellow streak. Glass leaves no streak at all, or scratches the tile. Chalcopyrite (another brass-yellow sulfide often confused with pyrite) leaves a greenish-black streak very similar to pyrite's, so the streak alone does not separate pyrite from chalcopyrite, but it does definitively rule out gold and glass.

An unglazed porcelain streak plate costs less than a dollar and is the most important tool for on-the-spot mineral identification. If a seller will not allow a streak test on a rough specimen, that is a reason for caution.

## Hardness Test

Pyrite scores 6 to 6.5 on the Mohs scale. In practical terms, this means:

1. A copper coin (Mohs ~3) will not scratch pyrite.
2. A steel pocket knife or steel file (Mohs ~5.5) will barely scratch pyrite, or may not scratch it at all.
3. Pyrite will scratch glass (Mohs ~5.5) easily.
4. A fingernail (Mohs ~2.5) will not scratch pyrite.

Gold has a Mohs hardness of 2.5 to 3, which means a copper coin or even a fingernail can scratch it. If a brass-yellow metallic mineral cannot be scratched by a steel knife, it is not gold. If it can be scratched by a fingernail, it is not pyrite.

Glass beads and resin imitations are typically 5 to 6 on Mohs — close to pyrite — so hardness alone may not rule them out. Use hardness alongside the other tests.

## Weight and Density

Pyrite has a specific gravity of 4.8 to 5.01, meaning it weighs about five times as much as an equal volume of water. For comparison:

| Material | Specific gravity |
|---|---|
| Pyrite (FeS2) | 4.8–5.01 |
| Chalcopyrite | ~4.2 |
| Glass | ~2.5 |
| Resin / plastic | ~1.0–1.4 |
| Gold | ~19.3 |

A pyrite specimen feels noticeably heavy for its size — heavier than most crystals. Glass and resin imitations feel conspicuously light. Gold is dramatically heavier still; a gold nugget the size of a fingernail feels far heavier than a pyrite cube of the same size. If a piece that looks like pyrite feels unusually light, it is likely glass or resin.

You do not need laboratory equipment to notice this. Picking up two similar-looking objects and comparing weight is a useful field check.

## Crystal Habit and Surface Texture

Natural pyrite most commonly forms as cubes, pyritohedra (twelve pentagonal faces), or combinations of those forms. The cube faces are often striated — marked with fine parallel grooves that run perpendicular to each other on adjacent faces. These striations are a crystal growth feature; they do not appear on glass, molded resin, or dyed stone.

Perfect spheres, perfectly round beads, and uniformly smooth surfaces are a signal that the object was cut and polished from a larger piece or poured in a mold. Well-cut pyrite beads do exist and are genuine, but a bead's smoothness cannot confirm or deny authenticity — only the other tests can.

Raw natural pyrite specimens often display matrix rock (the surrounding host rock from which they were extracted), uneven surfaces, and occasionally minor surface tarnish. The [pyrite meaning and properties page](/gemstones/pyrite/meaning) describes what genuine material looks like in natural form.

## Distinguishing Pyrite from Chalcopyrite and Marcasite

Two minerals are commonly mistaken for pyrite by sellers and buyers alike:

**Chalcopyrite** (CuFeS2) is a copper-iron sulfide with a similar metallic luster, but with a distinctly more iridescent, multi-colored tarnish surface (gold, purple, blue) when oxidized. Fresh surfaces are brass-yellow. Its streak is greenish-black like pyrite's, but its hardness is lower (3.5 to 4) — a steel knife scratches chalcopyrite readily.

**Marcasite** is the orthorhombic polymorph of FeS2 — chemically identical to pyrite but with a different crystal structure. Antique jewelry described as "marcasite" is nearly always pyrite (cut and set in silver). True marcasite is rarer, less stable, and not commercially used in modern jewelry.

## Spotting Glass and Resin Fakes

Glass and resin imitations used in the crystal trade are usually either smooth spheres or faceted pieces that replicate polished pyrite's luster under artificial lighting. Signs of a fake:

- Uniform color and luster throughout, with no variation from any angle
- Air bubbles visible inside (resin) or seen with a loupe
- Extremely light weight for the size
- No streak on porcelain, or a white/grey streak (glass scratches porcelain)
- A price that is suspiciously low even for tumbled pyrite

## A Note on Lab-Created Pyrite

Synthetic pyrite does not currently exist as a consumer product. Unlike emeralds or sapphires, which have established lab-grown markets, pyrite is too abundant and inexpensive to warrant industrial synthesis for the gem trade. If a seller advertises "lab-created pyrite," treat it with scepticism. The distinction between lab-created and natural gemstones is covered in [lab-created vs natural gemstones](/compare/lab-created-vs-natural-gemstones). For pyrite, the practical question is natural mineral versus glass or resin, not lab versus natural.
