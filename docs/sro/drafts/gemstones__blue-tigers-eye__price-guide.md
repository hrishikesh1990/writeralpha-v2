# Blue Tiger's Eye Price Guide

Blue Tiger's Eye is an affordable gemstone. Retail bracelets range from roughly $8 to $30 for standard quality, rising to $30–$80 for premium beads or specialty designs. Tumbled stones are broadly available for $2–$8 each at most crystal retailers. The price is driven by chatoyancy quality, bead size, polish, and seller type — not by rarity, since Blue Tiger's Eye is widely mined in South Africa and other locations.

## Retail Price Ranges (September 2026)

Prices below reflect what a retail buyer can expect to pay for genuine polished Blue Tiger's Eye. Wholesale prices (what shops buy at) are lower.

These ranges are drawn from wholesale platform MSRP data and public retail listings checked in September 2026. Prices vary by grade, country of purchase, and seller. High-traffic marketplaces (Etsy, Amazon) span a wide range including both overpriced and underpriced material.

## What Drives Price

**Chatoyancy quality** is the primary value driver for Blue Tiger's Eye. A stone with a sharp, well-centered, bright chatoyant band — the moving silky line visible when you rock the stone under light — is more desirable and commands more than a piece with a faint or diffuse band. Well-cut cabochons that align the fiber direction precisely with the stone's dome show the best effect.

**Color depth and consistency** matters secondarily. Rich steel-blue or blue-green material with even distribution fetches more than pale or patchy specimens. Material that retains more of its original crocidolite blue tone (rather than oxidizing toward gold) tends to be preferred by collectors.

**Form and finish** affect price predictably: faceted pieces are rarer and more expensive than tumbled, and a high-polish finish costs more than a matte tumble.

**Bead size** directly affects bracelet price: 6mm bead bracelets are cheaper than 12mm or 16mm equivalents because larger beads require more material per piece.

**Seller type**: Specialist crystal shops and artisan jewelers charge more than bulk marketplace sellers, but they are also more likely to sell genuine material and provide sourcing information.

**Origin**: South Africa is the primary commercial source. Material described specifically as South African hawk's eye is not necessarily priced higher, but origin labeling is a quality signal from an informed seller.

## How to Avoid Overpaying

- **Compare chatoyancy, not just color.** A piece with strong chatoyancy is worth more than a uniformly blue but flat-looking stone of the same size.
- **Request physical photos or video.** The chatoyant band is only visible when the stone is moved; static flat photos cannot reveal quality. Any reputable seller of Blue Tiger's Eye for more than $15 should provide video or a rocking GIF.
- **Check size and weight.** Bracelet listings that do not specify bead size may be smaller than expected. Verify bead size (6mm, 8mm, 10mm, 12mm) before purchasing.
- **Be cautious of very low prices on bracelets.** A bracelet under $5 on most platforms is likely dyed glass or synthetic cat's eye glass, not genuine Blue Tiger's Eye. See [how to identify Blue Tiger's Eye](/gemstones/blue-tigers-eye/how-to-identify) for the key visual tests.
- **Consider where the stone is from.** Ethically sourced material may cost slightly more. For guidance on what ethical sourcing looks like in the crystal trade, see [ethical crystal sourcing](/learn/ethical-crystal-sourcing).

## What Blue Tiger's Eye Is Not Worth a Premium For

Blue Tiger's Eye is not a rare stone. It is commercially mined in significant volumes and widely available. Claims that a particular piece is "museum grade," "investment grade," or "extremely rare" should be treated skeptically unless the seller can provide specific grading or certification. The stone's durability (Mohs 6.5–7, well above the threshold for everyday wear — see [Mohs hardness scale](/learn/mohs-hardness-scale)) does not make it collector-grade in the same sense as rarer minerals.
