# How to Identify Real Red Jasper — Tests and Red Flags

Real Red Jasper is an opaque microcrystalline quartz with a Mohs hardness of 6.5–7, a dull to waxy surface, and natural color variation from brick red to red-brown. Common substitutes are dyed quartz or agate, dyed lesser-quality jasper, red glass, and plastic. The tests below require no specialist equipment and can be done at home or at a market stall.

## What Genuine Red Jasper Looks Like

Red Jasper is always opaque — no light passes through it even at thin edges. The color is red to red-brown, caused by iron oxide (hematite or goethite) inclusions distributed throughout the stone. Because these inclusions are natural, the color in genuine material tends to vary slightly across the surface: subtle patches, swirls, or streaks of slightly darker or lighter red are common. A perfectly uniform, saturated, candy-apple red is a warning sign.

The surface texture after polishing is smooth but has a dull to waxy luster rather than a glassy shine. A gemological glass luster is characteristic of glass, not jasper.

The fracture in unpolished or chipped areas is conchoidal — smooth, curved break surfaces, like broken glass — which is diagnostic of the quartz family.

## Hands-On Tests

### Temperature test

Hold the stone in your palm for 10 seconds. Genuine quartz-family stones, including jasper, are slow to warm — they will feel noticeably cool initially and take a while to reach body temperature. Glass also starts cool but warms faster. Plastic warms almost immediately. This test is most useful for distinguishing plastic from stone; it is less reliable for glass.

### Hardness test

Red Jasper at Mohs 6.5–7 will scratch window glass (Mohs ~5.5) without difficulty. Press a corner of the stone firmly across a glass surface. Genuine jasper leaves a clear scratch. Glass imitations may not scratch glass or may leave only a faint mark, since some glass formulations approach Mohs 6. This test works best with a pointed or unpolished edge of the specimen.

Do not attempt this test on a polished cabochon you want to buy — ask the seller to demonstrate on a separate piece or a waste edge.

### Weight

Red Jasper has a specific gravity of 2.5 to 2.9, making it moderately heavy for its size. Glass tends to feel slightly heavier (SG approximately 2.5–2.8, similar range), so weight alone does not distinguish them reliably. Plastic is obviously lighter. A piece that feels very light for its size is likely plastic or resin.

### Dye test (for dyed stone)

Some agate, low-grade jasper, or chalcedony is dyed red to pass as higher-quality red jasper. To test: dampen a cotton ball or cloth with acetone (nail-polish remover) and rub it firmly across an inconspicuous part of the stone. Dye will transfer to the cloth, leaving a colored smear. Natural iron oxide coloration will not. This test can damage a polished surface, so apply it to the back or base of a piece.

A faster visual check: look at the stone under magnification or bright light. Dyed material sometimes shows concentrated color in surface cracks or pits — the dye pools in low areas — while natural color is distributed through the body of the stone.

### Light transmission test

Hold the stone up to a bright light source (phone flashlight, window). Genuine Red Jasper is fully opaque — no light passes through even at thin edges. If you see any translucency or a reddish glow at edges, the material may be dyed carnelian, red agate, or glass rather than jasper.

## Common Fakes and Imitations

| Imitation | How to spot it |
|---|---|
| Red glass | Glassy luster; may feel heavier; no color variation; translucent at edges |
| Plastic or resin | Warms quickly; scratches easily with a fingernail; very light |
| Dyed agate or chalcedony | Color concentrates in surface cracks; acetone test positive |
| Dyed low-grade jasper | Unnaturally uniform color; acetone test may be positive |
| Synthetic resin composites | Lightweight, warm quickly, may show mold seams |

## What to Ask a Seller

For online or market purchases, useful questions include:

- What is the country of origin?
- Has this stone been dyed or stabilized?
- Can you provide a return or exchange if the stone turns out to be imitation?

Red Jasper is an abundant, affordable stone. A price that seems extremely high for a tumbled piece — above $20 for a standard single stone at current retail (September 2026) — may not be justified by the material itself.
