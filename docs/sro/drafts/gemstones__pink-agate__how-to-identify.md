# How to Identify Real Pink Agate

Most pink agate sold in the crystal market is dyed — white or gray chalcedony artificially colored pink. Genuine untreated pink agate exists, most notably from Botswana, but it is less common and more expensive. Real agate is also sometimes confused with glass or plastic. This guide covers how to distinguish natural from dyed agate, genuine agate from glass, and what price signals to watch.

## Natural vs Dyed Pink Agate: The Core Issue

Agate is a microcrystalline quartz mineral with natural microscopic porosity. This makes it one of the easiest minerals in the trade to dye — it absorbs color deeply when immersed in dye solution under controlled conditions. The technique has been used since at least the 19th century, originating in Idar-Oberstein, Germany, and is now standard practice with material from Brazil and India. Dyeing is not fraudulent provided it is disclosed, but it is not always disclosed at the retail level.

Natural pink agate gets its color from trace iron oxide impurities deposited during formation. The pink is typically softer, more variable in saturation, and uneven across the banding — different bands may be white, cream, or pale rose in the same stone. Dyed material tends to show more uniform, vivid color.

## How to Tell Dyed from Natural: Practical Tests

### 1. Inspect drill holes and chipped edges

This is the most reliable at-home test. On a dyed stone, the outer surface absorbs color but the interior remains significantly paler. Look at any drill hole (in beads or pendants) or a naturally chipped edge under good light. If the inside is noticeably lighter than the outside, the color is a surface or near-surface treatment.

On natural pink agate, the color runs through the material, though the intensity may vary between bands.

### 2. Look at the banding under magnification

Use a 10x loupe or a phone macro lens. Genuine agate — whether natural or dyed — shows characteristic concentric or parallel banding (alternating layers of chalcedony). Look for:

- **Color pooling in cracks or pits**: a sign of dye concentrating in porous areas
- **Sharp color boundaries exactly at the surface**: suggests the dye did not penetrate deeply
- **Consistent vivid color through all bands including normally opaque white bands**: unusual in natural material; white chalcedony bands absorb dye differently from translucent bands, producing color variation — very uniform coloring across all bands suggests heavy treatment

### 3. Acetone swab test

Dab a cotton bud lightly moistened with acetone (nail-polish remover) on an inconspicuous area of the stone. If color transfers to the cotton, the stone is dyed. Natural color does not transfer. Note: this test works for surface or poorly-fixed dyes; well-fixed dyes may not transfer even when present.

### 4. Price as a signal

Very cheap pink agate — tumbled stones sold for under $1–$2 per piece, or heavily discounted bulk lots — is almost always dyed. Natural untreated pink agate from Botswana typically retails at $4–$6 per tumbled piece (AA grade, September 2026). Unusual brightness or uniformity of color at a very low price is a reliable indicator of treatment. See the [price guide](/gemstones/pink-agate/price-guide) for current retail ranges.

## Real Agate vs Glass or Plastic Imitations

Some "pink agate" sold online or at markets is not agate at all — it is glass or polymer resin colored to look like the stone.

### Temperature test

Agate is a mineral and remains cooler to the touch than glass or plastic for the first few seconds of handling. Hold the specimen in your palm: real agate warms slowly; glass warms faster; plastic warms almost immediately. This test works best for loose stones, not pendants.

### Bubbles under magnification

Glass often contains small spherical bubbles visible under a 10x loupe. Agate does not. If you see circular or oval inclusions that look like air pockets, the material is almost certainly glass.

### Hardness

Agate (Mohs 6.5–7) will scratch a glass plate (Mohs about 5.5) without being scratched itself. Plastic (typically Mohs 1–3) will be scratched by a steel knife blade (Mohs 5.5). Test on an inconspicuous area. Note that this test will scratch the stone surface, so use it only where damage will not be visible.

### Banding realism

Natural agate banding is irregular, with slight variations in band width and color gradation. Glass imitations often have evenly spaced, perfectly parallel bands or swirled patterns that look manufactured. Under magnification, glass has a more uniform internal structure.

## What Genuine Natural Pink Agate Looks Like

Natural, untreated pink agate from Botswana shows:

- Soft banding in pale rose, peach, cream, and white
- Slight translucency in thinner bands when held to light
- Color variation between bands, not uniform saturation
- Waxy surface luster, not glassy
- Conchoidal fracture on unpolished areas
