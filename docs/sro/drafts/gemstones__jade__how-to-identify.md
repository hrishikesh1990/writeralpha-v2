# How to Tell Real Jade from Fakes and Imitations

Real jade is either jadeite or nephrite — two different silicate minerals with distinct physical properties. The most common substitutes are serpentine, glass, dyed quartzite, aventurine, and prehnite. Several at-home checks catch most imitations; definitive identification requires gemological testing.

## What "Real Jade" Actually Means

Jade is not a single mineral. The name covers two separate species:

- **Jadeite**: NaAlSi2O6, a pyroxene. Mohs hardness 6.5–7, specific gravity 3.2–3.4, refractive index 1.654–1.667. Rarer and generally more valuable.
- **Nephrite**: Ca2(Mg,Fe)5Si8O22(OH)2, an amphibole. Mohs hardness 6–6.5, specific gravity 2.9–3.1, refractive index 1.600–1.627. Tougher due to its fibrous interlocking crystal structure.

Both display a waxy to greasy luster. Neither is glassy or high-reflectance in the way glass and many fakes are. A piece must be one of these two minerals to count as genuine jade; everything else is either an imitation or a simulant. For a discussion of price differences between the two, see [jade price guide](/gemstones/jade/price-guide).

## Common Fakes and How to Spot Each

### Serpentine

Serpentine (Mohs 3–5, SG 2.5–2.6) is the most widespread jade substitute and is sometimes sold as "new jade" or "Bowenite jade." It is significantly softer than real jade.

**Test**: scratch an inconspicuous spot with a steel knife blade (Mohs ~5.5). Serpentine scratches easily; genuine jade does not. Serpentine also has a lower density — a piece feels lighter than a same-size nephrite piece. Its luster tends to be more resinous and less waxy than nephrite.

### Glass

Glass is used to simulate high-quality jadeite, particularly the translucent green variety.

**Test**: examine the surface under a 10x loupe. Glass almost always contains small spherical air bubbles; jade contains no bubbles. Glass also warms more quickly in the hand than jade — genuine jade holds a cool temperature noticeably longer due to its higher thermal conductivity and mass. Glass may also display a more vitreous (mirror-like) luster than jade's characteristic waxy surface.

### Dyed quartzite (dyed "jade")

Quartzite is sometimes cut and dyed to resemble jade. Dyed quartzite is harder than serpentine (Mohs ~7) but betrays itself in other ways.

**Test**: examine the surface and any cracks under a loupe. Dye concentrates in grain boundaries and fractures, producing an uneven color pattern with darker pooling along surface irregularities. Genuine jade's color is more diffused through the material and does not pool at cracks. Dyed stones may also fade over time.

### Aventurine

Aventurine quartz (Mohs ~7) has a similar hardness to jade and is sometimes mistaken for nephrite, particularly in its green form.

**Test**: look for aventurescence — a glittering or spangled optical effect caused by plate-like inclusions of fuchsite mica. Genuine jade does not produce this sparkle. Aventurine also lacks jade's characteristic fibrous or interlocking texture when viewed through transmitted light.

### Prehnite and chrysoprase

Both are occasionally sold alongside or instead of jade. Prehnite (Mohs 6–6.5) has a more uniform, mint-green color. Chrysoprase is a chalcedony (Mohs 6.5–7) with a more saturated apple-green color.

**Test**: both lack jade's waxy luster; chrysoprase has a waxier surface but a different transparency character. Neither has jade's typical orange-peel surface texture visible under magnification on cut pieces.

## At-Home Tests You Can Do

1. **Temperature retention**: hold the piece in your closed hand for 30 seconds, then open your hand and place the stone on your inner wrist. Genuine jade stays notably cool; glass and plastic warm faster.
2. **Surface texture**: run a finger across the surface. Real jade typically has an even, slightly waxy feel. Plastic feels lighter and warm; glass feels slicker.
3. **Weight**: genuine jadeite is dense (SG 3.2–3.4). A piece that seems lighter than expected for its size may be serpentine, glass, or plastic.
4. **Loupe inspection**: check for bubbles (glass), dye pooling at cracks (dyed material), or glitter/sparkle (aventurine).
5. **Scratch test**: on an inconspicuous spot only. A steel knife blade will scratch serpentine but not genuine jade.

## Tests That Require Equipment

- **Specific gravity**: a precise SG measurement (using water displacement) will separate jadeite (3.2–3.4), nephrite (2.9–3.1), serpentine (2.5–2.6), and glass (~2.5). This is practical with a precision scale.
- **Refractive index**: requires a refractometer. Jadeite RI 1.654–1.667; nephrite RI 1.600–1.627.
- **UV fluorescence**: Type B jadeite (polymer-impregnated) may show patchy whitish fluorescence under long-wave UV that natural jadeite does not show.
- **Spectroscopy (Raman or FTIR)**: definitive non-destructive identification. Available at gemological laboratories.

## When to Get a Certificate

For any piece priced over a few hundred dollars, request a certificate from GIA, GGTL, or another accredited gemological laboratory before purchase. The certificate will confirm species, color origin (natural vs treated), and treatment status. Online purchases on platforms that do not provide certificates carry higher risk of imitation material — see [are online crystals real](/learn/are-online-crystals-real) for broader guidance.

Price is itself an identification signal: an apparent "high-quality jadeite bangle" offered at $30 is almost certainly not genuine.
