# Amethyst vs Purple Quartz: Are They the Same?

Amethyst is the mineralogically accepted name for purple quartz. The two terms refer to the same mineral — silicon dioxide (SiO₂) — colored by iron impurities and irradiation. "Purple quartz" is a descriptive phrase; "amethyst" is the specific variety name used by mineralogists, gemologists, and the gem trade. The practical difference matters mainly when other purple minerals are being passed off under the same label.

## Why "Purple Quartz" and "Amethyst" Mean the Same Mineral

Amethyst's purple color results from iron atoms (Fe³⁺) that substitute for silicon in the quartz lattice. Natural gamma irradiation causes those iron atoms to lose an electron, creating [FeO₄]⁰ color centers that absorb visible light and produce the characteristic violet hue. The iron content responsible for color is low — typically 10–100 parts per million — so chemical analysis alone cannot reliably confirm amethyst in the field.

The name amethyst comes from the Greek "a-methystos," meaning "not drunk": ancient Greeks believed it warded off intoxication. Mineralogically, amethyst is a color variety of macrocrystalline quartz with no distinct species status — it is the same SiO₂ as clear quartz (rock crystal), smoky quartz, and citrine.

## Physical Properties That Apply to Both

| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| Chemical formula | SiO₂ |
| Mohs hardness | 7 |
| Crystal system | Trigonal |
| Luster | Vitreous |
| Specific gravity | ~2.65 |
| Streak | White |

Hardness of 7 means amethyst sits comfortably above common scratching agents. For context on the full scale, see [the Mohs hardness scale guide](/learn/mohs-hardness-scale).

## Color Range and What Affects It

Amethyst's color ranges from pale lavender through medium violet to deep "royal purple." The most valued material (historically described as "Deep Siberian" grade) shows 75–80% primary purple with 15–20% secondary blue or red flashes. Color is often unevenly distributed in natural crystals: it concentrates in growth zones near rhombohedral faces and may appear in phantom-like layers in prismatic specimens.

Two factors degrade the color:
- **Heat above approximately 300–400 °C** causes amethyst to lose its violet color and turn yellow, orange, or brown — producing material sold as citrine or ametrine. This is a deliberate commercial practice in much of the citrine on the market.
- **Prolonged UV exposure** (direct sunlight) slowly destroys the color centers and fades the stone.

## What Else May Be Sold as Purple Quartz

Not every purple stone labeled "purple quartz" is amethyst:

- **Purple fluorite** (CaF₂): Mohs 4, perfect cubic cleavage, and a distinctly different luster. Much softer than quartz — easily scratched with a steel knife.
- **Purple glass or synthetic**: uniform color, may show air bubbles under magnification, no streak on porcelain.
- **Dyed quartzite or agate**: color concentrated along grain boundaries, visible under magnification.

Genuine amethyst leaves a white streak on unglazed porcelain and cannot be scratched by a steel knife (Mohs 7 vs ~5.5 for steel). The [amethyst identification page](/gemstones/amethyst/how-to-identify) covers the full test sequence.

## Color Zoning: A Natural Amethyst Signal

Natural amethyst almost always shows some degree of color zoning — deeper purple at crystal tips, paler or colorless zones toward the base. Uniformly saturated purple throughout a cut stone, especially a large one, can indicate glass or synthetic material. Tilting a genuine faceted amethyst under a light will usually reveal some color concentration at the culet.

## Geographic Sources

Major producing localities include Brazil (Minas Gerais, Rio Grande do Sul), Uruguay, Zambia, Madagascar, and Russia (Ural Mountains). Brazilian and Uruguayan material is abundant and accounts for most commercial supply; Zambian and Russian amethyst tends toward deeper violet and smaller crystals.

The [amethyst stone profile](/gemstones/amethyst) covers origin, varieties, and traditional associations in full.

## Metaphysical Traditions

Amethyst is traditionally associated with calm, intuition, and protection. In Vedic traditions, it is linked to the crown chakra and is used by practitioners seeking clarity and peace. These are cultural associations, not evidence-based outcomes. No controlled clinical study has demonstrated that wearing amethyst produces measurable physical or psychological effects beyond expectation.

## Which Term to Use

Use "amethyst" when purchasing, identifying, or discussing the stone in any mineralogical or trade context — it is the universally understood name. "Purple quartz" is a useful descriptive phrase but can cause confusion when other purple minerals are present in a collection or a seller's inventory. If a seller cannot confirm that their "purple quartz" is amethyst, request a streak test or refer to a gemologist.
